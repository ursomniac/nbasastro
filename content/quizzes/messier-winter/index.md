    # Q1 — M 79: the only winter globular — correct at position 3
    - id: 1
      image: "m_79-equ-1m.webp"
      text: >-
        Summer evenings are full of globular clusters. Among the winter
        Messier objects there is exactly one. Which is it?
      answers:
        - text: >-
            M 35 in Gemini
          correct: false
          explanation: >-
            M 35 is an open cluster: a loose, young group of stars in the
            Galaxy's disk, not a dense ball of old stars.
        - text: >-
            M 1 in Taurus
          correct: false
          explanation: >-
            M 1, the Crab Nebula, is the debris of a star that exploded as
            the supernova of 1054. It isn't a cluster at all.
        - text: >-
            M 79 in Lepus
          correct: true
          explanation: >-
            Pierre Méchain found it in 1780. It lies about 41,000
            light-years away, and the Milky Way may have taken it from
            another galaxy, possibly the Canis Major Dwarf. Globulars crowd
            toward the Galactic center, behind us in winter, so the winter
            sky has almost none.
        - text: >-
            M 41 in Canis Major
          correct: false
          explanation: >-
            M 41 is a bright open cluster just south of Sirius, rich but
            loose, and far younger than any globular.

    # Q2 — M 1: why it is number one — correct at position 1
    - id: 2
      image: "m_1-equ-31m.webp"
      text: >-
        The Crab Nebula heads Messier's catalog as M 1. Why does it get the
        first number?
      answers:
        - text: >-
            Messier found it in 1758 while following a comet, and it looked
            so much like one that it started his list.
          correct: true
          explanation: >-
            He called it the first of all the objects he had discovered,
            and it is the find that set him compiling the catalog. He
            later learned that John Bevis had seen it first, in 1731, and
            acknowledged it.
        - text: >-
            It is the brightest object in the catalog.
          correct: false
          explanation: >-
            It is much fainter than the Orion Nebula or the Pleiades, both
            of which are also in the catalog.
        - text: >-
            Messier was the first person ever to see it.
          correct: false
          explanation: >-
            John Bevis saw it in 1731, decades earlier. Messier found it
            independently.
        - text: >-
            The catalog is ordered by position in the sky, and the Crab
            comes first.
          correct: false
          explanation: >-
            Messier numbered objects roughly in the order he added them.
            M 2, right after the Crab, is in Aquarius, on the far side of
            the sky.

    # Q3 — M 45: why the Pleiades — correct at position 4
    - id: 3
      image: "m_45-s30-69m.webp"
      text: >-
        Messier's catalog was a list of things comet hunters might mistake
        for comets. Nobody could mistake the Pleiades for a comet. Why is
        it in the catalog as M 45?
      answers:
        - text: >-
            Messier discovered it.
          correct: false
          explanation: >-
            The Pleiades have been known since antiquity. Messier only
            measured its position, on March 4, 1769.
        - text: >-
            Lacaille had already listed it, so Messier included it too.
          correct: false
          explanation: >-
            Lacaille's 1755 catalog covered the southern sky. The Pleiades
            weren't in it.
        - text: >-
            Through Messier's telescope it looked fuzzy, like a comet.
          correct: false
          explanation: >-
            That's the opposite of the puzzle. In a telescope the Pleiades
            are unmistakably a group of stars.
        - text: >-
            Probably to make his list longer than Lacaille's 42 entries,
            though no one knows for certain.
          correct: true
          explanation: >-
            His list stood at 41 objects. On a single night, March 4, 1769,
            he measured four well-known bright objects (M 42 to M 45) and
            published 45. It was the only night he made an exception for
            objects that were already famous. In his introduction he said he wanted a catalog
            more complete than earlier ones. Owen Gingerich called the
            additions "frosting." Messier never said why himself.

    # Q4 — M 42 and M 43: one nebula, two numbers — correct at position 2
    - id: 4
      image: "m_42-equ-10m.webp"
      text: >-
        Just north of the Orion Nebula (M 42) sits a small comma of
        nebulosity with its own number, M 43. What is M 43?
      answers:
        - text: >-
            A separate nebula far behind M 42, lined up by chance.
          correct: false
          explanation: >-
            Not a chance alignment. M 43 belongs to the same cloud, at the
            same distance.
        - text: >-
            Part of the Orion Nebula, cut off from it by a lane of dark
            dust.
          correct: true
          explanation: >-
            Jean-Jacques d'Ortous de Mairan described it before 1731, and
            Messier gave it its own number in 1769. Physically it's one
            nebula; a dense dust lane in front makes it look like two.
        - text: >-
            Gas blown out of M 42 by a recent supernova.
          correct: false
          explanation: >-
            No supernova is involved. The gas in both glows because hot
            young stars inside it light it up.
        - text: >-
            A reflection nebula, shining only by reflected starlight.
          correct: false
          explanation: >-
            Like M 42, M 43 glows on its own: its gas is energized by a hot
            star embedded in it. For a true reflection nebula, look at
            M 78.

    # Q5 — M 47: the lost Messier — correct at position 3
    - id: 5
      image: "m_47-s50-3m.webp"
      text: >-
        For almost two hundred years, nothing in the sky matched Messier's
        position for M 47. How was it finally found?
      answers:
        - text: >-
            Someone noticed the cluster had drifted away from where
            Messier saw it.
          correct: false
          explanation: >-
            Star clusters do move, but far too slowly to shift visibly in
            two centuries.
        - text: >-
            It turned out to be a duplicate entry for M 46, which is nearby.
          correct: false
          explanation: >-
            M 46 and M 47 are neighbors, about a degree apart, but they are
            two different clusters, and Messier saw both.
        - text: >-
            Messier had made a sign error in his offsets from a reference
            star. Reverse it, and the position lands on a bright cluster.
          correct: true
          explanation: >-
            Messier measured M 47 from the star "2 Navis." Applying his
            offsets with the opposite signs gives NGC 2422, which T. F.
            Morris identified as M 47 in 1959. M 48 was "lost" too: Messier's
            declination was off by about 5 degrees.
        - text: >-
            It had faded, and was rediscovered when it brightened again.
          correct: false
          explanation: >-
            A cluster of stars doesn't fade and return as a whole. The
            problem was the written position, not the cluster.

    # Q6 — M 78: the reflection nebula — correct at position 1
    - id: 6
      image: "m_78-equ-30m.webp"
      text: >-
        M 78, above Orion's belt, is a reflection nebula. What makes it
        shine?
      answers:
        - text: >-
            Dust reflecting and scattering the light of nearby hot stars.
          correct: true
          explanation: >-
            Dust scatters blue light more strongly than red, which gives
            reflection nebulae their bluish color. M 78 is the brightest of
            a group that includes NGC 2064, 2067, and 2071, about 1,350
            light-years away. Méchain found it in 1780, the same year he
            found M 79.
        - text: >-
            Gas glowing because ultraviolet light from hot stars energizes
            it.
          correct: false
          explanation: >-
            That describes an emission nebula like M 42. M 78's light is
            mostly starlight bounced off dust instead.
        - text: >-
            A shell of gas thrown off by a dying star.
          correct: false
          explanation: >-
            That's a planetary nebula, like NGC 2438 in M 46.
        - text: >-
            Debris from an exploded star.
          correct: false
          explanation: >-
            That's a supernova remnant, like M 1.

    # Q7 — M 35 and NGC 2158 — correct at position 2
    - id: 7
      image: "m_35-s50-5m.webp"
      text: >-
        In a low-power view of M 35, a small, faint, grainy smudge sits
        just off its edge: the cluster NGC 2158. Why do the two look so
        different?
      answers:
        - text: >-
            NGC 2158 is a distant galaxy behind M 35.
          correct: false
          explanation: >-
            It's a star cluster in our own Galaxy, just much farther away
            than M 35.
        - text: >-
            NGC 2158 is about four times farther away and more than ten
            times older.
          correct: true
          explanation: >-
            M 35 is about 2,800 light-years away and roughly 150 million
            years old. NGC 2158 is well over a billion years old. Distance
            makes it small and faint; age has removed its brightest, most
            massive stars.
        - text: >-
            NGC 2158 is closer but has fewer stars.
          correct: false
          explanation: >-
            The other way around. It's the more distant cluster, and its
            stars look faint because they're far away.
        - text: >-
            NGC 2158 is a nebula, not a cluster.
          correct: false
          explanation: >-
            The grainy look is unresolved stars. A larger telescope or a
            longer exposure breaks it into individual stars.

    # Q8 — M 46 and NGC 2438 — correct at position 4
    - id: 8
      image: "m_46-s50-10m.webp"
      text: >-
        The planetary nebula NGC 2438 appears inside the open cluster M 46.
        What is its relationship to the cluster?
      answers:
        - text: >-
            It's a former cluster star that is now shedding its outer
            layers.
          correct: false
          explanation: >-
            That would be the natural story. The measured motions say
            otherwise.
        - text: >-
            It's far in the foreground, much closer to us than the cluster.
          correct: false
          explanation: >-
            A natural guess, but a 2008 study placed it at about the same
            distance as M 46.
        - text: >-
            It's a hole in the cluster's background glow, not a real
            object.
          correct: false
          explanation: >-
            It's a real planetary nebula: a shell of gas thrown off by a
            dying star.
        - text: >-
            It's about as far away as the cluster, but moving too
            differently to be a member.
          correct: true
          explanation: >-
            Kiss and colleagues (2008) found both at similar distances,
            but the nebula's speed along our line of sight differs from the
            cluster's by about 30 km/s. It's passing through, not part of
            the family.

    # Q9 — M 93: the prow of the ship — correct at position 1
    - id: 9
      image: "m_93-equ-7m.webp"
      text: >-
        In 1781 Messier described M 93 as lying "between the Greater Dog
        and the prow of the ship." Which ship did he mean?
      answers:
        - text: >-
            Argo Navis, the ship of the Argonauts
          correct: true
          explanation: >-
            Argo Navis was a huge constellation, later split up by Lacaille
            into Puppis, Carina, and Vela. Messier also measured M 47 from a
            star he called "2 Navis." M 46, M 47, and M 93 are all in Puppis
            today.
        - text: >-
            Puppis
          correct: false
          explanation: >-
            M 93 is in Puppis today, but Puppis is only a piece of the ship
            Messier had in mind.
        - text: >-
            Carina
          correct: false
          explanation: >-
            Carina, the keel, is another piece of the same ship, farther
            south than M 93.
        - text: >-
            Columba
          correct: false
          explanation: >-
            Columba lies near Canis Major, but it's a dove, not a ship.

    # Q10 — M 41: the oldest record — correct at position 3
    - id: 10
      image: "m_41-s50-12m.webp"
      text: >-
        M 41, just south of Sirius, may hold an unusual distinction among
        Messier objects. What is it?
      answers:
        - text: >-
            It was Messier's very first discovery.
          correct: false
          explanation: >-
            That honor goes to M 1, in 1758. Messier recorded M 41 in 1765,
            and Giovanni Hodierna had seen it before 1654.
        - text: >-
            It is the nearest open cluster to the Sun.
          correct: false
          explanation: >-
            At about 2,360 light-years it isn't close by cluster
            standards. The Hyades, in Taurus, are much nearer.
        - text: >-
            It may have been recorded by Aristotle, around 325 BC.
          correct: true
          explanation: >-
            The identification is possible, not certain. M 41 was also the
            object that brought Messier's list to 41, the number he later
            padded out to 45.
        - text: >-
            It is the only Messier object found without a telescope.
          correct: false
          explanation: >-
            Several were known to the naked eye long before telescopes,
            including the Pleiades and the Beehive.

    # Q11 — M 67: the survivor — correct at position 2
    - id: 11
      image: "m_67-s30-17m.webp"
      text: >-
        Most open clusters break apart within a few hundred million years.
        M 67 in Cancer is about 4 billion years old. How has it survived?
      answers:
        - text: >-
            It's so massive that its gravity holds it together.
          correct: false
          explanation: >-
            Mass helps, but M 67 has already lost most of its original
            stars. It survived because of where it is.
        - text: >-
            Its orbit keeps it well above the Galactic plane, away from
            the gas clouds that tear clusters apart.
          correct: true
          explanation: >-
            M 67 sits about 1,400 light-years above the plane and crosses it
            only briefly. Close passes by giant molecular clouds are what
            pull most clusters apart. It isn't the oldest open cluster
            known, but at about 4 billion years it's remarkably old for one.
        - text: >-
            It was captured recently from another galaxy.
          correct: false
          explanation: >-
            There's no evidence for that. Its survival is explained by its
            orbit within our own Galaxy.
        - text: >-
            A dark cloud shields it from disruption.
          correct: false
          explanation: >-
            Clouds of gas and dust are the danger, not the protection.

    # Q12 — M 44: Galileo's "cloud" — correct at position 4
    - id: 12
      image: "m_44-s30-36m.webp"
      text: >-
        Around 130 BC Hipparchus listed the Beehive cluster (M 44) as
        Nephelion, "Little Cloud." What did Galileo's telescope show it to
        be in 1610?
      answers:
        - text: >-
            A cloud of glowing gas.
          correct: false
          explanation: >-
            That's what "nebula" meant to observers then, and it's what
            Galileo's telescope disproved here.
        - text: >-
            A comet that never moved.
          correct: false
          explanation: >-
            Praesepe had been watched for nearly two thousand years in the
            same place. Comets move.
        - text: >-
            Nothing: it was too faint for his small telescope.
          correct: false
          explanation: >-
            It's visible to the naked eye from a dark site, and an easy
            target even for Galileo's small telescope.
        - text: >-
            A swarm of more than forty small stars.
          correct: true
          explanation: >-
            In Sidereus Nuncius he reported that Praesepe was not one star
            but a mass of more than forty small stars. Several of the old
            "nebulous" stars turned out the same way. The Beehive is about
            600 light-years away and roughly 600 to 700 million years old.
