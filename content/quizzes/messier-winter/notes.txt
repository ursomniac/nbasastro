## Fact pass 1 results (2026-10-21)

Proposed set: 11 questions (within the 8–12 range). Bob to confirm, or add a 12th.

KEEP (verified):
1. **The only globular** → M79 (Lepus). Méchain 1780; ~41,000 ly away. Origin: the Milky Way
   "might have" taken it from another galaxy, possibly the Canis Major Dwarf — say "may have",
   not "was" (NASA Hubble Messier page). Link `galactic-anticenter` for why winter has so few.
2. **Why M1 is first** → Bevis saw it first (1731). Messier found it independently on
   Aug 28, 1758 while following the comet of 1758 (De la Nux); it looked like a comet. He called
   it "the first of all that I have discovered" and it heads the first catalog (Mém. Acad. 1771)
   (SEDS M1). The comet-hunting motive for the catalog as a whole is standard.
3. **Why is the Pleiades on a list of things not to mistake for comets?** → REFRAMED: correct
   answer is "No one knows for certain." Facts: Messier observed M45 on March 4, 1769; the first
   catalog (1771) had 45 objects, only 17 his own discoveries; it included objects found by
   others (SEDS M45; encyclopedia.pub). Popular guesses (rounding to 45; outnumbering Lacaille's
   42 southern nebulae) — NO source found; may appear only as "one popular guess", or not at all.
4. **M42 vs M43** → M43 (De Mairan's Nebula, found before 1731) is physically part of the Orion
   Nebula, but a dense dust lane (the northeast dark lane) makes it look separate. Messier
   catalogued M43 in 1769 (Wikipedia M43). Don't claim to know WHY Messier gave it its own number.
5. **The lost Messier** → M47. Hodierna before 1654; Messier 1771. His position — measured as
   offsets from the star "2 Navis" — matches nothing; apply the offsets with the opposite signs
   and you land on NGC 2422. Identified by T. F. Morris, 1959 (SEDS M47; Wikipedia M47).
   Wording: "a sign error in his offsets" (SEDS and Wikipedia differ on which coordinate).
   Distractor/aside: M48 was also "lost" — Messier's declination was off by about 5° (Wikipedia
   M48); Oswald Thomas identified it before Morris (SEDS Morris bio).
6. **The reflection nebula** → M78. Méchain 1780; ~1,350 ly; "the brightest diffuse reflection
   nebula in a group" incl. NGC 2064/2067/2071 (Wikipedia M78). Don't say brightest in the sky.
7. **Same field, much older and farther** → M35 (~2,800 ly, ~150 Myr) with NGC 2158 (about 4×
   farther, over 10× older, i.e., >1.5 Gyr) (APOD 2021 May 10).
8. **The planetary nebula that isn't a member** → M46 / NGC 2438. CORRECTION: not "in front of"
   — it lies at about the same distance (1.4–1.9 kpc vs cluster 1.5–1.7 kpc) but moves ~30 km/s
   differently along our line of sight (78 vs 48 km/s), so it isn't a member now
   (Kiss et al. 2008, arXiv 0809.0327). Good "looks related, isn't" question.
13'. **REWRITTEN: "the prow of the ship"** → Messier described M93 (March 20, 1781) as "between
   the Greater Dog and the prow of the ship." Which ship? → Argo Navis, the giant constellation
   later split by Lacaille (1750s) into Puppis, Carina, Vela (and Pyxis, per BAA). Messier also
   used "2 Navis" as M47's reference star. M46, M47, M93 are now in Puppis
   (Universe Today M93; SEDS M47; BAA "Sailing with the Argonauts").
   [verify: IAU formal adoption date of Puppis, if quoted]
15. **M67's survival** → ~4 Gyr (estimates 3.2–5); ~2,600–2,900 ly away (Wikipedia M67). CORRECTION:
   "one of the oldest known" — Wikipedia: "M67 is not the oldest known open cluster." Why it
   survived: it sits ~1,400 ly above the Galactic plane (consistent with b = +31.9° at ~2,800 ly)
   and crosses the plane only briefly, away from the giant molecular clouds that tear clusters
   apart (Ken Croswell). Distractors: "very massive" (it has lost most of its original mass —
   initial ~10× current per Hurley et al.), "recently captured", "protected by a dark cloud".
