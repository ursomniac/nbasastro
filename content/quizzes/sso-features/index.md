    # Q1 — Olympus Mons: why so big — correct at position 3
    - id: 1
      image: "olympus-mons.webp"
      text: >-
        Olympus Mons on Mars stands about 21 km above the Martian datum,
        more than twice the height of Everest above sea level. What let a
        volcano grow that large?
      answers:
        - text: >-
            Mars's stronger gravity packed the lava into a taller pile.
          correct: false
          explanation: >-
            Mars's surface gravity is only about 38% of Earth's. Weaker
            gravity helps a tall mountain support itself; it isn't what
            built it.
        - text: >-
            Colliding crustal plates pushed it up, like the Himalayas.
          correct: false
          explanation: >-
            Olympus Mons is a shield volcano, built from lava flows rather
            than folded crust, and Mars shows no Earth-style moving plates.
        - text: >-
            The crust stayed fixed over one hotspot, so lava kept piling up
            in the same place.
          correct: true
          explanation: >-
            On Earth, plate motion carries a volcano away from its hotspot
            (the Hawaiian chain is the result). On Mars the crust stays
            put, so one volcano gets all the lava.
        - text: >-
            A single enormous eruption built it all at once.
          correct: false
          explanation: >-
            It grew through countless flows over a very long span of
            Martian history, not in one event.

    # Q2 — Caloris Basin: the name — correct at position 1
    - id: 2
      image: "caloris.webp"
      text: >-
        Mercury's largest impact basin, about 1,550 km across, is called
        Caloris. What does the name refer to?
      answers:
        - text: >-
            Heat: the Sun is almost directly overhead there at every second
            perihelion.
          correct: true
          explanation: >-
            "Calor" is Latin for heat. Mercury's spin and orbit are locked
            so that the same two longitudes take turns facing the Sun at
            closest approach, and Caloris sits near one of them.
        - text: >-
            The astronomer who discovered it.
          correct: false
          explanation: >-
            Caloris was found in Mariner 10 images in 1974. No person is
            involved in the name.
        - text: >-
            A Roman painter, following the rule for naming Mercury's
            craters.
          correct: false
          explanation: >-
            Mercury's craters really are named for artists, musicians,
            painters, and authors, but Caloris isn't one of them. The name
            is Latin, not a person.
        - text: >-
            Its floor is still molten.
          correct: false
          explanation: >-
            The basin is roughly 3.8 to 3.9 billion years old, and the
            plains that fill it are long solid.

    # Q3 — Maxwell Montes: the Venus exception — correct at position 4
    - id: 3
      image: "maxwell-montes.webp"
      text: >-
        Features on Venus are named for women and goddesses, with only
        three exceptions. Which of these is one of the exceptions?
      answers:
        - text: >-
            Ishtar Terra
          correct: false
          explanation: >-
            Ishtar was the Babylonian goddess of love. The "continents" of
            Venus are named for goddesses of love.
        - text: >-
            Aphrodite Terra
          correct: false
          explanation: >-
            Aphrodite is the Greek counterpart of Venus herself, so this
            one follows the rule exactly.
        - text: >-
            Lakshmi Planum
          correct: false
          explanation: >-
            Lakshmi is the Hindu goddess of prosperity; the plateaus of
            Venus are named for goddesses of prosperity.
        - text: >-
            Maxwell Montes
          correct: true
          explanation: >-
            Named for physicist James Clerk Maxwell. It, Alpha Regio, and
            Beta Regio were named from early Earth-based radar maps, before
            the convention existed, and the names were kept.

    # Q4 — Pele on Io: the red ring — correct at position 2
    - id: 4
      image: "io-pele.webp"
      text: >-
        Io's volcano Pele sits inside a red ring well over 1,000 km across.
        What is the ring?
      answers:
        - text: >-
            The rim of a giant impact crater.
          correct: false
          explanation: >-
            The ring has changed shape between spacecraft visits, which a
            crater rim can't do. Io's surface is renewed so quickly that
            impact craters don't survive on it.
        - text: >-
            Sulfur-rich fallout from Pele's volcanic plume.
          correct: true
          explanation: >-
            Pele's plume rises roughly 300 to 400 km and rains material
            back down in a ring. Voyager 1's discovery of that plume in
            March 1979 confirmed that Io is volcanically active.
        - text: >-
            Glowing lava flowing outward from the vent.
          correct: false
          explanation: >-
            Pele does have a lava lake, but it is tiny compared with the
            ring. The ring is a surface deposit seen in reflected sunlight.
        - text: >-
            A color artifact of the camera.
          correct: false
          explanation: >-
            It's real, and it has been imaged by Voyager, Galileo, and
            later spacecraft. Voyager's cameras were insensitive to red, so
            they recorded the ring as dark.

    # Q5 — Conamara Chaos, Europa — correct at position 3
    - id: 5
      image: "conamara-chaos.webp"
      text: >-
        Galileo's close-ups of Conamara Chaos on Europa show a jumble of
        angular blocks set in rough, lower ground. What does this terrain
        record?
      answers:
        - text: >-
            Flows of molten rock that cooled and cracked.
          correct: false
          explanation: >-
            Europa's surface is water ice. Whatever moved here was icy or
            watery, not rock.
        - text: >-
            Dunes of ice grains piled up by wind.
          correct: false
          explanation: >-
            Europa has almost no atmosphere, so there is no wind to move
            anything.
        - text: >-
            Crust that broke into rafts, which drifted and rotated before
            freezing in place again.
          correct: true
          explanation: >-
            The blocks carry older ridge patterns that can be fitted back
            together like puzzle pieces. They moved in something mobile:
            liquid water, slush, or warm ice.
        - text: >-
            Open water photographed between floating icebergs.
          correct: false
          explanation: >-
            Everything in the image is frozen solid. Chaos terrain is cited
            as evidence for an ocean below, but the images alone can't say
            whether the blocks moved in water, slush, or warm ice.

    # Q6 — Tiger stripes, Enceladus — correct at position 1
    - id: 6
      image: "enceladus-tiger-stripes.webp"
      text: >-
        Four long fractures cross the south pole of Saturn's moon
        Enceladus, nicknamed the "tiger stripes." What did Cassini find
        erupting from them?
      answers:
        - text: >-
            Water vapor and ice grains.
          correct: true
          explanation: >-
            Cassini discovered the plumes in 2005 and later flew through
            them. Some of the grains are salty, which points to liquid salt
            water below rather than surface ice turning to vapor.
        - text: >-
            Molten sulfur.
          correct: false
          explanation: >-
            Sulfur volcanism is Io's specialty, at Jupiter. Enceladus is
            an ice world.
        - text: >-
            Liquid methane.
          correct: false
          explanation: >-
            Right planet, wrong moon: liquid methane belongs to Titan.
        - text: >-
            Nitrogen gas.
          correct: false
          explanation: >-
            Nitrogen-driven plumes are the leading explanation for the
            dark streaks Voyager 2 saw on Neptune's moon Triton, not for
            Enceladus.

    # Q7 — Kraken Mare, Titan — correct at position 2
    - id: 7
      image: "kraken-mare.webp"
      text: >-
        Kraken Mare, mapped by Cassini's radar, is the largest sea on
        Saturn's moon Titan. What is it a sea of?
      answers:
        - text: >-
            Liquid water.
          correct: false
          explanation: >-
            Titan's surface is near -180 °C. Water there is as hard as
            rock; it's what the "bedrock" is made of.
        - text: >-
            Liquid methane, mixed with ethane.
          correct: true
          explanation: >-
            Sunlight steadily converts methane to ethane, so ethane-rich
            seas were expected. Cassini's radar found Kraken dominated by
            methane instead, which is still not fully explained.
        - text: >-
            Liquid nitrogen.
          correct: false
          explanation: >-
            Nitrogen is the main gas in Titan's atmosphere, and some of it
            dissolves in the seas, but the liquid itself is hydrocarbon.
        - text: >-
            Nothing. Like the lunar maria, it's a dry plain.
          correct: false
          explanation: >-
            The Moon's "seas" are solidified lava. Titan's are the only
            stable bodies of surface liquid known beyond Earth.

    # Q8 — Verona Rupes: which world — correct at position 4
    - id: 8
      image: "verona-rupes.webp"
      text: >-
        Verona Rupes may be the tallest cliff in the solar system. It has
        been photographed by exactly one spacecraft, once. Where is it?
      answers:
        - text: >-
            Mercury
          correct: false
          explanation: >-
            Mercury has plenty of great scarps, but its rupes are named for
            ships of discovery, such as Discovery Rupes.
        - text: >-
            Mars
          correct: false
          explanation: >-
            Mars holds the tallest volcano and the largest canyon system,
            but not this cliff.
        - text: >-
            Titania, moon of Uranus
          correct: false
          explanation: >-
            Right planet and right author: Titania's features also come
            from Shakespeare. Wrong moon.
        - text: >-
            Miranda, moon of Uranus
          correct: true
          explanation: >-
            Voyager 2 imaged it in January 1986. Features on Miranda are
            named from Shakespeare; Verona is the setting of Romeo and
            Juliet. With only one flyby's images, height estimates run from
            5 to 20 km.

    # Q9 — Sputnik Planitia, Pluto — correct at position 1
    - id: 9
      image: "sputnik-planitia.webp"
      text: >-
        Sputnik Planitia, the western lobe of Pluto's "heart," is a bright
        plain with no confirmed impact craters, divided into polygons tens
        of kilometers across. What is it mostly made of?
      answers:
        - text: >-
            Nitrogen ice.
          correct: true
          explanation: >-
            At Pluto's temperatures nitrogen ice is soft enough to flow.
            The polygons are convection cells, slowly overturning and
            erasing craters. A 2026 study suggests liquid nitrogen may even
            well up through cracks at the plain's northern edge.
        - text: >-
            Water ice.
          correct: false
          explanation: >-
            Water ice on Pluto is rigid and behaves like rock. It forms the
            crust beneath the plain and the mountains along its edge.
        - text: >-
            Carbon dioxide ice.
          correct: false
          explanation: >-
            Dry ice is a feature of the Martian polar caps. Pluto's
            volatile ices are nitrogen, methane, and carbon monoxide.
        - text: >-
            Salt.
          correct: false
          explanation: >-
            Bright salt deposits are the answer for a different dwarf
            planet: Ceres.

    # Q10 — Occator bright spots, Ceres — correct at position 3
    - id: 10
      image: "occator.webp"
      text: >-
        As Dawn approached Ceres in 2015, bright spots in Occator crater
        stood out against the dark surface. What did they turn out to be?
      answers:
        - text: >-
            Patches of exposed water ice.
          correct: false
          explanation: >-
            This was the early favorite, and it's wrong, though water is
            part of the story: the material was left behind by brine.
        - text: >-
            Glowing lava.
          correct: false
          explanation: >-
            The spots shine by reflected sunlight. They only look brilliant
            because the rest of Ceres is so dark.
        - text: >-
            Salt deposits, mostly sodium carbonate.
          correct: true
          explanation: >-
            Salty water reached the surface and the water was lost, leaving
            a bright crust behind. The central spot is named Cerealia
            Facula, after a Roman agricultural festival.
        - text: >-
            Metal from the object that made the crater.
          correct: false
          explanation: >-
            Dawn's spectrometer identified carbonate salts, not metal.

    # Q11 — Saturn's hexagon: discovery — correct at position 2
    - id: 11
      image: "saturn-hexagon.webp"
      text: >-
        A six-sided jet stream circles Saturn's north pole. How was it
        discovered?
      answers:
        - text: >-
            Galileo sketched it at the telescope in 1610.
          correct: false
          explanation: >-
            Galileo's telescope couldn't even show Saturn's rings as rings.
            The hexagon is small, low-contrast, and wraps around the pole.
        - text: >-
            In 1988, in Voyager images taken years earlier.
          correct: true
          explanation: >-
            The Voyagers flew past near Saturn's equator and saw the pole
            only at a slant. The hexagon appeared when David Godfrey
            re-projected the images into a view from above the pole.
        - text: >-
            The Hubble Space Telescope found it in the 1990s.
          correct: false
          explanation: >-
            Hubble and ground-based telescopes confirmed it in the early
            1990s, after the Voyager discovery.
        - text: >-
            Cassini found it on arriving at Saturn.
          correct: false
          explanation: >-
            Cassini gave us the famous views, but the north pole was in
            winter darkness when it arrived. Its first look at the hexagon
            was in infrared.

    # Q12 — Neptune's Great Dark Spot — correct at position 4
    - id: 12
      image: "great-dark-spot.webp"
      text: >-
        Voyager 2 photographed an Earth-sized storm on Neptune in 1989,
        the Great Dark Spot. What has happened to it since?
      answers:
        - text: >-
            It's still there, as long-lived as Jupiter's Great Red Spot.
          correct: false
          explanation: >-
            That was the expectation. Neptune's dark spots turn out to last
            years, not centuries.
        - text: >-
            It was shown to be an imaging artifact.
          correct: false
          explanation: >-
            It was real: Voyager tracked it for weeks and measured winds
            around it.
        - text: >-
            It drifted north, where Hubble found it again.
          correct: false
          explanation: >-
            Hubble did find a dark spot in the northern hemisphere, but it
            is regarded as a new storm, not the old one relocated.
        - text: >-
            It had vanished by the time Hubble looked in 1994.
          correct: true
          explanation: >-
            Hubble has since watched several dark spots appear and fade.
            The original was seen up close by one spacecraft, for a few
            weeks.
