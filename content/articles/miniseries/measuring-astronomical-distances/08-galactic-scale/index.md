## Introduction

Throughout this series we've been measuring outward: the Earth, the Sun, the nearest stars, and then
-- once parallax ran out -- the first rungs of a distance ladder built from stars whose true brightness we
could trust. With that in hand, we can finally answer a question that would have stopped
any astronomer a century ago: *where are we?*

The answer is that the Sun sits about 8.2 kiloparsecs -- roughly 26,700 light-years -- from the center of the Milky
Way. That number is now known to better than one percent.   It's part of our "galactic address".

{{< nbas-image src="fig-galaxy-overview.webp" width="600"
caption="An artist's concept of the Milky Way seen from above, based on infrared surveys -- the kind of picture we can only draw, never photograph. Labels added; the scale is set by the Sun's 8.2 kpc distance from the center. Credit: NASA/JPL-Caltech/R. Hurt (SSC-Caltech)."
alt="Artist's view of a barred spiral galaxy seen face-on, with a bright central bar and spiral arms winding outward. A gold line runs from the Sun, partway out in the disk below the bar, up to the center, labeled 8.2 kpc (26,700 light-years). A scale bar in the lower left marks 5 kpc." >}}

But an address isn't a map. Knowing how far you live from the middle of town tells you nothing
about which streets run where, where the old neighborhoods are, or what's on the other side of the
river. And we have a problem that no mapmaker on Earth ever faced: we can't leave and survey from different
locations and perspectives to really understand the terrain.  _Every_ measurement of our Galaxy is made from 
inside it from a single vantage point, looking out through the very thing we're trying to map.  

This chapter is about how that map got made -- and about a quiet shift that happens along the
way, though honestly we're at the point where the map is *still* being made, and portions are still marked "Here be dragons"!
Until now, every new measurement in this series was built to answer a question someone was already asking. 
Here, for the first time, the measurements start providing information about questions that nobody had thought to ask.

The next chapter, [A Tour of the Galaxy](/articles/2027/01/a-tour-of-the-milky-way-galaxy/), tours the map.  We'll get to 
take our understanding of the distances and scales we've established for our "neck of the galactic woods" and compare 
them to other regions of the Galaxy.

## Three Maps, Three Mistakes

Three times in two centuries, astronomers drew a confident map of the Milky Way. All three were
wrong, and they were wrong in the same interesting way: each map only showed the edge of its maker's
view, and not the edge of the Galaxy.

### Herschel: the edge of a telescope

In 1785 William Herschel published "star gauges" of 683 patches of sky, counted with his
20-foot reflector. His reasoning was simple: if stars are spread more or less evenly through
space, then a direction with more stars must be a direction where the system goes deeper. Count
the stars in each direction, and you can sketch the shape of the whole.

{{< nbas-image src="Herschel-Galaxy.webp" 
caption="William Herschel's cross-section of the Milky Way, from his 1785 star gauges (Phil. Trans. 75, 1785; drawing credited to Caroline Herschel). The Sun is near the middle; the cleft on one side is dust. Public domain, via Wikimedia Commons."
alt="Herschel's 1785 cross-section of the Milky Way: a flattened, ragged outline with the Sun near the center and a cleft on one side" >}}

(It's often said that Herschel assumed every star was equally bright. He did use that
idea elsewhere -- we met it in [Building the Distance Ladder](/articles/2026/12/building-the-distance-ladder/) -- but the
star gauges didn't need it.)

Herschel's gauges rested on two assumptions: that stars are spread evenly, and that through his telescope he would see
*all* of them, right out to the edge.  The last one is where the hypothesis broke -- badly. His famous cross-section diagram
-- a flattened, ragged slab with the Sun near the middle and a deep cleft on one side (making it look like a bird with a large 
open beak) -- is only a map of how far his telescope _could_ see.  That cleft (not understood at the time) is dust.

The remarkable part is that Herschel eventually figured this out himself. By 1817, larger telescopes were
showing him stars the smaller ones had missed, along with a faint haze he couldn't resolve at
all. He concluded that even his giant 40-foot instrument couldn't reach the bottom of the Milky
Way. He had discovered that his map was a portrait of his own limits.

{{< clear >}}

### Kapteyn: the middle of the fog

A century later, Jacobus Kapteyn tried again with vastly better data: photographic star counts
from fields spread all over the sky, combined with the statistical distance methods he'd helped invent. His
model, finished around 1920--22, was a flattened system with the Sun only about 650 parsecs from its center.

Why so central? Because in fog, you always seem to be standing in the middle of what you can
see. Dust dims distant stars about equally in every direction along the plane of the Galaxy, so
the visible "edge" lands at the same distance all the way around you -- wherever you happen to
stand.

{{< nbas-image src="fig-fog.webp" fullwidth="true"
caption="Why Kapteyn put the Sun near the middle (schematic). The fog is the same everywhere, so each observer sees only the stars near enough to shine through it -- and the visible patch is centered on the observer, wherever they stand."
alt="Two panels, each an even fog with a dashed oval marking the real system. In each, a blue glow of visible starlight surrounds a gold observer: near the middle of the oval in the left panel, near its edge in the right panel. Each glow is centered on its observer, not on the oval." >}}

But Kapteyn did not simply forget about dust. He wrote four papers on interstellar absorption
between 1904 and 1914, and in 1909 he proposed that dust would redden starlight. What talked him
out of it was a 1916 result from Harlow Shapley: the stars of the globular cluster M 13 _weren't_
nearly as red as Kapteyn's absorption predicted. Space, Shapley concluded, was transparent.

But ironically, they were both right -- just that it matters in what direction you're looking.  M 13 sits more 
than 40° above the plane of the Milky Way, where there's less dust to find. Shapley had tested the window, not the fog.


### Shapley: around the fog, but too far

Shapley's own map, from 1918, escaped the fog by using globular clusters, which swarm above and
below the dusty plane where they're easy to see. They bunch toward Sagittarius, and he reasoned
-- correctly -- that they must be centered on the middle of the Galaxy. That put the Sun far out
toward the edge, about 50,000 light-years from the center of a system some 300,000 light-years
across by his estimation.

{{< nbas-image src="fig-globulars.webp" fullwidth="true"

He got the direction right but the scale badly wrong: both numbers were roughly two to three
times too large. The reasons -- a calibration problem with his variable stars, plus the dust he'd
just declared absent -- are the story of [Building the Distance Ladder](/articles/2026/12/building-the-distance-ladder/),
and the view from the winter side of the sky is in
[The Galactic Anticenter](/articles/2026/12/the-view-out-the-back-door-the-galactic-anticenter/).

Three maps, each a faithful picture of how far its maker could see. That's worth remembering
when we get to the modern map, because even that has edges.

## Why the Kiloparsec

Each scale in this series has had its own natural ruler: the astronomical unit for the Solar
System, the parsec for the nearest stars. For the Galaxy it's the kiloparsec -- a thousand
parsecs, about 3,260 light-years.

The kiloparsec earns its place the same way the parsec did in
[Parsecs vs. Light Years](/articles/2026/12/parsecs-vs.-light-years/): it fits the instrument. Gaia measures
parallaxes in milliarcseconds, and a parallax of one milliarcsecond means a distance of exactly
one kiloparsec. So the same simple rule still works -- distance is one over parallax -- with the
units shifted by a factor of a thousand on both sides.   Plus it keeps the numbers small -- no line of
trailing zeros.

In kiloparsecs, the Galaxy's numbers come back down to human size:

- the center: 8.2 kpc away
- the edge of the stellar disk: about 14 kpc from the center, so the disk is nearly 30 kpc across
- the nearest star, Proxima Centauri: 0.0013 kpc

If you want to create a physical model, shrink the distance to Proxima down to 5 millimeters or just under &frac14; inch, and
the disk of the Milky Way becomes about a football field wide, with the Sun around the 15-yard line.   The AU, of course, would be
microscopic at this scale -- 20 nm, and the Sun only 0.17 nm across (smaller than a water molecule)!

is about 21 kpc away -- roughly 68,000 light-years. The light reaching your eye from it tonight

M 75 is also on the far side of the Galaxy:  about 9 kpc *beyond* the center.
So, how can we see it at all, through the whole dusty disk?  Because it sits about 9 kpc *below* the plane.
Its light never crosses the dusty middle of the Milky Way. It slips underneath.

## Two Walls: Geometry and Dust

Mapping the Galaxy means getting past two walls. One is built into the geometry of the measurement;
the other is sitting in space.

### The geometry wall: precision, not distance

Parallax doesn't stop working once you reach some fixed distance. What happens is subtler. Every
measurement has a fixed uncertainty in *angular* units, while the parallax itself shrinks with distance.
Sooner or later the uncertainty is as large as the thing being measured, and the distance dissolves into a guess.

Gaia shows the effect clearly. For a star of magnitude 15, its typical parallax uncertainty is
about 0.03 milliarcseconds. At 1 kpc, where the parallax is 1 milliarcsecond, that makes the distance
good to about 3 percent. At the distance of the Galactic center, however, the same uncertainty is closer
to a quarter of the whole -- 25%.  So the geometry wall isn't so much a "wall" at all -- it's a slow fade,
with good distance measures giving way to more uncertain ones until the uncertainty dominates.

### The dust wall: a matter of color

The second wall is the one Robert Trumpler found in 1930, and the one every rung of the ladder
has had to correct for ever since. In the plane of the Milky Way it isn't a correction anymore. It's a curtain.

Toward the Galactic center, visible light is dimmed by at least 30 magnitudes. That's a factor
of about a trillion. Near-infrared light, coming from the same place through the same dust, is only
dimmed by ~3 magnitudes -- a factor of about sixteen.   

{{< nbas-image src="eso0949i.webp" fullwidth="true"
caption="The same small patch of sky toward the Galactic center, about 12 arcminutes across, in visible light (MPG/ESO 2.2-meter telescope) and in infrared (VISTA). Infrared reveals huge numbers of faint red stars that dust hides completely in visible light. Credit: ESO/VISTA (CC BY 4.0)."
alt="Side-by-side images of the same star field: the visible-light view shows scattered stars and dark dust; the infrared view is packed with thousands of faint reddish stars." >}}

That wavelength dependence is the whole key. Dust blocks blue light far more effectively than red, red more than
infrared, and radio waves hardly at all. The dust wall isn't "we can't see." It's "we have to see
differently."

There's a historical irony here as well.  Through the early twentieth century, astronomy's workhorse
detector was the photographic plate -- but early plate emulsions were _far_ more sensitive to blue light
than to red. Dust steals the blue, but the plate images saw mostly blue. The astronomers trying to map the
Galaxy were working with exactly the color range _least_ able to get through the fog. Part of the wall 
was in the emulsion.

Getting past both walls meant changing the light we looked with: infrared to see through the
dust, radio to see through almost everything. But it also meant changing what we looked *for* -- no longer
just individual stars, but patterns in where whole populations of stars turn out to be.

## How We Know What's There

Nobody has ever photographed the Milky Way from outside. Every picture you've seen of our Galaxy as a
whole -- the glowing spiral with a bar across its middle -- is a drawing, or a picture of some
other galaxy standing in for ours. So how do we know what's out there?

Each part of the Galaxy leaves its own signature. Not in how the sky looks, but
in where stars turn out to be once we determine how far away they are:

| Part | How we know it's there | Tools |
|------|------------------------|-------|
| **Disk** | Star distances stretch far along the band of the Milky Way but stay within a thin layer above and below it. | parallax, star counts |
| **Spiral arms** | Distances to young things -- bright blue stars, glowing gas, newborn clusters -- bunch into bands instead of spreading evenly. | young tracers, radio |
| **Bulge** | Infrared maps of the whole sky show a glow around the center that's invisible in visible light. In 1994, maps from NASA's COBE satellite showed it as boxy, and "very bar-like." | infrared |
| **Bar** | Red clump stars -- a near-standard candle -- on one side of the center are closer than those on the other side. The middle of the Galaxy is elongated, about 10 kpc end to end, tilted about 30° from our line of sight to the center. | red clump distances |
| **Halo** | Globular clusters and RR Lyrae stars form a huge, round, thinning cloud far beyond the disk. Fast-moving old stars on tilted orbits pass through our own neighborhood. | globular cluster and RR Lyrae distances, motions |

Part of how we know we live in a barred spiral is honestly a matter of analogy: from inside, the
Milky Way looks like the edge-on spirals we photograph elsewhere, and our measurements fit that
picture far better than any other. We understand our own galaxy mostly from comparing with others,
imagining what the view would be with the Sun placed inside other galaxies, and then comparing that view
with what we see from home.

The rest of this chapter fills in this table -- and then watches two of the rows turn out to be
stranger than anyone expected.

## Spiral Arms -- Mapping a Forest from Inside It

{{< nbas-image src="m_101-s30-67m.webp" width="350" align="right"
caption="What we can't photograph of our own Galaxy, we can see in others. M 101, the Pinwheel Galaxy, nearly face-on: its arms stand out where young stars and glowing gas are concentrated, while older stars fill in the disk more smoothly. Bob Donahue, NBAS, Seestar S30, 67 min."
alt="A face-on spiral galaxy with a bright core and several loose, knotty spiral arms spreading out across a dark star field." >}}
The arms, paradoxically, are the hardest part to map, and the reason is worth understanding 
before anything else: a spiral arm isn't so much a thing -- it's a pattern.

First, a common misconception: the arms aren't the *structure* of a galaxy. They're a side effect
of something going on underneath.

The stars of the disk _don't_ stay put in the arms. They drift in and out of them as they orbit,
the way cars move through a traffic jam that stays in roughly the same place on the highway.
A star doesn't "know" it's in an arm; it just keeps to its orbit around the center, alongside its
neighbors. All that changes is the crowding: inside an arm there are simply more of them
around -- Galactic traffic congestion.

Gas feels that congestion far more than stars do. Where it piles up, it gets dense enough to
collapse and form new stars. The brightest, bluest of those stars live only a few million years,
too short a time to wander far from where they were born. So the arms glow with young stars and
glowing gas, while the old, ordinary stars that make up most of the disk are spread out much more
evenly. To map the arms, map the *young things*.

That's easy to say and hard to do, because so many of the young things live in the 
dusty plane, right behind the dust wall.

### A velocity becomes a ruler

This is where radio comes into play. Cold hydrogen gas gives off a faint radio signal at a wavelength of
21 centimeters; carbon monoxide in molecular clouds has other radio signals of its own. Radio goes
straight through the dust. Better still, each signal comes in at a precise frequency, and if the
gas is moving toward or away from us, that frequency shifts -- the same Doppler effect that drops
the pitch of a passing siren. The shift tells you the gas's speed along your line of sight.

Here's the trick, in the same four steps as the figure below.

1. **Measure how much hydrogen arrives at each speed.** Point a radio telescope in one direction
   and around 21 centimeters you'll see a feature that's broadened and smeared.   That's the 21 cm
   emission moved around by all of the hydrogen emitting features along your line of sight: at
   different distances and also with their own peculiar motions.  A bump shows extra gas at a particular speed.
2. **Each speed belongs to one ring.** Across most of its width, the disk -- gas and stars alike,
   the Sun included -- circles the center of the Galaxy at roughly the same speed, a little over
   200 kilometers per second. Same speed, but not the same circle. Gas on a smaller circle has less
   distance to cover, so it gets around faster -- the way runners on the inside lanes of a track,
   all running the same pace, keep pulling ahead of the runners outside them. From our seat on our
   own circle, the closer a ring is to the center, the faster its gas pulls ahead of us, and the
   bigger the speed we measure. Gas *outside* our circle falls behind and comes toward us.
3. **Each ring sits at one or two distances.** Walk out along the line of sight and the rings you
   cross get smaller, then bigger again. So the speed climbs, peaks at the line's closest approach
   to the center, and falls back. Inside our own circle, every speed turns up twice.
4. **Put the gas on the map.** Each bump in the spectrum becomes a pile of gas at one or two
   possible places along the line -- a candidate spiral arm, seen straight through the dust.

The speed of light turned travel time into distance back in the Solar System. This is something 
new: here, the motion of the thing itself tells us where it is. It won’t be the last time.

### Two answers, or none

Knowing the ring isn't quite knowing the distance.

A velocity ruler has three weak spots:

- **Two answers.** A line of sight aimed toward the inner Galaxy cuts across each ring twice --
  once on the way in, once on the way out. Both crossings are on the same ring, so the gas at both
  has the same measured speed: one speed, one ring, two possible distances. Only the smallest ring
  the line reaches -- where it just grazes it, at the "tangent point" -- is crossed once. That's
  also where the measured speed is highest, because it's the ring closest to the center.
- **Bent rulers.** The method assumes the gas moves in perfect circles, but the arms _themselves_
  spoil that. The star-forming region W3OH, in the Perseus Arm, has a kinematic distance of
  4.3 kpc. When its distance was finally measured directly, in 2006, it turned out to be
  1.95 kpc -- less than half. About 22 kilometers per second of extra motion, stirred up by the
  arm, had effectively "bent" the ruler.
- **No answer at all,** in exactly the directions we care about most. Look straight toward the center,
  or straight away from it, and the gas moves entirely across our line of sight, not along it. No
  Doppler shift, no distance. The far side of the Galaxy, behind the center, is the worst case of all.

When the first radio maps of the Milky Way's hydrogen were drawn in 1958, by Dutch and
Australian astronomers working together, they showed long, coherent structures that looked very
much like arms. Turning them into a real top-down map meant choosing between near and far
answers, and assuming circular motion everywhere. The picture was suggestive. It wasn't yet a map.

## The Far Side -- Parallax Comes Back, at Radio Wavelengths

The solution, when it finally came, was the oldest method in the book.

Parallax is still the gold standard: no assumptions about brightness, no assumptions about
motion, just geometry. The baseline hasn't changed since 1838 either -- it's still Earth's orbit,
the same one Bessel used for 61 Cygni. What changed is the *eye*.

Radio telescopes thousands of kilometers apart can be linked so their signals combine as if
they came from a single dish the size of a continent. The larger a telescope's effective aperture, the higher the resolution. 
That technique -- very long baseline interferometry (VLBI) -- pins down the positions of compact radio sources far more precisely 
than any optical telescope on the ground can.

{{< nbas-image src="fig-vlba-map.webp" fullwidth="true"
caption="The Very Long Baseline Array: ten 25-meter radio dishes run by the U.S. National Radio Astronomy Observatory, from Hawaii to the U.S. Virgin Islands. Recorded separately and combined later, their signals act like one telescope as wide as the farthest pair, Mauna Kea to St. Croix, about 9,400 km apart along the Earth's surface. Station positions: NRAO. Map outlines: Natural Earth."
alt="Map of North America and the Pacific with ten blue dots marking radio telescopes: Mauna Kea in Hawaii, Owens Valley, Brewster, Kitt Peak, Pie Town, Los Alamos, Fort Davis, North Liberty, Hancock, and St. Croix in the Caribbean. A dashed gold line joins Mauna Kea and St. Croix, labeled as the longest baseline, about 9,435 km." >}}

It needs the right targets, and nature supplies them. In the dense clouds where massive stars
are forming, water and methanol molecules can amplify radio waves into tiny, extremely radio bright
spots -- natural microwave lasers, called *masers*. They're compact and bright, which is exactly
what precise positions need. And they live in star-forming regions -- the same young places that
mark the spiral arms. Each maser is a pin stuck into an arm, with a distance attached that doesn't
depend on any assumption about how the gas is moving.

### Across the Galaxy

In 2017, a team using the Very Long Baseline Array measured the parallax of a water maser on the
far side of the Milky Way: 0.049 milliarcseconds. That's a distance of about 20 kpc -- some 66,000
light-years -- placing one of the Galaxy's major spiral arms, the Scutum--Centaurus Arm, on the
far side of the center.

By 2019, these radio programs had measured parallaxes for about 200 star-forming regions, most
of them good to about 0.02 milliarcseconds. From those alone, they worked out the Sun's distance
from the center -- 8.15 kpc, in close agreement with the value at the top of this chapter -- and
how fast the Sun is carried around the Galaxy, about 236 kilometers per second.

That's the same strategy we saw in building the ladder. Not every star is in a cluster, so a few
clusters with good distances were used to calibrate methods that work everywhere. Here, a few
hundred masers with good distances fix the Galaxy's rotation -- and that, in turn, makes the
kinematic distances for gas everywhere much more trustworthy.

{{< nbas-callout title="A pin map, honestly" >}}
Two hundred pins is not a billion stars. Masers exist only where massive stars are forming, the
part of the disk best seen from the Southern Hemisphere is still sparsely covered, and only *one*
of those 200 regions lies clearly beyond the Galactic center. We have a map of the far side the
way you'd have a map of a country if all you knew were where its biggest cities are: you can
guess where the roads and the countryside must be, but you can't see them.
{{< /nbas-callout >}}

## Distance to the Center by an Orbit

There's one more way to measure the distance to the center of the Galaxy, and it's the most
direct of all. Watch something orbit it.  The Sun's "Galactic year" is about 230 million years long; we need
to find something with an orbit much shorter than that.

At the very center of the Milky Way sits a black hole about four million times the mass of the
Sun, known as Sagittarius A\* ("A-star"). The dust wall hides it completely in visible light.
We know where it is -- amateurs point their telescopes just off the tip of the spout of
Sagittarius's Teapot and contemplate "the center of the Galaxy" -- even though nothing they see in the eyepiece is
remotely close to that point.

But switch to the near infrared -- the color that gets through -- and large telescopes can watch individual
stars swarming around it. One of them, called S2, is close enough and fast enough that we've
now watched it go all the way around the black hole. Its orbit takes about 16 years. At its closest approach, in
May 2018, it passed about 120 astronomical units from the black hole -- about 17 light-hours.
S2 is moving at roughly 7,650 kilometers per second, a little over 2.5 percent of the speed of light.

S2's orbit would look oddly familiar to a Solar System astronomer. Its closest approach, about
120 AU, is roughly where Voyager 1 crossed the edge of the heliosphere in 2012. The orbit is
extremely stretched (eccentricity 0.88), with a semi-major axis of about 1,000 AU -- not so
different in shape from the orbit of the dwarf planet Sedna (eccentricity 0.85, semi-major axis
about 500 AU). The comparison breaks on the mass at the center. Sedna takes about 11,400 years to
go around the Sun. S2, circling a black hole of some four million Suns, takes 16 years.

Here's why that orbit is a ruler. Imaging gives us the size of the orbit as an *angle* on the
sky. Spectra give us the star's speed in kilometers per second, through the Doppler shift, and
with Kepler's laws that speed and the 16-year period give the size of the orbit in *kilometers*.
An orbit whose size we know both as an angle and as a length is a distance measurement waiting
to happen -- the same idea that let Kepler's planetary orbits become a map of the Solar System
in [Measuring the Astronomical Unit](/articles/2026/11/measuring-the-astronomical-unit/), now
working at 8 kiloparsecs.

The result, from the GRAVITY instrument in 2019: 8.178 kpc, uncertain by only about a third
of a percent -- about 26 parsecs, or some 85 light-years. That's the number at the top of 
this chapter -- and it agrees closely with the 8.15 kpc the maser parallaxes found by an entirely 
different route. Two independent methods, one pure geometry and one pure orbit, land in the same place.

S2 will make its next close pass around 2034. Somewhere in that infrared glare, it's already
falling back in.

## What the Distances Revealed

So far, every measurement in this chapter was built to answer a question: Where are we? How far
is the center? What's on the other side?  But now the story takes an interesting turn. Once the tools could measure
distances across much of the Galaxy, they started uncovering things that nobody had been searching for.

It happened in four steps, and the steps tell their own story -- discovery turning from luck into
method.

### An accident: a galaxy behind the bulge

{{< nbas-image src="m_54-s50-20m-square.webp" width="300" align="right"
caption="Messier 54, Bob Donahue, NBAS, Seestar S50, 20 min"
alt="The globular cluster M 54: a compact, bright round cluster of stars, the core of the Sagittarius dwarf galaxy" >}}
In 1778, Charles Messier added a small, faint globular cluster in Sagittarius to his catalog as
number 54. M 54 looks like any other globular. It isn't.

In 1994, astronomers surveying the motions and chemistry of giant stars in the outer bulge of
the Milky Way found a group of stars that didn't belong. Their motions marked them as something
else entirely: a small galaxy, lying about 26 kpc (some 85,000 light-years) away on the far side of the Galactic center,
being slowly torn apart by our Galaxy's gravity. It had been found by its stars' motions, not by
any picture -- it sits behind the bulge, invisible on photographic plates. M 54 turned out to sit
at its heart. Messier had logged the core of another galaxy
216 years before anyone knew it was there.

Nobody was looking for the Sagittarius Dwarf Galaxy. It was found because someone measured
ordinary stars carefully enough to notice ones that were out of place.

### A surprise: the bulge that came out X-shaped

{{< nbas-image src="fig-wise-x-bulge.webp" width="300" align="right"
caption="The Milky Way's bulge in infrared light from NASA's WISE satellite. Above and below the dark dust lane, the bulge's stars form a faint X. Courtesy NASA/JPL-Caltech."
alt="A circular infrared view of the Galactic center: a bright horizontal band crossed by dark, reddish dust, with a bluish glow of stars above and below it that spreads out diagonally into a faint X shape." >}}
In 2010, two teams looking at red clump stars toward the bulge found something odd. Red clump
stars are nearly standard candles, so along any one line of sight they should pile up at one
brightness -- one distance. Instead, in field after field, they piled up at *two*.

One team wasn't sure what to make of it. The other proposed the geometry: two brightnesses meant
two distances. Along one sightline just below the center, the two groups sat about 6.5 and 8.8
kpc away. The nearer group dominated on one side of the center, the farther group on the other.
Put the pieces together and the bulge isn't a ball at all -- it's shaped like a peanut, or an X,
the thickened middle of the bar.

For six years, the X existed only in the distances. Then it turned up in a picture almost by
accident. Dustin Lang had reprocessed data from NASA's WISE infrared satellite to map galaxies far
beyond our own, and tweeted an image of the whole sky. Melissa Ness saw the Milky Way's bulge in
it and recognized the X. Their 2016 paper showed the shape directly -- the image at the top of this
section. The distances had found it first.

### Hunting: distance as a filter

By then, astronomers were *searching* with distance on purpose.

Here's the problem with finding faint structures in the halo. Look at any patch of sky well
away from the Milky Way's band, and nearby disk stars can outnumber distant halo stars a
thousand to one. Anything faint and far away is lost in the crowd.

The solution was to run main-sequence fitting backwards. Instead of asking "how far away is this
cluster?", take the known pattern of a cluster's stars -- their colors and brightnesses -- slide
it to a chosen distance, and keep only the stars that match. Everything at the wrong distance
falls away. In 2006, sifting a huge sky survey this way, Carl Grillmair and colleagues found long, thin ribbons
of stars crossing tens of degrees of sky: *stellar streams*, the remains of star clusters and
small galaxies being pulled apart and strung out along their orbits. One of them, GD-1, runs 63°
across the northern sky, and its stars turned out to be orbiting the Galaxy backwards.

A stream like that is invisible from inside it. Its stars are scattered among ordinary
foreground stars; only when you sort the sky by distance does the ribbon appear.

### A census: measure everything first

The last step is the one we're experiencing now. Gaia's third data release, in 2022, gave positions
for about 1.8 billion stars and parallaxes for nearly 1.5 billion of them. The earlier parallax
work in this series aimed at particular stars to answer particular questions. Gaia measures
everything and lets the questions come afterward. Its fourth data release, in December 2026,
is based on five and a half years of observations, and pushes all of this further.

The most famous answer so far came from Gaia's second data release. In 2018, a team comparing
the orbits and chemistry of halo stars found that a great many of them shared a common origin:
the remains of a galaxy a little larger than the Small Magellanic Cloud, swallowed by the Milky
Way about 10 billion years ago. It's now called Gaia-Enceladus. Nobody had asked whether it
existed. The census found it.

What the census has tied to it so far:

- **A big share of the halo around us.** Gaia-Enceladus's stars dominate the inner halo near the
  Sun, on long, stretched-out orbits that, on average, go slightly backwards around the Galaxy.
- **The "Gaia Sausage."** Earlier in 2018, another team had spotted the same stars as a sausage-shaped
  pile-up in a plot of stellar velocities: far too many stars plunging in and out toward the center.
  Two teams, one event, two names.
- **Globular clusters.** The discovery paper linked 13 globulars to it; a 2019 study counted 26,
  plus 6 possibles. Among them is M 79, in Lepus -- a winter-sky globular that came from another galaxy.
- **Maybe its heart.** That 2019 study suggested Omega Centauri, the Milky Way's biggest globular,
  is what's left of Gaia-Enceladus's core -- the same story as M 54 and the Sagittarius dwarf.
  Other teams link it to a different merger, so this one is still open.
- **A thicker disk.** The collision stirred up the young Milky Way's disk, helping to puff up what
  we now see as its thick disk.

Its name is a myth with a joke inside it: Enceladus was a giant, a child of Gaia, buried under Mount
Etna, where his struggles were said to shake the Earth. Not everyone agrees the story is that
tidy -- some studies argue the halo near the Sun holds debris from several mergers, not one
dominant one -- but the giant has stuck.

## What Do We Still Not Know?

Quite a lot. There are some fundamental or basic questions whose answers we haven't pinned down:

- **How many arms?** Infrared counts of older stars suggest two major arms; young stars and gas
  trace four. The far-side masers showed that at least one arm won't fit the simple spiral shape
  astronomers like to draw. Ironically, we can trace the arms of some galaxies millions of
  light-years away more clearly than we can see our own.
- **The far side** is still a sketch, built from a handful of pins, and the part of the disk best
  seen from the Southern Hemisphere is sparsely measured.  "Here be dragons."
- **The outer rotation** is disputed: some recent studies find the disk slowing down well beyond
  the Sun's orbit, and how much is still being argued.
- **Even the distance to the center** isn't quite closed. The Keck team's own fit to S2's orbit
  gives 7.96 kpc, about 3 percent short of GRAVITY's 8.18 -- a gap larger than either team's
  stated uncertainty.

This brings us back to where this chapter began. Herschel, Kapteyn, and Shapley each drew a map
that showed the edge of what they could see.  Honestly, ours does too. The difference is that we know where
our edges are -- and we know which instruments are helping to push them back.

But even where we stand now is good enough for something new: not just a map of the Galaxy, but a sense of
what it's like out there. In the next chapter, [A Tour of the Galaxy](/articles/2027/01/a-tour-of-the-milky-way-galaxy/), 
we take that map and become Galactic tourists.
