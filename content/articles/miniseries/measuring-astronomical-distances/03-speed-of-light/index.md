## Introduction

{{< nbas-image src="earth-moon-c.gif" fullwidth="true"
caption="A beam of light crossing from Earth to the Moon in real time: about 1.3 seconds. Animation by James O'Donoghue, CC BY 3.0, via Wikimedia Commons." >}}

A ruler is only as good as its calibration.   So far, we've looked at 
[distances in the solar system](/articles/2026/10/distance-at-solar-system-scale/) 
using light time as a unit of distance:  the Sun and the Moon are 8.3 light-minutes, 
and 1.28 light-seconds from Earth on average, respectively; Voyager 1 is nearly one light *day* 
from us.  But of course that conversion requires knowing the fundamental quantity:  the speed of light.

{{< clear >}}
---

## The Number

The speed of light, _c_ (as in the famous equation _E = m c <sup>2</sup>_) is
exactly 299,792,458 meters per second -- hardcoded since 1983.  We typically
round that out to 300,000 km/s or 186,000 miles per second: you can use any
velocity units of distance over time -- 1.8 trillion furlongs per fortnight, 
~1 foot per nanosecond, or going back to our relational measurements, 
just under 7.5 trips around the Earth per second.

But *exact* is a strong word -- aren't measured scientific quantities always being
continually refined?  Yes, but the twist here is that we've also defined the 
meter "exactly" as well -- this becomes clear further down.

---

## Too Fast to Catch

Originally, it wasn't even clear that there _was_ a speed of light. 
The first question was whether light travels at all, or simply
happens. The ancient Greeks argued about it, 
and tangled up in that argument was another question: does seeing 
work by light going out from our eyes to an object, or by something 
coming in from that object to us?

{{< nbas-image src="Empedocles_in_Thomas_Stanley_History_of_Philosophy.webp" width="250" align="right"
caption="Empedocles, from Thomas Stanley's The History of Philosophy (1655)." 
alt="Seventeenth-century engraved portrait of the Greek philosopher Empedocles" >}}

Empedocles (c. 494 – c. 434 BCE) had it both ways: our eyes shine
out like lanterns to meet what we look at, and that light takes time to
get from one place to another.  Aristotle would have none of it.
Light, he argued, doesn't move at all; it's a state of the transparent
air, present everywhere at once.  Over a short distance, he allowed,
light's travel might go unnoticed, but from one end of the sky to
the other?  That strained belief.[^aristotle]

Heron of Alexandria offered a proof.  Open your eyes at night, and
the stars are there at once.  If sight travels out from the eye to
the stars, it must get there instantly, so its speed must be infinite.
But turn it around: if light comes *in*, the starlight was already
on its way before you opened your eyes -- the stars would
appear at once no matter how fast light is. Heron's proof only
actually proves something if sight goes *out*.

It took about a thousand years for someone to turn it around
for good.  In his *Book of Optics*, written between 1011 and 1021 CE, Ibn al-Haytham 
(known in Europe as Alhazen, c. 965–c. 1040 CE) argued that seeing is light coming *into*
the eye, not going out of it.  One of his arguments: looking straight at a very bright 
light, such as the Sun, can damage your eyes.  Another turned Heron's stars against him: 
how could the eye possibly fill the whole sky the instant you open your eyelids?
Once light is something that travels from the object to you, "how fast?" becomes a real question.  
He also held that light has a finite speed, and that it slows
down in denser materials. (Hold that thought: someone measures exactly that in 1850.) 

Yet even by the 1600s, most of the big names still had light as instantaneous.
Johannes Kepler thought empty space offered no obstacle, so why would light take any time at all?

The obvious thing was to test it.  In 1629 Isaac Beeckman proposed firing a mortar
and watching for its flash in a mirror about a mile away, timing the return.

{{< nbas-image src="fig-lunar-eclipse-delay.webp" fullwidth="true"
caption="Descartes' test. If light took an hour to make the trip, the eclipsed Moon would appear a full Moon-width away from the point opposite the Sun. Nobody saw that. At the real speed of light the offset is about one arcsecond, far too small to notice."
alt="A dark reddish disc, the eclipsed Moon, centered on a cross marking the point exactly opposite the Sun. A dashed circle of the same size sits one Moon-width to the right along the Moon's path, labeled as where the Moon would appear if light took an hour." >}}

René Descartes, once Beeckman's protégé, told him not to bother: the sky had already run a far bigger experiment.
During a lunar eclipse, the Sun, Earth, and Moon are lined up.  If light were slow enough for
Beeckman to catch over a mile, then over the distance to the Moon the delay would be about an hour --
and in an hour the Moon moves about its own width across the sky.  We would see the Moon go dark
when it was visibly *out of line* with the Sun.  Nobody had ever seen that, so light must be instant.
Descartes staked a great deal on it: if any such delay were ever perceived, he wrote to Beeckman, 
his whole philosophy would be "completely overturned."[^descartes]

{{< nbas-image src="fig-galileo-lanterns.webp" fullwidth="true"
caption="Galileo's lantern test, and the size it would have needed to be. Over a mile, light's round trip is thousands of times quicker than a human can react; to stretch it to a single reaction time, the two hills would have to be 30,000 km apart."
alt="Two panels. Top: two figures labeled A and B stand on hilltops less than a mile apart holding lanterns; arrows show A uncovering his lantern and B answering. Bottom, to scale: a line 30,000 kilometers long drawn beside a circle representing Earth, 12,742 kilometers across, showing the line is more than twice Earth's width." >}}

Galileo, in *Two New Sciences* (1638), described a simpler version of Beeckman's test: two people with
shuttered lanterns some distance apart.  The first uncovers his lantern; the second uncovers his the
moment he sees the first; the first times how long it takes for the answering light to appear.
Galileo said he had tried it only "at a short distance, less than a mile," and couldn't tell
whether there was any delay.  Light, "if not instantaneous," was "extraordinarily rapid."[^galileo]  
In 1667 the Accademia del Cimento in Florence tried Galileo's experiment anyway, with the lanterns 
about a mile apart.  Still nothing.

{{< nbas-callout title="Try the Numbers: Galileo's Hills" kind="exercise" >}}

Light makes a round trip of a mile out and back in about 11 millionths of a second. A good human reaction takes about a fifth of a second -- nearly 20,000 times longer. Galileo wasn't timing light at all; he was timing his assistant.

So how far apart would the hills need to be for light's round trip to take even *one* reaction time? About 30,000 km each way -- more than twice the diameter of the Earth. Galileo's idea wasn't wrong. The planet just wasn't big enough.

{{< /nbas-callout >}}

Which means Descartes was right about one thing: the place to look was the sky.  His reasoning was
fine.  His distance still wasn't big enough.  At the real speed of light, the round trip to the Moon
takes about 2.6 seconds, and in that time the Moon moves about one arcsecond -- far too little for
anyone in the 1600s to notice.  "No delay observed" only ruled out a *slow* speed of light.
The delay was there, just too small to catch.

A mile was too short.  The Moon was too close.  Aristotle had it exactly backwards: a short distance
is where light's travel hides, and the huge distances of the sky are where it finally shows.
To catch light taking time, you need a distance measured in millions of kilometers -- and the only
place to find one is farther up.

## What You Can Actually Measure

Fundamentally, measuring a speed requires measuring a *time* over a *distance*,
and the answer comes out in whatever units are used for the distance.  This seems
obvious, but on astronomical scales (as we've seen) it's not possible to make
that distance measurement explicitly: you have to infer it from other observations.

If your distance is "across Earth's orbit," you get the speed of light in minutes
per AU. That's a perfectly good number, but in kilometers it's only as good as
your knowledge of the AU. If your distance is a surveyed line between two hills,
you get kilometers per second directly.

So instead of asking "how close did each experiment get?", we'll ask each one two
questions: what did it actually measure, and what else did it need to know? Watch
the "What else they needed" column empty out.

## c in the Solar System's Own Units

The first two real measurements of the speed of light both came from astronomy, 
and both came out in the solar system's own units: minutes per AU.  They settled the 
big question -- light takes time -- but turning them into kilometers per second meant 
knowing the size of Earth's orbit in kilometers, and nobody knew that well yet.

### Rømer: A Clock Orbiting Jupiter

{{< nbas-image src="Speed-Light-Romer-Jupiter.webp" width="200" align="right" 
caption="The geometry of Rømer's experiment.  From Rømer (1676), public domain."
alt="Diagram showing the position of the Sun, Earth in its orbit, and Jupiter with the orbit of Io, and the shadow cast of Jupiter by the Sun." >}}
In the 1670s, astronomers at the Paris Observatory were timing the eclipses of Io,
Jupiter's innermost large moon, for a very practical reason: finding longitude.
Io slips into Jupiter's shadow every 42½ hours, so regularly that it makes a 
clock that anyone with a telescope can read.

Except the clock didn't seem to keep time. 
Eclipses ran early for months, then late for
months -- and the error was spoiling the longitude tables.  
Ole Rømer's explanation: the clock is fine; the ticks do *happen* evenly at
Jupiter, but the distance between Jupiter and Earth keeps changing.   When Earth is
pulling away, each tick's light has a little farther to travel than the last, and
the lateness piles up.  The whole method fits in one line: the shift in eclipse
times equals the change in the Earth's distance to Jupiter, divided by the speed of light.

{{< clear >}}
{{< nbas-callout title="How Io Tells Your Longitude" >}}

Because we know Io's orbital period, we can predict the timing of its disappearances into
and reappearances out of Jupiter's shadow -- and everyone on Earth who can see Jupiter sees each
event at essentially the same moment.  What differs from place to place is the local time.
So observe an Io event, and compare the sidereal time of that event at Paris (the reference
longitude then; Greenwich today) with your own local sidereal time -- determined from the 
right ascension of any star crossing your meridian, or the position of the Sun.  
The difference is your longitude, at 15° per hour.

It worked well on land -- well enough to redraw the map of France.
King Louis XIV employed surveyors to use the technique at locations around France
to fix their positions accurately.   
When the new map was presented to the King -- France 
turned out to be smaller than previously thought, by about 20% -- causing the King to exclaim 
that he was losing more territory to cartographers than to his enemies!
At sea, however, the technique didn't work as well, for the simple reason that 
from the deck of a rolling ship you can't hold a telescope steadily enough to make the observations of Jupiter.

{{< /nbas-callout >}}


In 1676 the prediction went on the record: an eclipse in November would come about
ten minutes late. On November 9, 1676, it did.[^romer] (Check it yourself: by then Jupiter
was about 1.12 AU farther away than in late August, and at 8.3 light-minutes per AU
that's about 9.3 minutes.)   There's an irony here: the first written account 
of the idea is by Giovanni Cassini, the observatory's director -- who then spent 
the rest of his career rejecting it.[^cassini] 

{{< nbas-image src="fig-romer-ticks.webp" fullwidth="true"
caption="Io's eclipses happen on a steady schedule, but as Earth pulls away from Jupiter each one reaches us a little later than the last. (Schematic: the delays are greatly exaggerated.)"
alt="Two rows of tick marks. The top row, eclipses happening at Jupiter, is evenly spaced. In the bottom row, eclipses as seen from Earth, each mark falls further behind its expected position, with the final gap labeled late by extra distance divided by c." >}}

Rømer's stated result was that light takes about 11 minutes to cross
the distance from the Sun to Earth, or 22 to cross the whole orbit -- a speed in minutes per AU.
(The modern value is 8.3 minutes per AU.) Turning that into everyday units meant
knowing how big Earth's orbit is, and that step fell to someone
else. The Dutch scientist Christiaan Huygens, who was building a
theory of light as a wave, worked it through in his *Treatise on
Light* (1690): taking Earth's orbit to be about 22,000 Earth diameters
across, Rømer's 22 minutes meant light covers 16⅔ Earth diameters
every second -- about 212,000 km/s. Good measurement, uncertain
ruler.

### Bradley: The Star That Moved the Wrong Way

Even fifty years later, the question was still open -- Rømer's result had its doubters.
James Bradley settled it by accident. In 1725, he and Samuel Molyneux were hunting for something
else entirely: stellar parallax, the tiny back-and-forth shift of a nearby star as
Earth goes around the Sun.  Finding it would prove that Earth moves.

{{< nbas-image src="fig-aberration-vs-parallax.webp" fullwidth="true"
caption="The north-south shift of Gamma Draconis through the year. Parallax would put the star farthest south in December; Bradley found it farthest south in March, three months late. (The parallax curve shows timing only: the real effect is a thousand times smaller.)"
alt="Line chart over twelve months. A dashed curve labeled expected from parallax reaches its lowest point in December. A solid curve labeled what Bradley saw reaches its lowest point in March, about 20 arcseconds south, and its highest in September. An arrow between the two low points is labeled three months apart." >}}

Their star, Gamma (γ) Draconis, did shift, by about 20 arcseconds either way -- but 
at the wrong times of year. It peaked exactly when parallax should have been zero, 
a quarter of a year out of step. It wasn't parallax.  It was stellar aberration.
(The parallax of this star is only 0.021&Prime; -- it lies about 154 light-years away -- 
a thousand times smaller than the shift Bradley found, and far too small for him to detect.  Stellar 
parallax would not be measured for another century, and then on much closer stars.)

This is the same tilt you get moving through falling rain -- only with light.
Walking through a vertical downpour, you have to tilt your umbrella forward to
deflect the rain; the faster you walk, the more you need to tilt.  Earth moves through starlight 
at about 30 km/s, and starlight arrives at about 300,000 km/s, so the "umbrella" tilts by a ratio 
of about 1 in 10,000 -- which comes out to about 20 arcseconds.  Bradley's result, announced in 1729, 
did two things at once: it showed that light's speed is finite, and it was conclusive evidence that 
Earth really does move around the Sun. 

{{< nbas-image src="fig-aberration-umbrella.webp" fullwidth="true"
caption="Walk through rain that falls straight down and you tilt your umbrella forward. A telescope on the moving Earth has to do the same: it leans forward so that light entering the top is still inside the tube when it reaches the bottom."
alt="Two panels. On the left, a figure walks to the right through vertical rain, holding an umbrella tilted forward. On the right, a telescope tube tilted forward is shown at two moments as Earth moves to the right, with a vertical ray of starlight entering the top of its earlier position and reaching the bottom of its later position." >}}

But notice what he measured: a *ratio*, Earth's speed compared with light's. Earth's
speed in km/s comes from the size of its orbit, so -- once again -- the answer in
kilometers depended on the AU.  What Bradley could say was that light takes 8 minutes 
12 seconds to reach us from the Sun;[^bradley] the modern value is 8 minutes 19 seconds.

## Cutting the AU Out

To get the speed of light without the AU in it, you need a distance you can
survey -- which means bringing the measurement back down to the surface of the Earth again.
The problem of course is Galileo's situation all over again: a short distance needs a very fast
clock. The answer turned out to be a machine.

### Fizeau: A Toothed Wheel

{{< nbas-image src="fig-fizeau-wheel.webp" fullwidth="true"
caption="Fizeau's apparatus. Light from a lamp is turned by an angled sheet of glass, passes through a gap in the rim of the toothed wheel, and crosses to Montmartre, where a mirror sends it straight back. If a gap is still in place when it returns, the observer sees a point of light; if a tooth has moved in, the light is blocked."
alt="Schematic of Fizeau's experiment. On the left, an observer looks along the axis through an angled glass plate that also receives light from a lamp below. The rim of a toothed wheel crosses the axis at the focus of a telescope. The beam travels 8,633 meters to a second telescope with a mirror at its focus and returns. Two insets show the wheel's rim face-on: with a gap in place the light passes, with a tooth in place it is blocked." >}}

{{< clear >}}

{{< nbas-image src="fizeau-pirou.webp" width="254" align="right"
caption="Fizeau by Eugène Pirou (1841-1909)" 
alt="Photographic portrait of the French physicist Hippolyte Fizeau" >}}

In 1849, Hippolyte Fizeau (1819--1896) set up a telescope in the rooftop belvedere of a
house in Suresnes, west of Paris, and put a mirror on the heights of Montmartre, about 8.6 km
away. He sent a beam of light through a gap in a spinning wheel with 720 teeth, out to
the mirror, and back. If the wheel spins fast enough, then in the time the light takes
to make the round trip, the gap moves on and the next *tooth* arrives to block the
returning beam -- and the light goes dark. In his setup, the first eclipse came at
about 12.6 turns per second.

Then came the check that it really was the light's travel time: spin the wheel twice as
fast, and the light comes back through the *next* gap and shines again; three times as
fast, and it's blocked again; four times, and it shines. If light were instant, no
speed would ever darken it.

Knowing the number of teeth and the rotation speed tells you exactly how long the round
trip took. It's Galileo's lanterns with the human replaced by a gear: a stopwatch that
ticks in millionths of a second. From twenty-eight observations, Fizeau got about
315,000 km/s -- 5% high, but finally a value that didn't depend on the AU at all.[^fizeau] 

{{< nbas-callout title="Try the Numbers: Fizeau's Wheel" kind="exercise" >}}

A wheel with 720 teeth has 1,440 alternating gaps and teeth.  At 12.6 turns per second,
a gap is replaced by the next tooth in 1/(1,440 × 12.6) of a second -- about 55 millionths 
of a second.  In that time the light went to Montmartre and back: 2 × 8,633 meters.

Divide the distance by the time and you get about 313,000 km/s.  (Fizeau's published
315,000 was from taking the average of all of his twenty-eight runs.)

{{< /nbas-callout >}}

### Foucault: A Spinning Mirror, and Light in Water

{{< nbas-image src="fig-foucault-mirror.webp" fullwidth="true"
caption="Foucault's spinning mirror. While the light travels out to the fixed mirror and back, the spinning mirror turns slightly, so the returning beam lands beside the source instead of on it. The size of that shift gives the travel time."
alt="Diagram of a light source, a small spinning mirror, and a curved fixed mirror about 20 meters away. A blue ray goes from the source to the spinning mirror and on to the fixed mirror; a gold ray returns, reflects off the spinning mirror at a slightly different angle, and lands at a point next to the source." >}}

Léon Foucault replaced the wheel with a rapidly spinning mirror. In the time the
light took to go out to a fixed mirror and back, the spinning one had turned a
tiny bit, so the returning beam came back slightly displaced -- and the
displacement gave the time. The whole path was about 20 meters; it fit inside a 
laboratory. By 1862 he had 298,000 km/s, within about 0.6% of the modern value.

The same method settled an older argument. If light were a stream of particles,
it should travel *faster* in water than in air; if it were a wave, *slower*. In
1850 Foucault found slower -- just as Ibn al-Haytham had argued, eight centuries
earlier. 

And now the dependency could run backward.  Foucault made his 1862 measurement because
the astronomer Urbain Le Verrier suspected the accepted speed of light was a few percent 
too high -- and wanted a better value in order to pin down the astronomical unit.  With 
*c* known in kilometers per second, Bradley's ratio gives Earth's orbital speed, and so 
the size of Earth's orbit.

## A Different Road: Electricity

Meanwhile, the same number had been hiding somewhere nobody expected. In 1846,
Wilhelm Weber wrote down a law for the forces between electric charges, and buried
in the equations was a constant that had the dimensions of a *speed*. Nobody knew 
its value, or what it meant.

In the mid-1850s, Weber and Rudolf Kohlrausch measured it, and the experiment had 
nothing to do with light. They charged a Leyden jar -- an early capacitor -- and measured the
same charge two different ways. First by its *electric* push: share some of the
charge onto a metal ball and measure the force on a delicate torsion balance. Then
by its *magnetic* effect: let the jar discharge through a coil of thousands of turns
and watch how far the pulse of current kicks a magnetized needle. The two methods
give two different numbers for the same charge, and their ratio comes out as a
speed: about 310,000 km/s.[^weber]

In today's notation, that ratio is

<p style="text-align:center"><em>c</em> = 1 / &radic;(<em>&mu;</em><sub>0</sub> <em>&epsilon;</em><sub>0</sub>)</p>

where *ε*<sub>0</sub> sets how strongly electric charges push on each other, and
*μ*<sub>0</sub> sets how strongly electric currents push on each other through
magnetism. Weber and Kohlrausch weren't measuring those two constants -- in that form
they didn't exist yet -- and they weren't assuming them. They were measuring the
*ratio* directly, by weighing one charge both ways.  

James Clerk Maxwell recognized that the number matched the speed of light, and in 1865 drew the
conclusion: light *is* an electromagnetic wave. The speed of light isn't just how
fast one particular thing happens to travel; it's built into electricity and
magnetism, a property of empty space itself. And it had been sitting in Weber's
equations in 1846 -- three years before Fizeau first measured it on the ground --
without anyone knowing what it was.

(And this is where the letter *c* comes from: Weber's constant, "c" apparently for "constant." 
Maxwell called the speed of light *V*, and it took until the 1890s for *c* to stick. 
Einstein himself only switched in 1907.)[^c]

## Beating Down the Error

{{< nbas-image src="michelson-1921.webp" width="250" align="right"
caption="Albert A. Michelson in 1921. Autochrome by Auguste Léon, via Wikimedia Commons"
alt="Color portrait of Michelson with gray hair and a mustache, seated with his hands clasped, wearing a dark suit and bow tie in front of a green curtain" >}}

Now our "What else they needed" column above is empty.  From here on it becomes
"here's my value of *c* -- now make the error bar smaller."

Albert Michelson spent about fifty years refining Foucault's spinning mirror.
In the 1920s he timed light over a 35 km path between two California
mountains (Mt. Wilson, at the Observatory, and Mt. Baldy), a baseline that took surveyors 
two years to measure to the needed accuracy, and got 299,796 ± 4 km/s.  His last experiment 
ran light through a mile-long pipe with the air pumped out, because by then even air was 
slowing the light enough to matter. (He died in 1931 before it finished; the result was published in 1935.)

{{< nbas-image src="fig-c-uncertainty.webp" fullwidth="true"
caption="A century of beating down the error. Each step down the scale is ten times more precise; from Foucault to the first laser measurements, the uncertainty in the speed of light shrank by a factor of nearly half a million."
alt="Chart of the stated uncertainty in the speed of light against year, on a scale where each line is ten times smaller than the last. Points fall from Foucault in 1862 at plus or minus 500 kilometers per second, through Michelson in 1879 and 1926, Essen in 1950 and Froome in 1958, to Evenson in 1972 at about plus or minus 1 meter per second. An arrow at 1983 is labeled fixed by definition, no uncertainty left." >}}

After the Second World War came microwave cavities, radio, and then lasers: measure
a laser's frequency and its wavelength, multiply, and you have its speed.

### Measured Better Than the Meter

{{< nbas-image src="Krypton-86-lamp_NIST_49.webp" width="300" align="right"
caption="A krypton-86 lamp. From 1960 to 1983, the meter was defined by the wavelength of its orange light. NIST Museum, public domain, via Wikimedia Commons"
alt="Photo of a krypton-86 lamp, the light source that defined the meter from 1960 to 1983" >}}

By 1972 a laser measurement gave 299,792,456.2 ± 1.1 m/s -- and most of that
remaining uncertainty wasn't in the light. It was in the meter itself: the official
definition, based on a particular wavelength of krypton light (605.78021 nm), couldn't be pinned
down any better.  The speed of light was now known more precisely than the ruler 
used to measure it.

So in 1983 the ruler was redefined by the General Conference on Weights and Measures
(the CGPM, from its French name, _Conférence générale des poids et mesures_).
The speed of light was fixed at exactly 299,792,458 meters per second, and the meter became 
"the length of the path travelled by light in vacuum during a time interval of 1/299 792 458 of a second."[^metre]  
That's why *c* is "exact": not because nature is tidy, but because we stopped measuring the speed of
light and started measuring distances with it.

## What Else Changes the Travel Time

Using light as a ruler means assuming travel time is just distance divided by *c*.
Almost -- but not quite, and the corrections matter for anyone timing light across
the solar system.

**Stuff slows it down.** *c* is the speed in a vacuum. In air, light is slower by
about 0.03%; in water, about 25%; in glass, about a third.  That's why Michelson pumped 
the air out of his pipe -- and it's also why the internet, running through glass fiber, 
moves at only about two-thirds of *c*.

{{< nbas-image src="fig-light-in-media.webp" fullwidth="true"
caption="How fast light travels through different materials, as a share of its speed in a vacuum."
alt="Bar chart. Vacuum: 100 percent, 299,792 kilometers per second. Air: 99.97 percent. Water: 75 percent, about 225,400 kilometers per second. Glass and optical fiber: 67 percent, about 199,900 kilometers per second." >}}

**Your motion doesn't change it.** Everyone who measures the speed of light in a vacuum 
gets the same answer, however fast they or the source are moving.  That's what makes it 
usable as a universal ruler. 

**Gravity adds delay.** Light passing close to the Sun takes measurably longer to
make the trip. In the 1960s, radar signals bounced off Mercury and Venus when they were 
on the far side of the Sun came back about 200 millionths of a second late -- just as 
had been predicted from general relativity.  Measured right where the light is, 
its speed never changes; but near the Sun, space is stretched and clocks run slow, so 
seen from far away the trip takes longer than the distance alone would suggest.  Anyone 
using radar to measure the solar system has to correct for it.

caption="Radar to Venus on the far side of the Sun. The echo takes about 29 minutes to return, and the Sun's gravity adds about 200 millionths of a second to the trip."

## Wrapup

In the last article we used light as a ruler on credit: light-minutes to the Sun,
a light-day to Voyager. This was the calibration certificate. The first
measurements came out in the solar system's own units -- minutes per AU. Bringing
the distance down to Earth gave kilometers per second with no AU in it.
Electricity gave the same number from a completely different direction. And the
measurements got so good that the speed of light became the definition of the
meter.

Now run it backward. With *c* in hand, bounce a radar signal off a planet, time the
echo, and you have the distance -- and from that, the size of the AU itself. That's
the next article.
