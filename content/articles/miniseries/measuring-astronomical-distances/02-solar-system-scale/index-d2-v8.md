## Introduction

{{< nbas-image src="banner.png" fullwidth="true"
caption="The scale of the solar system."
alt="Diagram showing the scale of the solar system in log(AU) and in light time." >}}

In the [Measuring Distance](/articles/2026/10/measuring-distance/) article, we saw how quickly 
our everyday rulers -- steps, miles, hours of driving -- run out once we leave Earth behind. 
Now, we pick up where they gave out.

Here's the good news: we already have one piece of the Solar System's scale. You are
standing one astronomical unit from the Sun right now, and so is everyone you know. From this
single distance value we can assess the spatial relationship of everything else in the Solar System.
Before we do, though, it's worth noticing the one fact that makes the Solar System so
hard to picture: while the things in the Solar System are small, the spaces between them are enormous.

## Small Worlds, Big Gaps

The sizes of the planets, moons, and other bodies in the Solar System don't vary all that much:

**Solar System Bodies: Diameters Compared with Earth**

| Body | Diameter (km) | Size (🌍 = 1) | Visual Scaling / Notes |
| :--- | ----: | ----: | :--- |
| **Jupiter** | 142,984 | 11.209 | Over 11 times wider than Earth |
| **Saturn** | 120,536 | 9.450 | Nearly 9&frac12; times wider than Earth |
| **Uranus** | 51,118 | 4.007 | Slightly over 4 times wider than Earth |
| **Neptune** | 49,528 | 3.883 | Slightly under 4 times wider than Earth |
| **Earth** | 12,756 | 1.000 | Baseline |
| **Venus** | 12,104 | 0.949 | Earth's near-twin in size |
| **Mars** | 6,792 | 0.532 | Roughly half the width of Earth |
| **Mercury** | 4,879 | 0.383 | Roughly &frac13; the width of Earth |
| **Moon**  | 3,475 | 0.272 | Just over &frac14; Earth |
| **Pluto** | 2,376 | 0.186 | Less than 1/5th the width of Earth |
| **Eris** | 2,326 | 0.182 | Marginally smaller than Pluto, but more massive |
| **Haumea** | ~1,600 | ~0.125 | Highly elongated (~2,320 × 1,704 × 1,138 km) |
| **Makemake** | 1,430 | 0.112 | Roughly 1/9th the width of Earth |
| **Sedna** | ~1,000 | ~0.078 | Extreme, distant trans-Neptunian object |
| **Ceres** | 939 | 0.074 | ~1/14th of Earth; largest object in the asteroid belt |

Apart from the four giants, Earth outranks everything on this list. Nothing here is
beyond imagining: Mars is half the width of Earth, Jupiter a bit more than eleven Earths
across. Sizes, it turns out, are easy. Distances are another matter entirely -- and the
quickest way to see that is to use the biggest object in the Solar System as a ruler.

### How Many Suns Fit Between Earth and the Sun?

In the [previous article](/articles/2026/10/measuring-distance/), we lined up all the planets 
between Earth and the Moon, to demonstrate that they just barely fit as a way of making sense of the
Earth-Moon distance.  Let's try the same trick with the Sun itself: how many Suns, lined up edge to edge,
would fill the gap between the Sun's surface and Earth's?

**Suns end to end between the Sun's surface and Earth's surface**

| Parameter | Value | Space to fill | # Suns |
| :--- | --: | --: | --: |
| Radius -- Sun | 695,700 km | --- | --- |
| Radius -- Earth | 6,371 km | --- | --- |
| Astronomical Unit | 149,597,870.7 km | 148.9&times;10<sup>6</sup> km | 107.0 |
| Earth at perihelion (early January) | ~147.1&times;10<sup>6</sup> km | 146.4&times;10<sup>6</sup> km | 105.2 |
| Earth at aphelion (early July) | ~152.1&times;10<sup>6</sup> km | 151.4&times;10<sup>6</sup> km | 108.8 |

Distances like the AU are measured center to center, so to find the open space we
subtract both radii -- the Sun's and Earth's.

At the average distance it comes out almost exactly even: after 107 Suns, only about
16,000 km of space is left over -- room for about one more Earth and a bit. Just like the
planets between Earth and the Moon, it *just* fits. But "107" is only true on average.
In early January, when Earth is closest to the Sun, only 105 fit; in early July, nearly
109 do. (And another near-coincidence: the Sun is about 109 times wider than Earth.)

{{< nbas-image src="fig-suns-between-sun-earth.svg" fullwidth="true"
caption="107 Suns end to end."
alt="Diagram showing 107 circles of equal size end to end to demonstrate the Earth-Sun separation.  The lower panel shows the edge of the last Sun with a gap compared to the Earth for the remaining space." >}}

Think about what that means. The Sun is by far the largest thing in the Solar System, and
it takes more than a hundred of them to span the distance from us to our own star.

And of course if you try this with any of the outer planets, even the number of Suns begins to 
be a little incomprehensible - just over 2,000 to Uranus.

### The Asteroid Belt Is Emptier Than the Movies

If there's one place in the Solar System that movies have taught us is crowded, it's the
asteroid belt: ships weaving between tumbling boulders, rocks close enough to bounce off
each other.  

{{< nbas-image src="asteroid-belt-nasa-dark.png" width="450" align="right"
caption="Even NASA's artist's illustrations crowd the belt for effect. The real thing, seen from inside, would look like empty space with a few faint stars. Image: NASA."
alt="An artist's illustration of the asteroid belt, showing many rocky bodies close together." >}}

The real belt is nothing like that.
It's estimated to hold somewhere around 1.1–1.9 million objects larger than a kilometer across 
in the Main Belt -- which sounds like a lot until you see how much room they have. 
The belt is a vast ring around the Sun, roughly 1 AU across, so the average spacing of
those ≥1 km asteroids is on the order of 600,000 to 1 million km apart -- farther than the 
Earth-Moon separation.   Stand on one, and your nearest neighbor would usually be too far away 
to see at all, or at best would look like just another faint star.  

As an example: seen from a typical spot elsewhere in the belt, Vesta -- which has a very
reflective surface (albedo about 0.36, roughly three times our Moon's) -- would shine like a dim star of magnitude 5.0–6.5 most of the time; on a close approach of ~0.2 AU it might
flare up to magnitude 0.5–1.0.  Ceres -- the largest body in the belt, now classed as a dwarf planet -- has a dark surface (albedo about 0.09),
even darker than our Moon's.  It would typically be magnitude 6.5–7.0: about as bright
as Ceres looks from Earth at its best!   And neither would look like a rock in space: just a few arcseconds
across.  Spacecraft sent to the outer Solar System cross the belt without steering around
anything: NASA estimates the odds of hitting an asteroid while crossing the Main Belt at about
a billion to one.  (Sorry, C-3PO, your claim "the possibility of successfully navigating an asteroid 
field is approximately 3,720 to 1!" is just completely not true.)


Of all the "neighborhoods" in the Solar System, the one with the reputation for being
crowded is, by everyday standards, almost perfectly empty.

## The Astronomical Unit

{{< nbas-image src="au.svg" width="400" align="left"
caption="Maxmath12, CC0, via Wikimedia Commons"
alt="Simple diagram of the inner solar system showing the defintion of 1 AU." >}}


The first ruler is the one you're sitting on. The **astronomical unit** (AU) is the
average distance between Earth and the Sun: about 150 million kilometers, or 93 million
miles. Since 2012 it has been defined as exactly 149,597,870,700 meters.

That raises an obvious question: how do we know? It's a remarkable story -- centuries of
it -- and it gets its own article later in this series. For now, treat the AU the way you
treat a kilometer: you use kilometers every day without knowing how the meter was defined.
The AU is a unit first. Its history can wait.

Here's one way to "feel" it. Earth's orbit is roughly a circle with a radius of 1 AU, which
makes it about 940 million kilometers around. That's how far you've traveled around the
Sun since your last birthday -- 2&pi; AU -- at an average of nearly 30 kilometers every second 
(about 66,600 mph).

Measured in AU, the whole Solar System suddenly becomes readable:

**Solar System Bodies: Distances from the Sun and Orbital Periods**

| Body | Mean Distance (AU) | Perihelion (AU) | Aphelion (AU) | Orbital Period |
| :--- | ----: | ----: | ----: | :--- |
| **Mercury**   |  0.387 |  0.307 |  0.467 | 88.0 days |
| **Venus**     |  0.723 |  0.718 |  0.728 | 224.7 days |
| **Earth**     |  1.000 |  0.983 |  1.017 | 365.2 days |
| **Mars**      |  1.524 |  1.382 |  1.666 | 687.0 days |
| **Ceres**     |  2.766 |  2.558 |  2.977 | 4.6 years |
| **Jupiter**   |  5.204 |  4.950 |  5.459 | 11.9 years |
| **Saturn**    |  9.582 |  9.021 | 10.124 | 29.4 years |
| **Uranus**    | 19.201 | 18.282 | 20.099 | 84.0 years |
| **Neptune**   | 30.047 | 29.811 | 30.289 | 164.8 years |
| **Pluto**     | 39.482 | 29.658 | 49.305 | 247.9 years |
| **Haumea**    | 43.130 | 34.647 | 51.531 | 284.1 years |
| **Makemake**  | 45.790 | 38.104 | 53.376 | 305.0 years |
| **Eris**      | 67.781 | 38.257 | 97.306 | 557.4 years |
| **Sedna**     | ~544   | ~76.4  | ~1011  | 12,677 years |

The pattern jumps out. The four inner planets are packed within about 1.5 AU of the Sun.
Past the asteroid belt, the spacing opens up dramatically: Saturn is nearly twice as far
out as Jupiter, and Uranus twice as far as Saturn. Neptune, at 30 AU, is thirty
times farther from the Sun than we are. And the list doesn't stop there -- Pluto (whose
lopsided orbit brings it closer to the Sun than Neptune for part of each trip around), Eris, and Sedna, whose
orbit carries it out to hundreds of AU.

### Getting Around by Spacecraft

In the last article, our vehicles were feet, cars, and airplanes. At this scale, the only
vehicle we have is the spacecraft -- and even it struggles. A trip to Venus typically takes about 4 months. 
A trip to the outer planets takes years.

The two Voyager spacecraft, launched in 1977, show just how long:

| Event | Voyager 1 | Voyager 2 |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Launch | 5 Sep 1977 | 20 Aug 1977 |
| Jupiter | 5 Mar 1979 | 9 Jul 1979 |
| Saturn | 12 Nov 1980 | 25 Aug 1981 |
| Uranus | --- | 24 Jan 1986 |
| Neptune | --- | 25 Aug 1989 |

{{< nbas-image src="voyager-encounters.webp" width="500" align="right"
caption="Planetary encounters by the Voyager 1 and 2 probes.  NASA."
alt="Diagram showing the orbits of the outer planets with the trajectories of the Voyager 1 and 2 spacecraft and the dates of their planetary encounters." >}}

And those trips aren't even straight lines. On Earth, we know the difference between "as the crow flies" and road miles. In space the gap is bigger, for two reasons: the Sun's gravity bends every path, and the destination is moving. A spacecraft doesn't aim at a planet; it aims at where the planet will be when it arrives. The cheapest route from Jupiter to Saturn swings halfway around the Sun, about five times longer than the gap between their orbits, 18 to 22 AU of space to travel.

Voyager 2 needed twelve years to reach Neptune -- and that was with a boost from the
gravity of every giant planet along the way. At this scale, even our fastest machines
start to feel slow. So let's switch to the fastest vehicle there is.

## Light as a Ruler

Light travels about 300,000 kilometers every second. (How we worked that out is a story
of its own, and it's the subject of the next article.) That makes light-travel time a
second ruler for the same territory the AU covers.

Usually this gets presented backwards: "the light-time to Neptune is four hours." But
turn the question around and ask *how far light gets* in a familiar stretch of time --
a second, a minute, a day, a month. We have no feel at all for "173 AU" (the distance
Voyager 1 is from the Sun at present). However, we have a very good feel for what a day is.

**How Far Does Light Get?**

| In this long... | light travels about... | which gets you to... |
| :--- | ---: | :--- |
| 1 second | 300,000 km | Most of the way to the Moon (1.3 light-seconds) |
| 5 seconds | 1.5 million km | Sun-Earth L2, home of the Webb telescope |
| 1 minute | 18 million km (0.12 AU) | Not even an eighth of the way to the Sun |
| 8.3 minutes | 1 AU | The Sun |
| 1 hour | 7.2 AU | Just past Jupiter's orbit |
| 4 hours | 29 AU | Neptune |
| 1 day | 173 AU | Voyager 1, on November 18, 2026 |
| 1 week | ~1,200 AU | Beyond even Sedna's farthest point |
| 1 month | ~5,200 AU | Deep into the inner Oort Cloud (roughly) |

In about a second and a quarter, light reaches the Moon. In eight minutes it reaches the
Sun -- which means the Sun you see is the Sun as it was eight minutes ago. A full hour
barely carries it past Jupiter. Four hours to Neptune. And a day -- one ordinary day --
takes it out to where our most distant spacecraft is right now.

A few of these stops have stories worth telling.

### Five Seconds: Small Enough to Ignore

{{< nbas-image src="jwst-at-l2.jpg" width="450" align="right"
caption="JWST at the L2 point.  NASA, STScI, Public domain"
alt="Diagram showing the locations of the Lagrange Points compared Earth and Moon orbits.  An ellipse shows the halo orbit of the JWST spacecraft." >}}

About 1.5 million kilometers from Earth, directly away from the Sun, is a point called
Sun-Earth L2 -- four times farther than the Moon. The James Webb Space Telescope circles it 
in a "halo orbit" that varies between approximately 250,000 and 832,000 kilometers from the L2 point,
and so did the Gaia mission - now in a "retirement" orbit around the Sun.
The Roman Space Telescope will also reside there for its operations.

A signal takes about five seconds to get there, ten for the round trip. For mission
controllers, that's essentially nothing -- a short pause on a phone call. Keep that
five seconds in mind as a baseline, because the same physics, a bit farther out, starts
to matter a great deal.

### The 16-Minute Swing

Here's something the table hides: most of those light-times aren't fixed. Earth and the
other planets are all moving around the Sun, so the distance between us and any other
planet keeps changing.

For a planet in a nearly circular orbit, that change works out the same every time. When
Earth and the planet are on the same side of the Sun, we're closer by about 1 AU; when
they're on opposite sides, farther by about 1 AU. That's a swing of about 2 AU -- and
2 AU is about 16.6 light-minutes. Every planet's light-time swings by roughly the same
16 minutes.

{{< nbas-image src="fig-light-time-swing.svg" width="500"
caption="The range of one-way light time from Earth to each outer planet over 50 years (2026-2076), computed from an ephemeris. Every bar is roughly the same length -- the swing, plus a little from each orbit's eccentricity -- but only for Mars is that a big fraction of the total."
alt="Horizontal bars showing the minimum-to-maximum light time from Earth: Mars 3 to 22 minutes (7.2 times), Jupiter 33 to 54 minutes (1.6 times), Saturn 1 hour 7 minutes to 1 hour 32 minutes (1.4 times), Uranus 2 hours 24 minutes to 2 hours 50 minutes (1.2 times), and Neptune 4 hours to 4 hours 18 minutes (1.1 times)." >}}
{{< clear >}}

What changes is how much that matters. Neptune's four hours become four hours give or
take eight minutes -- a few percent, hardly worth mentioning. But for a planet close to
us, the same swing is enormous.

**Mars** is the extreme case. It's close enough that the swing dominates, and its
elongated orbit adds even more on top. A radio signal to Mars takes as little as about
3 minutes (182 seconds) when the two planets are at their closest, and as much as about 22 minutes 
when Mars is on the far side of the Sun. That's a sevenfold change -- and it means real-time
conversation with a crew on Mars will never be possible. At best, every question waits
six minutes for its answer.

In fact, the distribution of what the time sending a signal to Mars (or receiving one) 
shows that the median time is ~15 minutes (or a median distance of ~1.8 AU) with the
mode at 19-20 minutes:

{{< nbas-image src="fig-mars-light-time-histogram.svg" width="500"
caption="How the one-way light time between Earth and Mars is distributed over 50 years (2026-2076), computed from an ephemeris. The dashed line is the light time to the Sun."
alt="A histogram of the one-way light travel time between Earth and Mars, ranging from 3 to 22 minutes. There is a small peak at 5-6 minutes (5.8%) and a larger one at 19-20 minutes (11.4%). The median is about 15 minutes, and times under 6 minutes make up only 12% of the total." >}}
{{< clear >}}

The same swing is why some oppositions of Mars get excited headlines while others
don't. At opposition, Earth passes between Mars and the Sun, and Mars is as close and
bright as it gets for that cycle. But because Mars's orbit is noticeably elongated, how
close "close" is depends on where in its orbit Mars happens to be:

{{< nbas-image src="fig-mars-opposition-geometry.svg" width="500"
caption="Earth and Mars at each opposition, 2025-2045, with the Earth-Mars distance at closest approach. Oppositions near Mars's perihelion (lower right) bring it nearly twice as close as those near aphelion (upper left). Orbits and positions computed from an ephemeris (astropy)."
alt="Diagram of Earth's and Mars's orbits around the Sun, seen from above, with a line from Earth to Mars at each opposition from 2025 to 2045. The line for 2035, near Mars's perihelion, is the shortest at 0.38 AU; the line for 2027, near Mars's aphelion, is the longest at 0.68 AU." >}}
{{< clear >}}

At its best (the next time is in 2035), Mars comes within about 0.38 AU; at its worst,
like the coming opposition in February 2027, it stays about 0.68 AU away. Same event,
nearly double the apparent size -- and roughly 5x the brightness (about 1.75 magnitudes):

{{< nbas-image src="fig-mars-opposition-sizes.svg" width="500"
caption="Mars's apparent size at each opposition from 2025 to 2045, drawn to scale (angular diameter at closest approach, in arcseconds). The same Hubble image is used for every disk, so only the size is meaningful. Image: NASA. Dates UTC."
alt="Ten images of Mars in two rows, one for each opposition from 2025 to 2045, drawn to scale. The largest, 2035, is 24.6 arcseconds across; the smallest, 2027, is 13.8 arcseconds." >}}
{{< clear >}}

(Why the oppositions come about every 26 months, and why the great ones come only every
15 or 17 years, is a story about lapping on a racetrack -- we'll run that race in its own
article in February, just before Mars's next opposition.)

For almost everything else in the Solar System, the distances change too slowly to notice
in a lifetime. Halley's Comet is the rare exception: its orbit swings it from inside
Venus's orbit to beyond Neptune's (0.59 AU to 35.14 AU) over 76 years -- the one
dramatic change in distance a person can actually follow from beginning to end.
(Its most recent aphelion was in December 2023, so it's currently on its way back to the inner Solar System.)

### One Light-Day: Voyager 1

{{< nbas-image src="pale-blue-dot.png" width="450" align="right"
caption="Earth as seen by Voyager 1 on February 14, 1990, from about 40 AU. On November 18, 2026, Voyager 1 will be more than four times farther away. NASA/JPL-Caltech, public domain"
alt="The Pale Blue Dot image: Earth as a tiny point of light within a band of scattered sunlight." >}}

On November 18, 2026, at 10:16 UTC, Voyager 1 will be one light-day from Earth: a signal sent 
from mission control on one day will arrive at the spacecraft on the next. 
To reach this milestone, the spacecraft has been travelling for 
exactly 49 years, 2 months, and 13 days since its launch on September 5, 1977.

Voyager keeps moving outward, about 3.6 AU every year, so this is a milestone that
happens once and then slips into the past.  Voyager 2 is also still continuing outward, and
reaches this milestone around November 2035;  because of its path to visit the outer planets
Uranus and Neptune, it's moving at a slower speed (~15.3 km/s compared to Voyager 1's ~16.9 km/s).
Unfortunately, by the early 2030s, both spacecraft will no longer have enough power to continue
operations: from that moment onward, they'll continue their trek through the outer Solar System
silently.

### The Outer Wall

Beyond the planets, light-times start to be measured in days. Sedna, one of the most
distant known objects in the Solar System, is about 10.5 hours away by light at its
closest -- and almost six days at its farthest.

{{< nbas-image src="fig-sedna-orbit-scale.svg" width="500"
caption="Sedna's orbit to scale (perihelion ~76 AU, aphelion ~1,011 AU), with the distances light travels from the Sun in a day and in a week. Neptune's orbit -- and every planet inside it -- is the small circle around the Sun."
alt="A to-scale diagram: a long gold ellipse for Sedna's orbit extends from near the Sun to about 1,011 AU. A small dotted circle marks one light-day (173 AU), a large dashed circle marks one light-week (1,210 AU), and Neptune's orbit is a tiny circle around the Sun." >}}
{{< clear >}}

A light-week reaches about 1,200 AU; a light-month about 5,200 AU. Out there, we're into
the Oort Cloud -- the vast, sparse shell of icy bodies thought to surround the solar
system -- whose boundaries are only roughly known.  The table below shows the current expectations
of its dimensions.  The light-month is the outer wall for this article.

**Oort Cloud Size and Distance Ranges**

| Boundary Zone | NASA Est. Distance (AU) | One-Way Light Travel Time (from Sun) | Equivalent Distance |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Inner Edge | 2,000 to 5,000 AU | 11.5 to 29 days | ~300 billion to 750 billion km |
| Outer Edge | 10,000 to 100,000 AU | 58 days to 1.58 years | ~1.5 trillion to 15 trillion km |

## Wrapup

The planets are small worlds separated by enormous gaps. We've built two rulers for those
gaps: the AU, which turns the Solar System into a readable map, and light-time, which
turns distances into durations we can actually feel.

Beyond the light-month, the next rung is a light-year -- and a light-year gets us to the
stars. That step needs a new way of measuring altogether, one we'll explore later in this
series. First, though, we've been using the speed of light freely here. In the next
article, we'll find out how anyone ever managed to measure it.
