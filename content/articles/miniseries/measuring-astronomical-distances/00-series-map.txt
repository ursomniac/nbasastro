## Running Metaphor: "How far is far?" (series-wide)
Distance as lived experience. Two sides, running across the whole series.

### Side A — the vehicle (travel mode sets the scale)
In daily life, what counts as short / normal / long depends on how you're
getting there: a walk, a run, a bike ride, a drive, a train, a flight. The
metaphor has a LIFECYCLE across the series:
1. **It works** (measuring-distance, solar-system-scale): planets fit the
   everyday pattern once spacecraft are the vehicle — Venus is a hop,
   Neptune is a very long haul (Voyager 2's trip; duration UNVERIFIED).
2. **It gets upgraded** (speed-of-light onward): past the solar system,
   spacecraft trips become absurd (the Proxima "how many thousands of
   years?" question), so we switch to the fastest vehicle there is: light.
   Light-time = travel time by the ultimate vehicle. Proxima is a short trip
   by light; M 75 on the far side of the galaxy is a long one (distance
   UNVERIFIED).
3. **It breaks — the one-way street** (how-far-can-we-see): for the quasar
   of the week (z ~ 6), even light's trip stops being a simple journey.
   "How long did the trip take?" and "how far is it now?" give different
   answers. And a laser fired at it today would NEVER arrive — it's beyond
   the cosmological event horizon (~17 Gly comoving, confirmed in the
   how-far-can-we-see fact pass). By observer symmetry, their light sent
   today never reaches us either. We receive mail from places we can no
   longer write back to. Everyday intuition doesn't just fail — it inverts:
   seeing a place no longer means you could ever reach it.
   Fact-pass item: from memory, anything above roughly z ~ 1.8 is already
   beyond our event horizon — UNVERIFIED.

### Side B — the neighborhood (how crowded is it?)
Downtown / suburbs / semi-rural / the boonies — applied at every scale.
Measuring the gap between neighbors in units of the neighbors' own size is
itself a comparative ruler (ties to measuring-distance's thesis). Key
payoff (ratios from memory, UNVERIFIED): stars are separated by tens of
millions of their own diameters; galaxies by only ~25 (Andromeda). So
"rural" and "urban" flip with scale — stars almost never collide; galaxies
do. Plus the time dimension: physical density scales as (1+z)^3 (standard
cosmology), so "your neighborhood used to be a city" (exact factors for
5 / 10 Gyr ago UNVERIFIED; galaxy counts not conserved due to mergers).

## Throughlines (track across the series)
- Precision improves over time via successive methods/corrections:
  speed-of-light, measuring-the-au, parallax, ladder-cracks,
  hubble-tension (velocity correction layers).
- Measured, then made exact by definition: c (1983 meter redefinition) in
  speed-of-light; the AU (2012) in measuring-the-au.
- An early framework hands its native unit to everything built on it later:
  Kepler's Third Law -> AU (measuring-the-au); magnitude system -> parsec
  (parsecs-vs-light-years). Orbit-based distance recurs in galactic-scale
  (S2 orbiting Sgr A* -> distance to the galactic center; unverified).
- "Who published" vs. "who measured": Henderson/Bessel (parallax);
  Baade/Shapley credit dispute (ladder-cracks).
- Scale companions: solar-system-scale (AU) and galactic-scale (kpc) play
  the same role at successive scales.
- L2 callback: JWST (solar-system-scale) and Gaia (parallax) share the same
  parking spot.
- Parallax principle ("closer object, bigger angle, better precision"):
  Eros in measuring-the-au -> stellar parallax in parallax -> parsec
  definition in parsecs-vs-light-years.
- CANDIDATE, UNVERIFIED: c and the AU bootstrapped from each other (see
  speed-of-light and measuring-the-au notes).
