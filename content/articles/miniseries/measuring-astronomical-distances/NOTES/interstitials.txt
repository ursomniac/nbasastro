## I-1: JLU / SLU — Coining Our Own Unit
**Status:** idea, facts confirmed | **Possible series:** AF (or HSW)
(Moved out of old #1 beat 5b — author decision: this is an interstitial,
not a beat in measuring-distance.)

   A real, official "Lunar Distance" (LD, ~384,400 km) already exists and is
   used casually in astronomy (e.g., describing how close a near-Earth
   asteroid passed, in multiples of LD). There is no equivalent named unit
   for other planets' moon systems, even though the same trick could build
   the same kind of intuition for Jupiter's or Saturn's systems.
   - Genuinely nice hook: Io orbits Jupiter at ~421,800 km — almost exactly
     1 real Lunar Distance. So "1 JLU" (if loosely anchored to Io's orbit)
     lands close to the same raw km as our own Moon's orbit.
   - The payoff: same *distance*, wildly different *dynamics* — Io completes
     an orbit in under 2 days vs. the Moon's ~27, purely because Jupiter's
     mass is so much greater. This reinforces the comparative-unit idea
     while smuggling in a free orbital-mechanics/mass lesson.
   - **Saturn confirmed via search**: Dione orbits Saturn at a semi-major
     axis of 377,396 km — about 2% *less* than the Earth-Moon distance
     (matches Wikipedia's own stated comparison). Dione's orbital period is
     2.737 days, about one-tenth the Moon's ~27.3 days, again attributed
     directly to Saturn's much greater mass (95x Earth's). So Dione is the
     natural "SLU" anchor, mirroring Io almost exactly.
   - **This makes a genuinely strong table/graphic**: Io (~10% more than
     1 LD, ~15x faster) and Dione (~2% less than 1 LD, ~10x faster) both
     sit close to "1 Earth-Moon distance" from their respective planets,
     yet both orbit dramatically faster — a clean two-planet echo of the
     same mass/gravity lesson, not a strained one-off coincidence.
   - Framing note: present this explicitly as *us coining a teaching device*,
     not an established astronomical unit — should be transparent about that
     rather than implying JLU/SLU is real terminology.

## CANDIDATE: How close can Pluto and Neptune get?
(PINNED -- UNVERIFIED, all from memory)
 - Pluto's perihelion is inside Neptune's orbit, yet they never collide.
 - Two separate answers, and the difference between them IS the story:
   1. Orbits: how close do the two ellipses come in space? Pluto's orbit is
      tilted (~17 deg), so the paths never actually cross.
   2. Objects: how close do the two BODIES ever get? Pluto is locked in a
      3:2 resonance with Neptune (3 Neptune orbits per 2 Pluto orbits), which
      keeps Neptune far away whenever Pluto is near perihelion. Recalled:
      never closer than ~17 AU -- and Pluto gets closer to URANUS than to
      Neptune. VERIFY both.
 - Angle: "the orbits look like they cross on the diagram; the planets
   never come close" -- a diagram-vs-reality beat. Figure candidate: both
   orbits from above AND edge-on.
 - Home: after A02 (solar-system scale).

## CANDIDATE SECTION (or companion piece): Launch windows and the long way around
 - Launch windows to Mars recur every ~26 months = the synodic period.
 - Minimum-energy (Hohmann) transfers go halfway around the Sun:
   Jupiter -> Saturn half-ellipse ~22 AU long vs 4.4 AU "dot to dot," ~10 years.
   (Computed from circular orbits at 5.2 and 9.5 AU -- re-verify.)
 - Faster = straighter: Voyager 1 went Jupiter -> Saturn in ~20 months via
   gravity assist; path far shorter (get real value from JPL Horizons).
 - Rule: the cheaper the trip, the longer the path.
 - Only light travels essentially straight across the solar system.

 OK - getting around by spacecraft...  this adds another twist - solar system navigation.  Because the paths are NEVER in a straight line (CAN they be - I think that would not be possible - certainly not practical).    It's the same issue we saw with the synodic period calculation...   People tend to just compare semi-amjor axes of two bodies and "connect the dots" - the reality is that the actual path traveled is much longer because - first - you have the Sun's gravity to contend with, and second - everything is moving.  

So getting from Jupiter to Saturn isn't just "go another 4.4 AU" it could be ~22 AU distance traveled.
