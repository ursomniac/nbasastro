## Introduction

{{< nbas-image src="US_VT_BENN_HOME_26_0208-0003.webp" filter="lanczos" fullwidth="true" caption="Seven and a half hours of the sky turning, facing southwest on February 8, 2026 (thirty 15-minute frames, stacked). Michael A. Archer, NBAS, used with permission." >}}

Everyone knows the stars rise in the east and set in the west. It's one of the first things
anyone learns about the night sky, and it's true. But go outside on a clear January evening and
watch for an hour, and that sentence starts to feel incomplete. Face north and the stars aren't
heading west at all: they're wheeling slowly around a single point. Face east and they don't rise
straight up: they climb on a slant. The rule is right, but it describes something bigger than it
lets on.

That something is a single motion: Earth turning on its axis, once a day, carrying us and our
view of the sky along with it. This is the first of seven articles in
[As the Sky Turns](/miniseries/as-the-sky-turns/), a miniseries about what that one motion does
to the sky: which stars never set, why each season has its own constellations, why the stars in a
smart scope's image rotate, and how the sky itself shifts over thousands of years. This first part
covers the basics: what the turning looks like, why it looks different in each direction, and
how to measure it with nothing but your hand.

---

## Four Windows on One Motion

Every night out starts the same way: figuring out which way you're facing. So let's do the
same, one direction at a time, on a late-January evening, watching each part of the sky for one hour,
from 8 to 9 PM.

{{< nbas-image src="fig-four-windows.webp" fullwidth="true"
caption="One hour of sky motion from North Adams, 8 PM to 9 PM EST on January 27, 2027, facing each of the four compass points. Each dot is a star's position at 8 PM; the gold arrow shows its real path over the next hour."
alt="Four sky panels. Facing north, stars circle Polaris counterclockwise and Polaris barely moves. Facing east, stars including Regulus rise up and to the right on a slant. Facing south, Orion and Sirius move left to right in a shallow arc. Facing west, the Great Square of Pegasus sinks down and to the right." >}}

**Facing north,** find Polaris, a bit less than halfway up the sky. Over the hour, the stars
around it swing *counterclockwise*, like a slow clock running backward. The Big Dipper, low in
the northeast, is climbing, its bowl leading. Cassiopeia, high in the northwest, is on its way
down. Polaris itself barely moves at all.

**Facing south,** the motion is completely different. Here the stars move *left to right*, from
the southeast toward the southwest, each one climbing until it's due south, then sinking again.
Orion is the showpiece: its belt crosses due south just after 9 PM, about 46° up, with Sirius,
the brightest star in the night sky, following about an hour later and lower.

**Facing east,** stars are rising, but not straight up. They come up *on a slant*, up and to the
right. Regulus, the brightest star in Leo, is about 13° above the eastern horizon at 8 PM and 24°
up by 9. You might notice that the slant is fairly gentle; that angle depends on where you're
standing on Earth.

**Facing west,** it's the mirror image: stars sink *down and to the right* on the same kind of
slant. The Great Square of Pegasus is going down, its lowest corner dropping from about 16° to
just 5° above the horizon in that one hour.

And straight overhead? The stars there simply cross from east to west.

Four directions, four motions that look nothing alike: a wheel, an arc, a climb, a slide. But
they aren't four motions. They're one motion, seen from inside it. (From the Southern Hemisphere
it all runs in mirror image: observers there face north to watch the stars arc overhead from right
to left, and the stars near the south celestial pole circle it clockwise, with no bright star to
mark the spot.)

## One Motion Behind Them

The four windows share a secret: the sky isn't doing any of it. Earth is. It spins once a day,
from west to east, and carries us along. So everything beyond the planet appears to wheel the
other way, east to west, around the one point in the sky that Earth's axis aims at.

{{< nbas-callout title="Definition: Celestial Pole" kind="definition" >}}
The **north celestial pole** is the point in the sky directly above Earth's North Pole, where
Earth's spin axis points. Every star in the sky circles it once a day. Polaris sits very close to
it, close enough to look fixed over a night, but not exactly on it. (The **south celestial pole**
is the matching point above Earth's South Pole.)
{{< /nbas-callout >}}

{{< nbas-callout title="Definition: Celestial Equator" kind="definition" >}}
The **celestial equator** is Earth's equator projected onto the sky: the great circle halfway
between the two celestial poles, dividing the sky into northern and southern halves. A star on
the celestial equator rises due east and sets due west, from anywhere on Earth except the
poles themselves.
{{< /nbas-callout >}}

The whole sky turns as one piece. Constellations don't stretch or twist as they go around; the
Big Dipper that climbs out of the northeast on a January evening is the same shape when it stands high
overhead in spring. That's because the stars are so far away that nothing about Earth's motion
rearranges them. Even the full width of Earth's orbit, some 300 million km, shifts the nearest
stars by less than a second of arc, a change it took until 1838 to measure at all (the story is
told in [Parallax](/articles/2026/12/parallax/)).

{{< nbas-image src="fig-same-rate.webp" width="500" align="right"
caption="Every star turns 15° around the celestial pole each hour, but how far it moves across the sky depends on its circle: 15° × cos(declination). View looking straight down the axis; arcs drawn to scale."
alt="Concentric circles around the celestial pole with a one-hour wedge. The gold arc on each circle grows from 2.6 degrees near the pole to 15 degrees at the celestial equator." >}}

Here's the part that explains the four windows. Every star turns around the pole at exactly the
same rate: 15° an hour. But they don't all travel the same distance across the sky. A star near
the pole rides a small circle and barely seems to move. A star on the celestial equator rides the
biggest circle there is and sweeps across the sky fastest. Think of a merry-go-round: the horse at
the rim and the one next to the hub go around together, once per turn, but the rim horse covers
far more ground. On the sky, a star's hourly path shrinks from 15° on the celestial equator to
2.6° at declination +80°, as the figure shows.

Now go back to the four windows. Facing north, you're looking at the hub, where the circles are
small and tight around Polaris. Facing south, you're looking out toward the rim, where the big
circles arc across the sky with the horizon cutting them off. Facing east and west, you're
watching the rim come up and go down, tipped at an angle set by where the pole sits in your sky.
One wheel, four views.

## Measure It Yourself

A full turn is 360°, and it takes (about) 24 hours, so the sky turns at about **15° an hour**.[^sidereal]
That's a quarter of a degree a minute: about the width of the full Moon every two minutes.

{{< article-table align="right" >}}

| At arm's length | Covers about |
|---|---|
| Pinky width | 1° |
| Three middle fingers | 5° |
| Closed fist | 10° |
| Index to pinky, spread | 15° |
| Thumb to pinky, spread | 25° |

{{< /article-table >}}

You don't need any equipment to check it. Your hand, held at arm's length, is a surprisingly
good protractor. The numbers in the table hold up across ages, too: people with smaller hands
generally have shorter arms, so a child's fist covers about the same 10° as an adult's. A star's
height above the horizon, its [altitude](/articles/2026/05/altitude-azimuth-and-air-mass/), runs
from 0° at the horizon to 90° straight overhead, about nine fists.

Better still, check your hand against the sky itself. The Big Dipper, low in the northeast on
January evenings, happens to contain every one of these measures: the two pointer stars of the
bowl are about three fingers apart, the bowl is a fist across, the handle is an index-to-pinky
spread, and the whole Dipper is a thumb-to-pinky span. Once you know how your own hand compares,
you can measure anything.

{{< clear >}}

{{< nbas-image src="fig-dipper-ruler.webp" fullwidth="true"
caption="The Big Dipper as a ruler. Each panel picks out one pair of stars whose separation matches a hand measure at arm's length. Angles computed from star positions; the Dipper's tilt changes through the night, but these spacings don't."
alt="Four small panels of the Big Dipper, each highlighting one pair of stars: Dubhe to Merak, 5.4 degrees, three fingers; Dubhe to Megrez, 10.2 degrees, a fist; Megrez to Alkaid, 15.7 degrees, index to pinky; Dubhe to Alkaid, 25.7 degrees, thumb to pinky." >}}

So in one hour, a star on the celestial equator moves about a fist and a half across the sky.
Stars closer to the pole move less, in proportion to the smaller circles they ride.

{{< nbas-callout title="Try It Tonight" kind="experiment" >}}
First, calibrate: hold your fist up to the Big Dipper's bowl and your spread hand to the whole
Dipper. Then pick a bright star sitting just above a rooftop, chimney or treetop, and note
exactly where you're standing. Come back to the same spot an hour later
and measure how far the star has moved, in fists. Then do it again facing the other way: one star in the south, such as one in Orion, and one in
the north, near Polaris. The southern star should have moved about a fist and a half; the northern one, much
less, and in a different direction.
{{< /nbas-callout >}}

## Through the Eyepiece

Point an untracked telescope at a star, a Dobsonian or any scope on a simple mount, and look
away for a few minutes. When you come back, the star is gone. The sky hasn't sped up; the
telescope magnifies the motion along with everything else.

On the celestial equator, the sky slides past at 15 arcminutes per minute of time, or 15
arcseconds every second. Away from the equator it's slower, by a factor of the cosine of the
star's declination: the same rim-and-hub effect, now in the eyepiece. Turned into time, here's
how long a star takes to drift across a 1° field of view:

| Target | Declination | Time to cross a 1° field |
|---|---|---|
| Orion's Belt | ≈ 0° | 4.0 minutes |
| Pleiades | +24° | 4.4 minutes |
| Double Cluster | +57° | 7.3 minutes |
| Polaris | +89.4° | hours |

The surprise is how little that changes until you get close to the pole. The Pleiades, about a
quarter of the way from the celestial equator to the pole, drift almost as fast as Orion's Belt.
The drift isn't cut in half until +60°, and most of the slowing happens in the last 30°. (The
cosine stays close to 1 for small angles and only drops steeply as it nears 90°.)

Magnification makes it worse: the drift is the same, but the window is smaller. At 100× with a
typical eyepiece, the field is about half a degree across, and Orion's Belt is through it in
about two minutes. The cure is a mount that turns with the sky, which is what tracking mounts
and smart scopes do. That cure brings a twist of its own: over a long session, the field of view
slowly rotates.

## Star Trails

{{< nbas-image src="US_CA_INYO_DVNP_22_0924-0003.webp" filter="lanczos" width="500" align="right"
caption="One hour of the sky turning around the north celestial pole: a single 61-minute exposure from Death Valley National Park, September 2022. Every trail covers the same angle around the center, about 15°, but trails far from the pole are much longer. Michael A. Archer, NBAS, used with permission."
alt="Concentric star trails circling a point near the center of the frame, short near the center and long toward the edges, with the Milky Way's glow across the upper left." >}}

A camera on a fixed tripod, with the shutter held open, records the turning directly. Each star
draws its own piece of a circle around the pole, and the length of that piece is simple
arithmetic: 15° for every hour of exposure.

Michael Archer pointed his camera toward Polaris from Death Valley and held the shutter open for
61 minutes. In that time the sky turned about 15°, and every trail in the frame covers that same
15° of its circle. But near the center the trails are tiny curls, and out toward the edges
they're long, sweeping arcs. It's the merry-go-round again, recorded on the sensor. Given a long
enough exposure, even Polaris draws a small arc of its own, because it's close to the pole but
not on it.

{{< nbas-image src="US_VT_BENN_HOME_26_0302-0001.webp" filter="lanczos" width="500" align="left"
caption="Facing southwest, March 2, 2026: 100 minutes of trails (ten 10-minute frames). Away from the pole the trails are long, nearly straight, and slant down toward the western horizon. Orion and Sirius are among them; the blue sky is moonlight. Michael A. Archer, NBAS, used with permission."
alt="Long, nearly straight star trails slanting down to the right across a deep blue sky above a line of bare trees." >}}

Turn around, face away from the pole, and the trails straighten out. Michael's frame from
his home, looking southwest for 100 minutes, shows them as long, nearly straight streaks
slanting down toward the western horizon: the rim of the merry-go-round, heading down. The image
at the top of this article is the same view held open for seven and a half hours, long enough
for the sky to turn about 112°, far more than the frame can hold.

{{< clear >}}

{{< nbas-callout title="How Do We Know It's Earth That's Turning?" >}}
For most of history, a turning sky and a turning Earth explained the night equally well. The
first simple, direct demonstration came in 1851. In the Meridian Room of the Paris Observatory,
Léon Foucault hung a heavy ball from an 11-meter wire and set it swinging; a month later he
repeated it under the dome of the Panthéon with a 67-meter wire. The swing holds its direction in
space while the floor turns beneath it, so the swing appears to rotate slowly, clockwise. At the
North Pole it would go all the way around once a day; at the equator, not at all; in Paris, once
in about 32 hours. (In North Adams, it would take about 35.)

There's an everyday proof, too, probably on a roof near you. A satellite TV dish points at one
fixed spot in the sky. A satellite can only stay up by orbiting, and this one holds still over
the same spot on the ground only because its orbit, 35,786 km above the equator, takes exactly as
long as one turn of the Earth. The dish never has to move because the satellite keeps pace with a
spinning planet.
{{< /nbas-callout >}}

## Wrapup

One motion, around one point, at one rate. Everything in this article, the wheel in the north,
the arc in the south, the slanted climb and slide in the east and west, the stars that race
through an eyepiece and the ones that barely move, the curls and streaks on a long exposure,
comes from Earth turning once a day beneath a sky that holds still.

Watch the north long enough, though, and you'll notice something else: some stars circle the
pole without ever touching the horizon, while others, far to the south, never come up at all.
Which stars fall into each group depends on where you're standing on Earth. That's where the
sky turns next.

[^sidereal]: More precisely, about 15.04° per hour: the stars come around in about 23 hours
56 minutes, not 24 hours. Where the missing four minutes go is a story of its own.
