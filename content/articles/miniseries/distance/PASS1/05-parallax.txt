# Article 5: Parallax — 61 Cygni to Gaia
**Series:** HP | **Position in arc:** 5 of 8

## Core Hook
Not a re-telling of the 61 Cygni article — a widening of the lens. That
piece told one star's detailed biography, with its parallax story as one

of three near-simultaneous breakthroughs, condensed rather than dropped, and

## Structure

### 1. The Three-Way Finish Line (1838-1839) — recast, not just repeated
This is the big addition the original article undersells. Verified
chronology:
- **Henderson (Alpha Centauri)**: actually collected his data *first*, at
  the Cape of Good Hope in 1832-1833 — years before Bessel even started
  his 61 Cygni campaign. But he sat on the reduction and didn't announce a
  result until January 1839.
- **Struve (Vega)**: began observing Vega in 1835, announced a preliminary
  parallax in 1837 (0.261" -> ~12.5 ly) — but this figure was significantly
  off. He later revised it in late 1839 to a much larger value (~26 ly),
  which happened to land close to the modern value (~25.3 ly) almost by
  correction rather than original precision.
- **Bessel (61 Cygni)**: started later than Henderson, but announced his
  result first (~December 1838), narrowly beating Struve and Henderson to
  publication.
- **The genuine irony worth foregrounding**: Bessel is conventionally
  credited as "first," but Henderson's underlying *data* predates Bessel's
  entire campaign. Priority went to whoever published, not whoever
  measured first — a good honest beat about how "who discovered X" stories
  often quietly mean "who published X" instead.
- 61 Cygni's own numbers (Bessel's 313.6 +/- 13.6 mas -> ~10.4 ly, refined
  to 360.2 +/- 12.1 mas by 1849) can be given briefly here, condensed from
  the full treatment in the existing article, with a direct link for
  readers who want the full false-starts saga (Piazzi, Arago/Mathieu,
  Peters, von Lindenau, Struve's own skepticism) rather than repeating it
  all here.

### 2. From One Star to Thousands — the proliferation era
The technique spread over the following century (photographic astrometry
programs, more observatories, more stars measured) — this section needs
lighter treatment and should NOT assert specific precision figures for this
middle era without verification at drafting time; general framing ("good
enough to extend from a handful of the nearest stars to thousands, but
still fundamentally limited by ground-based atmospheric blurring") is safer
than invented specifics.

### 3. Hipparcos (1989-1993) — leaving the atmosphere
- Launched 8 August 1989 (ESA), operated until August 1993.
- Name: "High Precision Parallax Collecting Satellite" — a deliberate nod
  to the ancient astronomer Hipparchus, worth a one-line mention.
- Measured ~118,000-118,200 stars to about 1 milliarcsecond precision (main
  catalogue), plus a lower-precision Tycho catalogue of over a million
  stars (~20-30 mas), later extended to 2.5 million stars (Tycho-2, 2000).
- The core conceptual point: first-ever *space-based* astrometry mission,
  escaping the atmospheric blurring that had capped ground-based precision
  for 150 years — a fundamentally different kind of improvement than "a
  better heliometer," more like changing the rules of the game.
- Good concrete precision analogy (verified): 2 milliarcseconds is roughly
  the angle subtended by a hair growing for one second, held at arm's
  length a meter away.

### 4. Gaia (2013-present) — the current state of the art
- Launched 19 December 2013 (ESA), orbits the Sun-Earth L2 point — the
  *exact same location* as JWST in article #3's notes, worth an explicit
  callback ("the same gravitational parking spot we met with JWST").
- Surveying roughly 1-2 billion stars (about 1% of the Milky Way's total
  stellar population) to precisions in the microarcsecond range (commonly
  cited as ~7-24 microarcseconds depending on star brightness) — roughly
  1,000x better than Hipparcos, which was itself ~100-200x better than
  the best ground-based efforts before it.
- Current major release is Gaia DR3 (June 2022) — this is the source of
  the 61 Cygni article's own "modern number" (285.9949 mas -> 11.404 ly),
  so this piece should land on that exact same figure to click together
  with the existing article rather than introduce a different number.
- **Check at drafting time**: a DR4 release has reportedly been planned
  around 2026 — given this arc's own publication window (Dec 2026-Jan
  2027), worth verifying whether DR4 has actually landed by the time this
  article goes live, since a newer, more precise number might exist by
  then.

### 5. Wrap-up — the pattern across three articles
Explicitly name the throughline: c (article 2), the AU (article 4), and
now stellar distance via parallax (this article) have all followed the
same shape — a rough first measurement, refined by better instruments and
independent methods, eventually reaching a precision where the "measured
value" and "true value" become almost indistinguishable. Tee up #6 (parsecs
vs. light-years): the parsec itself is *defined* by this exact technique
(1 arcsecond of parallax = 1 parsec, by construction) — setting up the
derivation #6 needs to do.
