# Article 1: Measuring Distance
**Slug:** measuring-distance | **Series:** AF | **Position:** 1 of 9 (prologue)

## Core Hook
Not a glossary of units — a piece about *perspective*. The point isn't "here are
the conversion factors," it's building an actual felt sense of scale, the way
*Powers of Ten* builds intuition by anchoring every jump to something concrete
you already understand, rather than just multiplying by ten and moving on.

The specific problem this piece has to solve: miles/km stay intuitive a long
way (a trillion km is still "a number of km," however large), but something
like a megaparsec has *nothing* in ordinary life to anchor against. This
article's job is to name that problem and introduce the solution astronomers
use at every scale: choose a ruler that keeps the numbers human-sized.

**Scope change from old #1:** this article no longer does the AU deep-dive
(table, worked examples, Mars). That moved to solar-system-scale. This piece
is the broad, book-style opening — and it must STAND ON ITS OWN as an NBAS
article (leave the reader more capable or curious; point toward the sky).

## Units Order (author decision)
Start in miles (the reader's everyday unit), then move to km (the science
unit), then onward to astronomical rulers. The article itself acts out the
comparative-unit idea.

## Beats (from v0 intro + old #1 beats 1, 5, 6)
1. **Everyday scale** — 15 miles to your favorite restaurant isn't a bad
   drive; walking 15 miles is extreme. We have real intuition for large-ish
   distances: the width of the continental US (XXXX miles) "reckons" even if
   we've never driven or flown it; we put 100,000+ miles on a car over years.
2. **The Moon still feels graspable** — ~238,xxx miles; it doesn't seem
   *that* far; it's the closest astronomical body.
3. **The switch to km** — hazier for US readers (less so elsewhere): ~[384,400]
   km. The grade-school 93,000,000 miles to the Sun (~150 million km, or
   150 Gm).
4. **Sizes vs. distances** — planet and even Sun *sizes* stay comprehensible
   (Venus xxxx, Mars xxxx, Jupiter xxxxx, Sun's diameter xxxxxx), but
   *distances* are where the zeros pile up and we lose our grip.
5. **Why raw miles/km break down** (old beat 1) — a unit is only useful if it
   stays legible. "Astronomers are no different, really" — so they build
   other units that let them internalize perspective.
6. **"It's not just linear distance — it's comparative distance."** (old
   beat 5 — now arguably this article's THESIS, not an aside). Science
   doesn't always convert everything back to one base unit; each domain
   picks a natural reference size:
   - Planetary sizes -> Earth radii / Earth diameters
   - Stars -> solar radii
   - Solar system -> AU
   - Stellar neighborhood -> light-years / parsecs
   - Galaxy structure -> galactic diameters
   - Clusters & groups -> galactic radii, or Mpc
   This isn't a quirk, it's a *pattern*. But flag honestly that the pattern
   gets *harder* to feel the further out you go — an AU means something
   because Earth-Sun is a real, given distance; a megaparsec has no
   everyday anchor.
7. **The ladder of rulers ahead** (old beat 6) — close to home, AU (the
   Earth-Sun distance, that 93,000,000 miles); out of the solar system,
   light-years or parsecs; then steps of 1000: kly/kpc, Mly/Mpc, and now
   Gly/Gpc. Plus the shift to light-time measures. Framed as "the same idea,
   applied at scales even harder to feel" — a glimpse, not a cliffhanger the
   article depends on. Should raise (not answer) "why two units — light-years
   AND parsecs?" since parsecs-vs-light-years answers it.

## Figures/Facts Needed (fact pass — none verified yet for this article)
- Width of the continental US (miles).
- Moon mean distance: 384,400 km is the figure used in old notes; the miles
  value ~238,855 is arithmetic (384,400 / 1.609344), not independently
  verified. Placeholders in v0 ("238xxx", "[400,000] km") should match.
- Diameters: Venus, Mars, Jupiter, Sun.
- Sun distance: 93,000,000 mi / ~150 million km — AU exact value
  149,597,870,700 m confirmed (IAU 2012); "150 Gm" = 1.5 x 10^11 m correct.
- Terminology answer for the v0 note: stepping by 10^3 matches SI prefix
  spacing (kilo, mega, giga); writing exponents in multiples of 3 is
  "engineering notation."
