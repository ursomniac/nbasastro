# Article 2: Distance at Solar System Scale
**Slug:** solar-system-scale | **Series:** AF | **Position:** 2 of 9

Built from old #1 beats 2-4 (AU, table, worked examples, Mars opposition)
and old #3's core hook + inside-out ladder from 1 second through 1 month.

## Core Hook (three hooks, folded together)
1. **The AU is the one rung readers already half-own.** You are standing
   1 AU from the Sun right now. This section has to do the actual work of
   making AU-scale distances *feel* like something — before later articles
   ask the reader to make the same leap for parsecs and megaparsecs with much
   less to hold onto.
2. **Light-time as a second ruler for the same territory** — the AU and
   light-time describe the same solar system two ways.
3. **The inversion (from old #3): why switch to time as a distance unit at
   all?** Instead of "here's the light-time to object X," ask "how far do you
   get in a familiar chunk of time?" — 1 second, 1 minute, 1 hour, 1 day,
   1 week, 1 month. This works because we already have real intuition for
   what a week or a month *feels* like, even though we have no intuition at
   all for what "173 AU" feels like. The inversion is the argument, not just
   a device.

## Structure (draft — see Open Questions on how AU and light-time combine)

### 1. Define the AU concretely
~150 million km (exact since 2012: 149,597,870,700 m), ~8.3 light-minutes,
Earth-Sun mean distance. No history here — measuring-the-au tells that story
(forward link; "a topic for a later article" until written).

### 2. Solar system table
Planets' (and notable bodies') distances from the Sun. v0 plan: columns for
km / AU / light travel time. Mercury through Neptune, plus reach objects like
Sedna to show how far the ruler still has to stretch within "our own
neighborhood."

### 3. Worked examples (the Powers-of-Ten heart of the piece)
Not just "Sedna is X AU away" — build intuition through concrete scenarios:
- **What 1 AU means at Neptune's distance vs. near Earth** (relative vs.
  absolute significance of "1 AU").
- **Mars — one distance variation, two consequences (merged from old #1
  and old #3):**
  - *Opposition size/brightness:* opposition distance ranges from about
    0.37-0.38 AU (perihelic) to about 0.68 AU (aphelic) — confirmed via
    multiple sources. Same event, nearly double the apparent size and
    roughly 5x the brightness depending on where in that range it falls.
    Explains something readers have noticed (why some oppositions are hyped
    and others aren't).
  - *Communication delay:* one-way light-time ranges from about 3 minutes
    (near close/perihelic opposition) to about 22 minutes (at conjunction,
    ~2.7 AU) — confirmed via search. Real-time conversation with a Mars crew
    is *never* possible; the delay varies ~7x across the ~26-month
    Earth-Mars cycle.
- Possible additional example: Jupiter or Saturn's distance variation, for
  contrast with how much *less* dramatic 1 AU is that far out — reinforcing
  relative vs. absolute.

### 4. The light-time ladder (inside-out, 1 second -> 1 month)

**1 second** — Light covers ~300,000 km/s. The Moon (384,400 km) is just
over 1 light-second away — "in about a second and a quarter, you've reached
the Moon."

**L2 (~5 seconds) — the "small enough to ignore" counterpoint** — Earth-Sun
L2 is about 1.5 million km away (~4x the Earth-Moon distance), giving a
one-way delay of about 5 seconds, ~10 seconds round-trip. JWST orbits in a
halo around L2 rather than sitting at the point, so its distance varies
(~1.5-1.8 million km), but the delay is essentially always "about 5
seconds." Roman Space Telescope is also planned for an L2 halo orbit —
verify directly rather than assume by analogy. Contrast point: a 5-second
lag is basically invisible to mission operations, vs. Mars (constrains
crewed-mission communication) and Voyager (forces fully autonomous
operation) — same phenomenon, wildly different consequences by scale.

**1 minute** — ~18 million km / ~0.12 AU. Not yet at the Sun — sets up the
Sun at ~8.3 light-minutes as the next real landmark.

**Minutes** — Sun: ~8.3 light-minutes (1 AU), "you're seeing the Sun as it
was 8 minutes ago." Mars: see worked example above (3-22 min).

**1 hour** — ~7.2 AU — just beyond Jupiter's orbit (~5.2 AU). Simple
checkpoint before spacecraft-scale distances.

**8 hours** = 57.6 AU - beyond Neptune and most of the Kuiper Belt.   

**1 day — the Voyager 1 tie-in (timely, not evergreen)** — ~173 AU.
Voyager 1 reaches 1 light-day from Earth on **November 18, 2026** —
confirmed via multiple sources (NASA-sourced reporting, EarthSky), converging
on that date and ~173.1-173.8 AU. Distance keeps growing (~3.6 AU/year), so
pin the "1 light-day" moment to the specific date rather than presenting
Voyager's distance as a fixed number that will go stale.

**1 week / 1 month** — rough scale only: light-week ~1,200 AU, light-month
~5,200 AU (~0.08 ly) — extreme trans-Neptunian/inner Oort Cloud territory.
Oort Cloud boundaries vary by source and aren't precisely nailed down —
present as approximate scale-setting, not precise boundaries. The
light-month is this article's outer wall.

### 5. Ending
Hand off outward: "beyond the light-month..." — the next rung (1
light-year, the nearest stars) belongs to the stars articles (parallax,
