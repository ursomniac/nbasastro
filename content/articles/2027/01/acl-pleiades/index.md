## Introduction

{{< nbas-image src="banner-m45.webp" fullwidth="true" 
caption="The Pleiades in the heart of the bull." alt="star map of Taurus overlaid with a rendering of a Bull and showing the Pleiades" >}}

{{< object-specs align="right" >}}

Of all the objects we examine, the Pleiades is the one you least need help finding. On a
January evening it rides high in the south, a small, tight knot of stars that catches the eye
even from town. Nobody discovered it. People have been looking at it, naming it, and telling
stories about it since long before there were records.

So "a closer look" means something different here. You don't need more magnification to see
the Pleiades. What you need is to know what you're looking at, and that turns out to go a
good deal deeper than six little stars.

Start with a puzzle. They're called the Seven Sisters, but most people count six. That isn't
a modern complaint: in the third century BC the Greek poet Aratus named all seven sisters and
then admitted that only six can be seen. We'll come back to why.

{{< nbas-image src="vedder-1885.webp" width="400" align="right"
caption="Elihu Vedder, The Pleiades (1885). Public domain, via Wikimedia Commons"
alt="Elihu Vedder's painting of the Pleiades as seven young women in the night sky." >}}
Nearly every culture that could see the Pleiades gave it a name, and most of them saw young
women: sisters, maidens, daughters. But not all of them did:

- **A hen and her chicks.** The old Hungarian name, *Fiastyúk*, means "a hen with chicks."
  Norse tradition made them Freyja's hens, and a Thai folk tale tells of a hen and her six
  chicks turned into stars.
- **A gathering.** The Japanese name, *Subaru*, means "coming together." (Yes, the car company
  — count the stars on the badge.)
- **A sieve.** One Belarusian name is *Sitechko*, "a sieve."
- **A mother and her daughters.** For some Māori iwi, Matariki is a mother surrounded by her
  six daughters. Its rising marks the Māori new year, and since 2022 New Zealand has kept it
  as a national public holiday.
- **Mothers of a god.** In India the cluster is Krittika, in some tellings the wives of the
  seven sages, in others the six mothers who raised the war god Skanda.
- **Sisters on the run.** In Greek myth the sisters flee the hunter Orion; in many Aboriginal
  Australian traditions, too, the Pleiades are young women pursued by a man in the stars we
  call Orion.

Many of these traditions share the same puzzle: seven are named, six are seen, and there's a
story about where the missing one went.

## A Family of Stars

The Pleiades is an open cluster: a family of stars born together from the same cloud of gas
and still traveling together through the Galaxy. At about 444 light-years (136 parsecs), it's
one of the nearest star clusters to us and the nearest of all the Messier objects.

The six or seven stars you see by eye are only the brightest members of the family. Using
positions and motions measured by the Gaia spacecraft, astronomers count roughly 1,200 to
1,500 stars that belong to the cluster, plus a thin stream of members that extends as far as 40
parsecs (130 light-years) from its center.
<!-- NOTE: how far away in the sky does this stream extend?  I'm getting ~17°. -->

The bright ones are hot, blue-white B-type stars. Even Pleione, one of the fainter named
stars, has about 3.4 times the Sun's mass and shines about 180 times as brightly. Stars like
that don't last long by cosmic standards, and their presence is the first clue that the
Pleiades is young.

The cluster isn't permanent, either. Gaia shows four of its massive members on their way out
of the inner cluster, and the stream around it is made of stars that have already drifted
away. Open clusters slowly come apart, and the Pleiades is no exception.

And the blue haze in photographs? It isn't left over from the cluster's birth. It's an
unrelated cloud of dust that the cluster happens to be passing through. More on that below.

## How Far Away?

How far away is the Pleiades cluster? For most of the twentieth century the answer came from
main-sequence fitting: compare the cluster's ordinary stars with nearby stars whose distances
are known, and slide one set against the other until they line up (we covered how that works
in [Building the Ladder](/articles/2026/12/building-the-distance-ladder/)). 
That put the cluster about 132 parsecs away.

Then, in the late 1990s, results from the Hipparcos satellite came in. Hipparcos measured the
parallaxes of dozens of Pleiades stars directly, the most straightforward distance method
there is, and the answer was about 118 parsecs: some 10% closer. Ten percent doesn't sound
like much. But if the cluster were that close, its stars would be about 0.2 magnitudes fainter
than similar stars near the Sun. Either the Pleiades had an unusual chemical makeup, or
something about young stars wasn't understood.

So astronomers measured it again, every way they could: ground-based parallaxes, the orbit of
the double star Atlas, an eclipsing binary, and the Hubble Space Telescope. Every result came
out between 131 and 135 parsecs. A fresh reduction of the Hipparcos data in 2009 still said
120, and the Hipparcos team stood by its number. The question was finally settled in 2014 by
radio telescopes spread around the world and working as a single instrument (VLBI). They
measured 136.2 parsecs, and the Gaia spacecraft has since agreed.

What went wrong? The leading explanation is that Hipparcos's small errors weren't independent
for stars close together on the sky. The satellite's field of view was only about a degree
across, and stars that shared it could share the same small error. Ordinarily, averaging many
stars makes errors cancel. But the Pleiades packs dozens of measured stars into that one small
patch, so averaging them just repeated the error. A reanalysis that allowed for this came out
at 129 parsecs, much closer to everyone else. The Hipparcos team never accepted that
explanation, and the lesson is an old one: even the most direct measurement deserves a second,
independent check.

The cluster is moving, too. Gaia measures it drifting across our sky by about 0.05 arcseconds
a year, which works out to the width of the full Moon every 36,000 years or so, while it
recedes from us at about 6 kilometers per second. All of its members share that motion;
that's how Gaia picks them out from the background stars.

The blue nebula doesn't share it. The dust cloud is moving through the cluster at about 11
kilometers per second relative to the stars, an unrelated visitor that happens to be passing
by.

## How Old?

The Pleiades is young, but exactly how young depends on which clock you read.

The traditional clock uses the brightest stars. Massive stars burn through their hydrogen
fastest, so the most massive stars still in their prime tell you how long the cluster has been
around. Read that way with the stellar models of 1981, the Pleiades came out about 78 million
years old. You can see that clock running: Alcyone, Atlas, Electra and Maia are all classed as
giants, the cluster's heaviest stars already beginning to swell as they run short of hydrogen
in their cores.

A second clock reads the faintest members instead. Young stars contain lithium, and lithium is
destroyed once a star's core reaches about 3 million degrees. Low-mass stars take longer to
get that hot, so as a cluster ages, the lithium disappears from lower and lower masses. Find
the faintest stars that have lost it, and you've read the clock. In 1998, spectra of the
Pleiades' faintest members put that boundary at an age of about 125 million years.

Why the difference? The first clock depends on how much the cores of massive stars mix with
their surroundings, which is hard to model. Models that allow more mixing give older turnoff
ages, closer to the lithium answer. The lithium clock rests on simpler physics, which is why we
use 125 million years here.

The lithium clock has a bonus. At 125 million years, the boundary falls at about 0.075 solar
masses, the dividing line between stars and brown dwarfs. Every Pleiades member fainter than
that boundary is a brown dwarf: an object that never gets hot enough inside to sustain
hydrogen fusion. The same family that gives us six bright stars also includes members far too
faint ever to see.

Either way, the dinosaurs were here first. At 125 million years, the Pleiades formed in the
Early Cretaceous, the time of the feathered dinosaurs preserved in the volcanic lake beds of
northeastern China's Yixian Formation. At 78 million years, it would be a Late Cretaceous
cluster instead. The age dispute doesn't change whether the cluster is younger than the
dinosaurs. It changes which dinosaurs were alive when it formed. Not that they could have watched it
happen: 125 million years ago the Sun and the Pleiades were in different parts of the Galaxy,
nowhere near each other. Everything in the Milky Way is in orbit, and the neighbors we see
today are only passing through.

## What You're Looking At

The named stars come from Greek myth: the seven sisters (Alcyone, Asterope, Celaeno, Electra,
Maia, Merope and Taygeta) and their parents, Atlas and Pleione. Alcyone is the brightest. Atlas
and Pleione sit together at the eastern end of the dipper, close enough that Pleione is easy
to lose. That turns out to matter.

The first person to look deeper was Galileo. When he turned his new telescope on the Pleiades
in 1610, he found "more than forty others invisible to the naked eye" around the familiar six,
and drew thirty-six of them in his *Sidereus Nuncius*. The first telescope ever pointed at the
cluster changed the answer to "how many stars are there?" Gaia is still changing it.

{{< nbas-image src="pleiades-3.webp" width="350" align="right"
caption="Galileo's drawing of the Pleiades from Sidereus Nuncius (1610). He drew the stars he could see by eye differently from the new ones his telescope revealed.  Credit: Bibliothèque nationale de France, Public domain, via Wikimedia Commons"
alt="Page from Galileo's Sidereus Nuncius headed Pleiadum Constellatio, showing about three dozen stars drawn as small star symbols, the brighter naked-eye stars drawn larger and outlined." >}}

Charles Messier added the cluster to his catalogue as number 45 on March 4, 1769, giving the
position of Alcyone. Why a comet hunter would list an object nobody could mistake for a comet
is an open question; one suggestion is that he simply wanted a longer list than his rival
Lacaille, whose catalogue had 42 entries.

### The Nebulosity

The blue glow in photographs is a reflection nebula: dust lit by the cluster's hot stars,
scattering their blue light toward us. Its brightest part, the Merope Nebula, has a history
that every observer will appreciate.

Wilhelm Tempel found it on October 19, 1859, from Venice, with a 4-inch refractor of his own.
Other astronomers, with much larger telescopes, looked and saw nothing. Some decided the
nebula must be variable. Arthur Auwers had a better explanation: "large, blurred, faint
objects are much more easily visible in small instruments than in large ones." A big
telescope's narrow field was completely filled by the nebula, so there was no dark sky around
it to compare against. It took photography in the 1880s to settle the matter. The nebula was
real, and it doesn't vary.

There's a smaller secret too. In 1890, E.E. Barnard, using the 36-inch refractor at Lick
Observatory, spotted a tiny bright knot just 36 arcseconds from Merope, now cataloged as
IC 349. It's brighter per unit area than the rest of the nebula, but it hides in Merope's
glare, and visual sightings are rare.

As for where the dust came from: it isn't the cloud the cluster formed in. It's an unrelated
cloud the Pleiades happens to be passing through right now, at about 11 kilometers per second.
(For more on clusters and the clouds they meet, 
see [A Tour of the Milky Way Galaxy](/articles/2027/01/a-tour-of-the-milky-way-galaxy/)).

## Finding and Observing It

Finding the Pleiades is easy: on a January evening, look high in the south, northwest of the
orange star Aldebaran, for a tiny dipper-shaped cluster. Observing it well is mostly about
choosing the right field of view.

While you're out there, keep an eye on the Moon. Because of the way the Moon's orbit slowly
wobbles, it can only pass in front of the Pleiades during certain stretches of years. We're in
one now: from September 2023 through July 2029 the Moon crosses the cluster every month, as
seen from somewhere on Earth. After that the next series won't begin until 2042. Most of these
events happen in daylight or below the horizon for any one location, so check our
Astronomical Events calendar for the ones you can see. Through 2027 the Moon's path creeps
steadily deeper into the cluster, its closest approach shrinking from about a degree in January
to about half a degree by late in the year.

### Naked Eye

Back to the puzzle. Why six?

Rank the cluster's stars by brightness and the answer jumps out. Six stars, from Alcyone at
magnitude 2.9 down to Taygeta at 4.3, form a tight group. Then there's a gap. The seventh
brightest, Pleione, is at magnitude 5.0, and after it come four more stars between magnitudes
5.4 and 5.8.

So under a sky that shows stars to about fifth magnitude, you see
exactly six. A darker sky doesn't add a seventh; it adds several at once. That's why people
report six, or nine, or a dozen, but rarely seven.

And Pleione makes things harder. It sits just 5 arcminutes from Atlas, which is nearly four times
as bright, so it tends to merge with its brighter neighbor. Pleione also varies. It's a rapidly
spinning star that throws off a disk of gas from time to time, and as that disk forms,
changes and dissipates over a cycle of about 34 years, Pleione's brightness wanders between
magnitudes 4.8 and 5.5. Aratus said only six could be seen. Galileo, nineteen centuries
later, called the seventh "scarcely ever visible." Neither of them was imagining things.

How many can you count? Try it on a dark night, then try again from a brighter location.
The difference is a rough measure of your sky and limiting magnitude.

If you own one optical instrument for the Pleiades, make it binoculars. The cluster is about
1.8 degrees across, nearly four times the width of the full Moon. A pair of 7×35
binoculars shows about 9 degrees of sky and a 10×50 pair about 6.5 (figures for Nikon's
Aculon A211 models; other models differ somewhat). So the whole cluster sits in the middle of the
field, surrounded by dark sky, and the six stars become dozens. (The binocular and eyepiece
views in this section were rendered with Stellarium.[^stellarium])

In a telescope, keep the power low. An 80 mm refractor with a 25 mm eyepiece (16×) shows about
3.3 degrees, enough to hold the whole cluster with a little room around it; a 17 mm eyepiece
(23×) shows about 2.2 degrees, and the cluster nearly fills it. Go much higher and you're
looking at a few stars at a time, which is a fine way to explore but not a way to see the
Pleiades.

The nebulosity is the telescope's real challenge, and Tempel's lesson still applies: low power
and a wide field. The Merope Nebula can be seen in a 6-inch telescope at 25× to 100×, with
Merope itself placed just outside the field so its glare doesn't swamp the view. IC 349 is
another matter entirely; visual reports of it are almost nonexistent.

### Smart Scopes

{{< nbas-image src="m_45-s30-69m.webp" width="420" align="right"
caption="Bob Donahue, NBAS, Seestar S30, 69 min"
alt="Color image of the Pleiades showing the bright blue-white stars of the cluster wrapped in blue reflection nebulosity, with fainter streaks of dust and a brighter fan of nebulosity at the bottom." >}}

For a smart scope, the question is whether the cluster fits. A Seestar S30 frames about 2.4 ×
1.2 degrees, enough for the whole cluster. A Seestar S50 frames 1.29 × 0.73 degrees: wide
enough for all nine named stars, as in the labeled image above, but not the outer members.
Narrower instruments, such as the eQuinox 2, capture only part of the core in a single frame.
There's also field rotation. Most smart scopes use alt-az mounts, so over a long session the
field slowly turns, and a cluster that just fits the frame at the start gets its corners
clipped in the final stack. Leave some margin, or use an equatorial mount if yours supports
one. If your smart scope can build mosaics, that helps too.

This is where a smart scope earns its keep. The dust that eluded the 19th century's best
telescopes shows up in minutes, streaks and all.

## Wrapup

The Pleiades rewards every kind of looking. By eye, it's the same small cluster Aratus
described and Galileo puzzled over, and the same six-or-seven question is waiting for you.
With binoculars, the six become dozens. In a telescope or a smart scope, the dust appears. And
behind all of it is a family of more than a thousand stars, measured to within a percent of
its distance, younger than the dinosaurs, and slowly drifting apart.

You're looking at the same object as everyone who ever named it. You just know a little more
about what you're seeing.
