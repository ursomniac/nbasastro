Jupiter is back. For the past few months it has been a late-night and early-morning planet,
but by the second week of January it clears the eastern horizon around 7:30 PM, and it rises
earlier every night. By early February it's up before the end of twilight, and on the evening
of **February 10** it reaches *opposition*: Earth passes between Jupiter and the Sun, and
Jupiter is up all night, at its biggest and brightest for the year.

This time it's in Leo, a little west of the Lion's brightest star, Regulus. And it has company:
Mars is also in Leo, following about three weeks behind, with its own opposition on February 19.
That's a story for another Planet Watch.

## Why This Apparition Is Special

Jupiter's four big moons orbit almost exactly over its equator, and Jupiter's axis is tilted
only about 3° to its orbit. So most of the time, as seen from the Sun or from Earth, the moons'
orbits are slightly tilted ellipses, and the moons pass above or below each other.

But twice in each 12-year trip around the Sun, at Jupiter's *equinoxes*, the Sun and the Earth
both line up with the plane of those orbits. Then we're looking at the moons' orbits edge-on,
and the moons start passing directly in front of each other (*occultations*) and through each
other's shadows (*eclipses*). Jupiter's equinox fell on December 16, 2026, and these "mutual
events" run from May 2026 through about August 2027. The best eclipse season, though, is
already winding down: most of them fall between October and January. If you want to catch
one, January is the time.

{{< nbas-image src="fig-jupiter-mutual-geometry.webp" width="500" align="right"
caption="Why the moons line up now. Near Jupiter's equinox we see their orbits edge-on, so they can pass in front of each other or through each other's shadows."
alt="Two diagrams of Jupiter's four big moons. Top: their orbits seen slightly tilted, as nested ellipses, with the moons passing above or below one another. Bottom: the orbits seen edge-on as a single line, with one moon passing in front of another and one moon falling into another's shadow." >}}

When one moon passes through another's shadow, the eclipsed moon fades over a few minutes and
then recovers. The deepest one in our window comes on the very evening this article goes up:
at 9:09 PM on Sunday, January 10, Io's shadow falls across Ganymede for about seven minutes,
and the two moons' combined light drops by almost a full magnitude. The catch is that Jupiter
will be only about 18° up in the east, so you'll need a clear eastern horizon. A week later
there's an easier one, with Jupiter much higher.

## Observing

### Naked Eye

You can't miss it. At about magnitude −2.5 at opposition, Jupiter outshines every star in the
sky, including Sirius. In mid-January look east in the early evening; by February it's well up
in the east-southeast as the sky gets dark. Unlike the twinkling stars around it, it shines
with a steady light.

### Binoculars

caption="Stellarium rendering of 7x35 binocular view"

Steady binoculars will show you the four Galilean moons: Io, Europa, Ganymede, and Callisto,
lined up like tiny stars on either side of the planet. Brace your arms on a car roof or a fence,
or use a tripod, because a shaky view hides them. Look again the next night and the pattern will
have changed. Io circles Jupiter in less than two days, so it can switch sides overnight.

Even a small telescope shows Jupiter's two dark equatorial belts, and on steady nights more
detail inside them. The Great Red Spot comes around every 10 hours or so, so it's on the side
facing us only part of the time. Our [solar system dashboard](/solar-system-dashboard/) lists
when it crosses the middle of the disk.

The moons put on a show too. When a moon crosses in front of Jupiter (a *transit*), it can be
surprisingly hard to see against the bright clouds. Its shadow is much easier: a small, sharp,
black dot. Watch how the shadow and the moon line up over these weeks. Before opposition the
shadow leads the moon across the disk; by opposition night they nearly coincide. On February 9,
Io and its shadow start across Jupiter within a minute of each other.

### Smart Scopes

{{< nbas-image src="jup-equ.webp" width="350" align="left"
caption="Bob Donahue, NBAS, eQuinox 2 image, single frame showing the Great Red Spot."
alt="Small, soft smart-scope image of Jupiter showing its dark cloud bands and the Great Red Spot." >}}
A smart scope can't show a transit as well as a sharp eyepiece view, but it can do something an
eye can't: measure. Take a run of short exposures through a mutual event and you can watch one
moon fade and recover. (This would be a great challenge to attempt.)

In general, smart scopes aren't suited to planets. You can get a somewhat crude image, either by
taking video or by adjusting the gain and exposure time to show either the bands on the disk or
the Galilean satellites, depending on how much control your smart scope's app gives you over the
camera.

## Closing

Jupiter will be with us in the evening sky through the spring. Opposition is the evening of
February 10, when it rises at sunset and stays up all night, about 45″ across and as close as it
gets this year: 652 million kilometers, or about 36 light-minutes. But don't wait for opposition
for the moons: the mutual-event season is the rare part, and it's fading fast.
