Look at the image above before you look at anything else in this article. The Crab Nebula is
the small knot in the middle -- and most of the frame is everything else. That is
the honest scale of the thing: a scrap of light in a field of stars, easy to pass over. Most
pictures of the Crab crop in tight to show off the filaments, and that's understandable. But this
view is closer to what the sky is actually doing, and it makes the next fact land harder: that
small knot is the wreckage of a star that people watched explode, a little under a thousand years
ago, and it is still flying apart. Hubble photographed it in 1999 and again in 2024, and when the
two images were released side by side last March, the filaments had visibly moved.

At the eyepiece, honestly, it doesn't look like much. Your first reaction might be "oh,
another grey blob." That reaction is the right place to start, because a grey blob is exactly what
the first people to find it saw -- and the story of how that blob turned into one of the most
important objects in the sky is the reason it's worth going back to.

## Discovery: A Comet That Wasn't

The English physician and amateur astronomer John Bevis came across it first, around 1731, with a
small refractor, and marked it as a nebula on the star atlas he was preparing. Charles Messier found
it on his own on August 28, 1758, while he was following the comet of that year. Through his
telescope it looked like a comet: a small, fuzzy patch with no stars in it. But it didn't move.

For a comet hunter that's a trap, and Messier decided to keep a list of such traps so that he --
and anyone else sweeping the sky for comets -- wouldn't fall into them again. This object became
the first entry, and Messier called it "the first of all that I have discovered." Every "M" number
you'll ever look up starts here. The catalog that amateurs now work through for fun began as a list
of things *not* to bother with.

## The Guest Star of 1054

Seven centuries before Bevis, astronomers at the Song dynasty court in China recorded something in
the same patch of sky. On July 4, 1054, they reported a "guest star" -- their term for a star that
appears where none had been before -- a short distance from the star we call Zeta Tauri, the tip of the
Bull's southern horn. It was bright enough to see in daylight for 23 days. It stayed visible at
night until April 1056: 653 days in all.

Japanese records mention it too, and they put its appearance a week or two *before* the Chinese
report, so "first recorded" is safer than "exploded on." Europe, oddly, has no convincing record at
all; why is still an open question. And you may have seen the "Supernova Pictograph" at Chaco Canyon
in New Mexico -- a star, a crescent Moon and a handprint, painted on a cliff below an Ancestral
Puebloan great house. It's a lovely idea, and the park signposts it, but careful reviews have not
been able to confirm that it records this event specifically.

For centuries nobody connected the guest star to the faint nebula. That link came in stages. In
1921, Knut Lundmark compiled the old Chinese guest-star records and noted in passing that this one
lay close to the nebula, without claiming they were the same thing. In 1928, Edwin Hubble proposed
that they were. In 1939, Nicholas Mayall argued that the guest star must have been a *supernova* --
a star destroying itself, far more powerful than an ordinary nova. And in 1942, Jan Oort and the
sinologist J.J.L. Duyvendak gathered the historical accounts that put the identification beyond
reasonable doubt. The Crab became the first object in the sky tied to a supernova that people had
actually seen.

What convinced Hubble in the first place wasn't the old records. It was the nebula itself, which
was getting bigger. That's the next part of the story -- but first, the name.

## Why "Crab"?

{{< nbas-image src="M1Rosse-1844.webp" width="300" align="right"
caption="Lord Rosse's 1844 drawing of M 1, made with his 36-inch reflector at Birr Castle (Philosophical Transactions, 1844). Wikimedia Commons."
alt="An old pencil-style drawing of a nebula with long filaments trailing from one end, vaguely like a crab's legs." >}}

In the early 1840s, William Parsons, the third Earl of Rosse, turned his 36-inch reflector at Birr
Castle in Ireland on M 1 and drew what he saw: a mottled body with filaments streaming away from it.
The drawing, published in 1844, looked a bit like a crab -- or at least enough like one for the name
to stick. A few years later, in 1848, he looked again with his new 72-inch telescope, the largest
in the world, and couldn't confirm the resemblance at all. It was too late. The name had already
caught on, and it's been the Crab ever since, through more than a century of photographs that look
nothing like a crustacean.

There's something reassuring in that drawing. Rosse was working at the absolute edge of what the
eye could do, with the biggest telescopes on Earth, and he still saw something different each time
he looked. Every observer who has squinted at M 1 and wondered whether that faint texture is real is
in good company.

## Watching It Grow

In 1921, Carl Lampland at Lowell Observatory compared photographs of the Crab taken years apart and
announced that parts of it had changed. The same year, John Duncan at Mount Wilson measured the
motions and found the nebula expanding -- its outer filaments drifting outward by up to about a
sixth of an arcsecond per year. That doesn't sound like much. But it does mean the nebula is young.
Run the motion backward, and all that material started from a single point only a few centuries
ago -- which is what led Hubble to the guest star of 1054.

Here's where it gets interesting. When you actually *do* these calculations -- take each filament's
present position and speed and trace it back -- the filaments don't meet in 1054. They meet
*later*.

Every measurement of the main body of the nebula comes out decades too late: Duncan's 1939 result
gave 1172, later studies 1140 and 1130, and the most recent ones about 1090 to 1105. The explosion
date is known from the records. So the only way to make the numbers work is if the filaments are
moving *faster now* than they used to. Something has been pushing them.

There's a neat confirmation. A faint plume of filaments sticking out of the north side of the
nebula -- the "jet" -- sits farther from the center than everything else. When astronomers ran the
jet's motion backward, it came out at 1055, give or take a couple of decades. The part of the
nebula that got the least push gives the right answer. 

The same measurements also gave the first real distance to the Crab. If you know how fast a
filament is crossing the sky (in arcseconds per year, from old and new photographs), and how fast
it's moving toward or away from us (in kilometers per second, from the Doppler shift in its light),
you can work out how far away it must be -- as long as you assume the nebula is about as deep as
it is wide. Duncan's 1939 attempt gave about 4,200 light-years. The Crab isn't a neat sphere,
though, so that method always carried real uncertainty; for decades the honest answer was "somewhere
between about 4,500 and 9,000 light-years." Today the Crab's distance comes from a direct
[parallax](/articles/2026/12/parallax/) of the pulsar at its center: just over half a
thousandth of an arcsecond, measured by both the Gaia spacecraft and radio telescopes, giving about
6,200 light-years.

That outward motion is something you can, in principle, catch yourself. At about 1,500 kilometers
per second and 6,200 light-years, the fastest filaments move about a sixth of an arcsecond a year --
roughly 3 arcseconds over 20 years. In the images in this article, that's two or three
pixels. You won't see it by glancing at two pictures, but carefully aligned and blinked, images
taken a decade or two apart do show it, and amateurs have done exactly that. If you image the Crab
this winter, keep the file.

## The Engine Inside

In 1942, while Mayall and Oort were settling the history, Walter Baade was studying the nebula
itself with the 100-inch telescope at Mount Wilson. He picked out one of two faint stars near the
center -- the "south preceding" star, about 16th magnitude -- as the likely remnant of the star that
exploded. Rudolph Minkowski took its spectrum and found it featureless, unlike any normal star.

Baade had reason to look. In 1934, he and Fritz Zwicky had made a startling suggestion: that a
supernova "represents the transition of an ordinary star into a neutron star" -- a collapsed core
only a dozen or so miles across, made almost entirely of neutrons. Nobody had any idea how to see such a
thing. It took radio astronomy to find it. In late 1968, David Staelin and Edward Reifenstein
picked up rapidly pulsing radio signals near the Crab, and a team at the Arecibo telescope 
pinned down the source and its period: one pulse every 33 milliseconds. On January 15, 1969, John
Cocke, Michael Disney and Donald Taylor, using a 36-inch telescope at Kitt Peak, saw the same star
flashing in visible light, thirty times a second. Baade's star was the pulsar.

A pulsar is a spinning neutron star sweeping a beam past us like a lighthouse. The Crab's spins
about 30 times a second, and it's slowing down -- its period grows by about 38 billionths of a
second every day. That slowdown is the point. The energy the pulsar loses as it spins down is enough
to power the entire nebula. It drives a wind of particles and magnetic field outward from the
center, and that wind is what has been pushing the filaments and speeding them up.

{{< clear >}}
{{< nbas-image src="c_33-s30-70m.webp" width="400" align="left"
caption="The Veil Nebula, also caused by a supernova that exploded as much as 20,000 years ago. Bob Donahue, NBAS, Seestar S30, 70 min"
alt="The Veil Nebula: arcs of thin, glowing filaments across a dense star field." >}}
It also explains why the Crab looks the way it does. Most supernova remnants that amateurs image --
the Veil Nebula in Cygnus is the classic example -- are shells: a ring of filaments with an empty
middle, the blast wave plowing into the gas around it. The Crab is *filled in*. Its center glows
because the pulsar keeps feeding it from the inside. Astronomers call this kind of object a pulsar
wind nebula, and the Crab is the textbook example.

## Where the Light Comes From

Look again at the color images. There are two different kinds of glow in them, and they come from
two completely different physical processes.

The filaments -- the tangled threads -- glow the way a neon sign does. They're
gas from the exploded star, energized until its atoms give off light at a few specific colors:
the deep red of hydrogen-alpha, nearby lines of nitrogen, the blue-green of ionized oxygen, and
others.

The smooth, bluish-white haze filling the middle is something else entirely. In the 1940s, Baade
and Minkowski could see that this part had no lines at all -- just a continuous spread of light
across every color -- and the only explanation on offer, extremely hot gas, didn't hold up. 
In 1953, the Soviet astrophysicist Iosif Shklovsky proposed that it came from electrons moving 
at nearly the speed of light, spiraling through magnetic fields: *synchrotron* light, the 
same thing physicists had seen in particle accelerators. That kind of light should be polarized, 
and in 1954 two Soviet astronomers, Vashakidze and Dombrovsky, found that it was. The Crab was 
the first place synchrotron light was confirmed on a cosmic scale. Those electrons, and the 
magnetic fields they spiral in, come from the pulsar's wind.

This is where filters come in, and the two images in this article show the difference. A
dual-band filter passes two narrow slices of the spectrum -- one around hydrogen-alpha (wide enough
to take in the nitrogen lines beside it), one around ionized oxygen -- and blocks most of the rest.
That lets nearly all of the filament light through while throwing away most of the smooth
synchrotron glow, which is spread across every color.

The image at the top of this article was taken through an Optolong L-eNhance, a filter of exactly this kind made for color
cameras: a 10-nanometer window at hydrogen-alpha and a 24-nanometer window covering hydrogen-beta
and ionized oxygen. Together those windows let through only about a tenth of a smooth glow's
visible light, which is why the filaments stand out so strongly in that image. The glow isn't gone,
though. The synchrotron light is bright enough that, after nearly four hours of exposure, what gets
through the filter still fills the center. The eQuinox 2 image below was taken with no filter at
all, so it records both kinds of light at full strength. The dual-band filter built into the
Seestar S50 works the same way as the L-eNhance, with slightly wider windows.

## Finding and Observing It

{{< nbas-image src="m_1-mikefraietta-crop.webp" fullwidth="true"
caption="Mike Fraietta, NBAS (see image details at top)"
alt="The Crab Nebula: a bright, whitish interior laced with fine filaments, fringed with orange-red and touches of teal, in a field of stars." >}}

M 1 sits just over a degree northwest of Zeta (&zeta;) Tauri (Tianguan), the third-magnitude star at the tip of the
Bull's southern horn. At the end of January it's ideally placed. From North Adams, by the time the
sky is fully dark (about 6:40 PM in late January), it's already more than 55 degrees up in the
east-southeast, and it crosses the meridian, due south and 69 degrees high, at around 8:50 PM. It
crosses about four minutes earlier each night after that.

In a small telescope at low power, it's an oval glow, brighter in the middle and fading at the
edges, with no stars in it -- Messier's comet that wasn't. More magnification and patience bring out
a mottled interior and ragged edges; some observers describe a faint "S" shape in the brighter
parts. The filaments themselves are a challenge even for large amateur telescopes, which reach for
them with averted vision on the steadiest nights. Some observers find a nebula filter helps; it's
worth trying both ways.

It's still a grey blob. But it's a grey blob you now know is less than a thousand years old,
expanding at 1,500 kilometers a second around a dead star spinning 30 times a second.

{{< clear >}}
### Binoculars?

This is a challenge rather than a promise.  Experienced observers report it as 
a "ghostly little smudge" in 7×50s under semi-rural skies, and as barely 
detectable, with averted vision, in 10×70s at a dark site. Use Zeta Tauri as your
pointer, brace the binoculars on something solid, and look about one degree to the northwest. 

### Smart Scopes

{{< nbas-image src="m_1-equ-31m.webp" width="350" align="right"
caption="Bob Donahue, NBAS, eQuinox 2, 31 min, no filter"
alt="The Crab Nebula as a small oval among scattered stars." >}}

The Crab is a good smart-scope target: bright, compact, and high in the sky all winter evening. Two
experiments are worth trying. If your scope has a dual-band or light-pollution filter, take one
stack with it and one without, and compare: the filtered image brings the filaments forward, the
unfiltered one shows more of the glow between them (see *Where the Light Comes From*). And keep your best image.
In ten or twenty years, shoot it again with the same scope, align the two, and blink between them.
The nebula will have grown by a few pixels -- you'll have measured the expansion of a supernova
remnant from your backyard.

## Wrapup

Song dynasty court astronomers, John Bevis, Charles Messier, Lord Rosse with his giant reflectors,
the plate-measurers of the 1920s, Baade at Mount Wilson, three astronomers at Kitt Peak catching a
star flashing thirty times a second -- they all looked at the same small patch of sky beside Zeta
Tauri, and each of them saw more than the last. The Crab was the first object tied to a supernova
people had actually witnessed, and its pulsar was the proof that Baade and Zwicky's 1934 guess was
right: some stars end as neutron stars.

When you find it this winter, it will probably look like a grey blob. But it's also a blob that's still exploding.
