Mercury has a reputation as the planet most people have never seen. It never strays far from
the Sun, so it spends most of its time lost in the glare, and when it does come out, it's low in
twilight for a couple of weeks and then gone again. Articles like this one usually say the same
thing: here it is, go catch it before it slips away.

We'll do that. Late January and early February 2027 is a good chance from New England, and the
details are below. But this time we'll also answer a question most people never think to ask:
why are some of Mercury's appearances so much better than others? The answer involves the
seasons, the shape of Mercury's orbit, and one surprise: Mercury's best showings aren't always
the ones where it gets farthest from the Sun.

---

## When and Where to Look

The window runs from about **January 22 to February 10**, with the best evenings from about
**January 26 through February 7**. Start looking about half an hour after sunset (sunset is
around 4:50 to 5:15 PM EST over this stretch), low in the **west-southwest**. Mercury will be
roughly a fist's width above the horizon at its best: about 10 to 11° from February 1 to 5.
Mercury is farthest from the Sun in our sky, at **greatest elongation**, on February 3.

You'll need a clear, low horizon in that direction. Trees, hills and buildings that don't
matter for most observing matter a lot here. Mercury is technically in Aquarius, but in bright
twilight you won't see Aquarius's faint stars; just look for the brightest point of light low in
the glow.

On **February 8**, a thin crescent Moon (only about 5% lit) sits roughly 9° above Mercury, a
nice guide and a lovely sight. The evening before, an even thinner crescent sits just below and
slightly to the right of Mercury, only about 6° up: a real challenge, even with binoculars (see **Binoculars**, below).

For real-time positions and brightness, see the Mercury panel on our
[Solar System Dashboard](/solar-system-dashboard/).

## Observing

### Naked Eye

{{< nbas-image src="rad-mercury.webp" width="300" align="right"
caption="A smartphone photo of Mercury just above the tree line, June 2026."
alt="Twilight sky over a dark line of treetops, with a single small bright point, Mercury, just above the trees." >}}

Mercury looks like a single, star-like point, often with a pinkish or orange cast. That color
isn't Mercury's (up close, Mercury is gray, as the MESSENGER image at the end of this article shows); it's the same thick layer of air
near the horizon that reddens the setting Sun. Because it's so low, it may twinkle and shimmer
more than a planet higher in the sky.

Mercury is brightest at the start of this apparition: about magnitude −1 in late January, still
−0.4 at greatest elongation on February 3, then +1 by February 10 and +2 by February 12. In late
January it's lower but brighter; in early February it's higher but fading. Either way, once
you've found it, you've joined a fairly small club.

If you have a night-sky app on your phone or tablet, try capturing Mercury against the horizon.
A phone camera can sometimes pull it out of the glare when your eyes can't.

### Binoculars

Binoculars make Mercury much easier to find in bright twilight. Sweep slowly along the horizon in
the west-southwest, then look up with your unaided eyes to see it.

**Wait until the Sun has fully set before you sweep with binoculars.** Mercury is close to the
Sun in the sky, and catching the Sun in binoculars, even for a moment, can permanently damage
your eyes.

{{< nbas-image src="mer-moon-070227-2300-7x35.webp" width="400" align="right"
caption="Mercury and the very young Moon as they might look in 7×35 binoculars on February 7, 2027, at 6:00 PM EST (23:00 UT). Rendered in Stellarium."
alt="Simulated circular binocular view of a twilight sky: Mercury as a bright point, with a hair-thin crescent Moon about three and a half degrees below and slightly to its right, near the horizon." >}}

A particularly difficult challenge comes on the evening of **February 7**: an extremely young
Moon, only about a day and a quarter past New Moon, sits less than 3½° from Mercury, close enough
to put both in the same binocular field. The Moon is sinking into the horizon haze the whole
time, so timing matters:

{{< clear >}}

| Time (EST) | Sun | Mercury's height | Moon's height |
|------------|-----|------------------|---------------|
| 5:30 PM | 3.7° below | 12° | 9° |
| 5:40 PM | 5.5° below | 10½° | 7° |
| 5:50 PM | 7.2° below | 9° | 5° |
| 6:00 PM | 9.0° below | 7° | 4° |
| 6:15 PM | 11.7° below | 4° | 1° |

The best chance is about 5:45 to 6:00 PM, once the sky has darkened a little but before the Moon
gets too low. Find Mercury first, then look about 3½° below it and slightly to the right. With
a clear, flat horizon, some luck and a steady tripod, you might see the Moon. (This is also a good
challenge for phone and tablet photographers!)

### Telescopes

Why does Mercury fade while it climbs? Its **phase**. Mercury starts this apparition on the far
side of the Sun, mostly lit, and swings toward us. As it does, we see less and less of its sunlit
side. Over these three weeks its disk nearly doubles in size, but the sunlit part shrinks faster
than the disk grows.

{{< nbas-image src="fig-mercury-phases.webp" fullwidth="true"
caption="Mercury's phase and size through the apparition, drawn to scale. As Mercury swings toward Earth it grows from 5 to nearly 10 arcseconds across, but the lit part shrinks from 93% to 10%, and the planet fades by about three magnitudes. Sunlit side toward the Sun; a telescope may flip or rotate the view."
alt="Five disks of Mercury in a row, growing larger from left to right. The first is almost fully lit, the second gibbous, the middle one half lit, and the last two are thin crescents on large, mostly dark disks. Labels give the date, percent lit, size in arcseconds and magnitude." >}}

In late January Mercury is a small gibbous disk. At greatest elongation it's about 0.94 AU from
Earth and only about 7 arcseconds across, half lit, like a tiny first-quarter Moon. By February
10 it's a crescent nearly 9 arcseconds across but only 18% lit. Seeing the phase is a satisfying
thing to do, but don't expect surface detail: the air near the horizon churns, and the image
will shimmer. Even at 100×, Mercury's 7-arcsecond disk looks only about a third the size of the
full Moon to your unaided eye, seen through the thick, churning air near the horizon. The phase
can be glimpsed in moments of steady air; the drawing above shows what you're trying to catch.
Use moderate power and catch it as early in twilight as you can, while it's
highest. And as with binoculars, don't point the telescope west until the Sun is down.

## Why Some Elongations Are Better Than Others

Because Mercury's orbit lies inside ours, we always see it near the Sun, swinging back and forth
from one side to the other. Astronomers measure how far it gets as an angle, the **elongation**:
the angle between Mercury and the Sun as seen from Earth. When Mercury reaches its farthest point
east of the Sun, at **greatest eastern elongation**, it's an evening object, setting after the
Sun. At **greatest western elongation** it's a morning object, rising before the Sun.

This might seem backwards: at its _eastern_ elongation, we look for Mercury after sunset, in the
_west_. But "east" describes where Mercury is relative to the Sun, not where it is in our sky.
Mercury sits east of the Sun along the ecliptic, so as the sky turns westward through the
evening, the Sun sets first and Mercury follows it down.

Mercury reaches greatest elongation about six times a year, alternating between evening and
morning. Here are all six for 2027, as seen from North Adams:

| Greatest elongation | Sky | Angle from the Sun | Mercury's height when the Sun is 6° down |
|---------------------|-----|--------------------|------------------------------------------|
| February 3 | Evening | 18° | 11° |
| March 17 | Morning | 28° | 4½° |
| May 28 | Evening | 23° | 12° |
| July 15 | Morning | 21° | 8° |
| September 25 | Evening | 26° | 2° |
| November 4 | Morning | 19° | 12° |

Look at September. Mercury gets farther from the Sun than at any other evening elongation this
year, and yet it barely clears the horizon. February, the smallest evening elongation of the
year, is one of the best. The mornings show the same puzzle in reverse: March has the biggest
angle of the whole year and one of the worst views, while November's small angle is the best
morning of the year. How is that possible? Three things are going on.

### The season matters: how steeply the ecliptic meets the horizon

The Sun, Moon and planets all travel along roughly the same line across the sky, the
**ecliptic**, and Mercury never strays far from it. After sunset, Mercury sits somewhere along
the ecliptic above the place where the Sun went down. So what matters is the angle at which the
ecliptic leaves the horizon.

One line in the sky meets the western horizon at the same angle every evening of the year: the
**celestial equator**, the projection of Earth's equator onto the sky. From our latitude, 42.7°
north, it slants up at 90° − 42.7°, about 47°. The ecliptic is tilted 23½° to the equator, and
the two cross at two points: the March equinox point in Pisces and the September equinox point
in Virgo. Which way the ecliptic heads as it climbs out of the western horizon depends on where
the Sun is along it, and that depends on the season.

On February 3, the Sun sets in Capricornus, south of the equator. Climbing east from the Sun, the
ecliptic runs up through Aquarius (where Mercury is), crosses the equator in Pisces, about 35°
up, and carries on north into Aries. Because it heads north as it climbs, it stands steeper than
the equator. On September 25, the Sun has just passed the September equinox point in Virgo, and
the ecliptic heads the other way: south, through Libra, Scorpius and Sagittarius. It stands
flatter than the equator and arcs low across the southern sky.

{{< nbas-image src="fig-mercury-ecliptic-seasons.webp" fullwidth="true"

In September the ecliptic runs low through Libra, Scorpius and Sagittarius, below the equator, with Mercury only 2 degrees high." >}}

From our latitude, the evening angle swings from about 70° (47° plus 23½°) on March evenings to
only about 24° (47° minus 23½°) on September evenings. It's the same reason the evening crescent
Moon stands tall in spring and lies low in autumn.

Mornings are the mirror image. Before dawn, the ecliptic stands steeply out of the eastern
horizon in late summer and fall, and lies low in spring. That's why March's big 28° morning
elongation lifts Mercury only about 4½°, while November's 19° lifts it 12°.

The season comes from where *Earth* is in its orbit. The next two factors come from Mercury.

### Mercury's eccentric orbit

Mercury's orbit is the most stretched-out of any planet's. Its distance from the Sun swings
between about 0.31 and 0.47 AU (1 AU is Earth's distance from the Sun). When Mercury reaches
greatest elongation near the close end of its orbit, it simply can't get very far from the Sun
in our sky. Near the far end, it can.

That's exactly what happens in 2027. Mercury passes perihelion, its closest point to the Sun, on
February 6, just three days after this elongation, so the angle tops out at 18°. In September
it's near aphelion, its farthest point, and the angle opens to 26°.

{{< nbas-image src="fig-mercury-orbit-elongations.webp" fullwidth="true"
caption="Why Mercury's greatest elongation changes. Seen from above, with Earth at the bottom and distances to scale: on February 3, 2027 Mercury is near perihelion, only 0.31 AU from the Sun, so from Earth it never gets more than 18° from the Sun. On September 25 it is near aphelion, 0.45 AU out, and the angle opens to 26°. (Positions computed for 2027.)"
alt="Two top-down diagrams of Mercury's slightly oval orbit around the Sun, with Earth below. In the February panel Mercury is near the orbit's closest point to the Sun and the angle between the Sun and Mercury, seen from Earth, is 18 degrees; in the September panel Mercury is near the far point and the angle is 26 degrees." >}}

So the two factors pull in opposite directions this year. February gets a small angle but a
steep ecliptic; September gets a big angle but a flat one. The season wins.

### Mercury's tilt

The third factor is smaller. Mercury's orbit is tilted about 7° to Earth's, so Mercury doesn't
sit exactly on the ecliptic. In the three-panel figure above, it's a couple of degrees above the
line in May (helping a little) and a few degrees below it in September (making a bad evening
worse).

### And one bonus: brightness

Being near perihelion has an upside. In February Mercury is about a third closer to the Sun than
it is in September, so its surface gets roughly twice as much sunlight, and at the same phase it
looks brighter for it.

Mercury is getting new attention. The European–Japanese BepiColombo mission was due to enter
orbit around Mercury on November 21, 2026, with its two orbiters separating in December and
science operations planned to begin in April 2027. It will be the first spacecraft to study
Mercury from orbit since MESSENGER's mission ended in 2015.

From the ground, the planet hasn't changed: a small, bright point low in the twilight, there for
a couple of weeks and then gone. But now you know why this apparition is a good one when a
bigger elongation in September will be a poor one, and why Mercury is brightest before it's
highest. The same reasoning works for Venus too, except that Venus's orbit is so nearly circular
that only the season matters.
