## Introduction

{{< nbas-image src="banner.webp" fullwidth="true" 
caption="Locating IC 342 in Camelopardalis" >}}

{{< object-specs align="right" >}}

IC 342 should be one of the showpieces of the winter sky. It's a big spiral galaxy, about 11
million light-years away, which is close by galaxy standards. We see it almost exactly face-on,
and it spans about 20′ of sky, two-thirds the width of the full Moon. ESA's Hubble team put it
plainly: it "would be among the brightest in the sky were it not for its dust-obscured location."

That location is the problem. IC 342 lies only about 10° from the plane of the Milky Way, so
we have to look at it through the dusty disk of our own galaxy. The dust dims it by roughly a
factor of five, reddens what light does get through, and scatters thousands of foreground
stars across the view. That's where the nickname comes from: the Hidden Galaxy.

## A Galaxy Nobody Could Place

William Frederick Denning, an English amateur, came across IC 342 in August 1892 while sweeping
for comets. What he saw was only the bright central core, a small fuzzy spot. The faint spiral
arms around it don't show at the eyepiece unless the sky is very dark. That's how it entered the
Index Catalogue.

It took until 1934 for Edwin Hubble and Milton Humason to recognize it as a spiral galaxy.
Harlow Shapley and Carl Seyfert followed up a year later and blamed the faintness of the arms on
dust in our own galaxy. Shapley even counted IC 342 as a member of the Local Group, our galaxy's
immediate family. It isn't. But that dust made its distance hard to pin down for decades:
when you can't tell how much light the dust is stealing, you can't tell how far away something is.

The answer came from Hubble Space Telescope observations in near-infrared light, which passes
through dust far better than visible light. Measuring the brightest red giant stars in IC 342
gave a distance of about 11 million light-years. IC 342 turns out to lead a group of its own,
the IC 342/Maffei group, one of the nearest galaxy groups to ours. Its neighbors Maffei 1 and
Maffei 2 sit even deeper in the Milky Way's dust and weren't discovered until 1968.

## What You're Looking At

{{< nbas-image src="c_5-equ-33m.webp" width="420" align="right"
caption="IC 342 from northern Vermont, 8 April 2024: Unistellar eQuinox 2, 33 minutes. Nearly every star here is a Milky Way star in front of the galaxy. (The faint arcs in the outer field are processing artifacts.)"
alt="Face-on spiral galaxy IC 342 behind a dense field of foreground stars" >}}

Start with the stars. Almost every one of them belongs to our own galaxy, between us and IC 342.
They're the same thing that hides it: the Milky Way's disk, seen edge-on.

Behind them is the galaxy itself. The bright center is a busy star-forming core, confirmed in
2003 as an "H II nucleus," a region of glowing hydrogen gas where new, hot stars are forming.
Around it, patchy spiral arms wind outward, broken up by dark lanes of IC 342's own dust. The
arms show up in a smart-scope image far better than at an eyepiece. At this distance the visible
disk is something like 50,000 to 70,000 light-years across (estimates vary), a little smaller
than our own Milky Way.

## Finding and Observing It

Camelopardalis, the Giraffe, is big and faint, without a bright star to its name, so it helps to
navigate from its neighbors. Start at Mirfak, the brightest star in Perseus, and head toward
Polaris. IC 342 lies almost exactly on that line, a little less than halfway: about 18° from
Mirfak and 21° from Polaris. The nearest guide star is 4.6-magnitude γ Camelopardalis, about 3°
farther toward Polaris.

From our latitude, IC 342 is circumpolar: it never sets. It stands highest, about 65° up, on
January evenings around 8 or 9 PM, and even at its lowest it's still about 20° above the
northern horizon.

Visually, it's a test of dark skies. Use low power and a wide field, give your eyes time to
adapt, and use averted vision. Most observers see the brighter core first, with a faint glow
around it perhaps 10′ to 15′ across. Picking out the arms takes a large aperture and a truly
dark sky.

### Why a "Bright" Galaxy Can Be Hard to See

A galaxy's catalog magnitude, its *integrated* magnitude, measures all of its light added up, as
if it were squeezed into a single point like a star. That's a fair description of a star. It's a
poor guide to a galaxy, because a galaxy spreads that light over an area of sky, and what your
eye actually responds to is how bright each patch is. That's *surface brightness*: light per
unit area, usually given in magnitudes per square arcminute or per square arcsecond. Like
ordinary magnitudes, bigger numbers mean fainter.

alt="Three panels show the same total light as a sharp point, a bright small disk, and a dim large glow. Below, a chart places M 33 at 14.72, M 31 at 14.74, M 101 at 15.08 and IC 342 at 15.96 magnitudes per square arcminute; IC 342 is the faintest per area." >}}

Spread the same total light over twice the area and every patch gets half as bright. That's why
two galaxies with similar catalog magnitudes can look completely different at the eyepiece, and
why a big, nearby galaxy like IC 342 can be harder to see than a smaller, more distant one.

By this measure, IC 342 is the faintest of these four big spirals: about 1.2 magnitudes per
square arcminute fainter than M 31 and M 33, which means each patch of it is roughly three times
dimmer. It's also about twice as dim per patch as M 101, which many observers already find
challenging. Some of that is the galaxy itself, and some is our own galaxy's dust, which steals
blue light more than red.

It helps to think in photons. Every second, a fixed number of photons from the galaxy enters
your telescope, and the aperture sets that number: a bigger telescope catches more.
Magnification doesn't add a single one. It only decides how large a patch of your retina those
photons get spread across. Low power packs them into a small image, so each part of it is
brighter. High power spreads the same photons over a bigger image, so each part is dimmer.

What magnification can't do is lift the galaxy above the sky. The sky's photons are packed or
spread in exactly the same proportion, so the galaxy's contrast against the background is the
same at any power. What low power buys you is a brighter image overall, and a dark-adapted eye
picks out a faint smudge more easily in a brighter view. There's a limit, though. Once the beam
leaving the eyepiece (the *exit pupil*) is wider than your own dark-adapted pupil, some of the
light misses your eye altogether. So the lowest useful power is the one whose exit pupil matches
your pupil: about 7 mm for young eyes, less as we age. For faint, large galaxies, aperture, low
power, and dark skies are what work.

foreground star field. Because it never strays far from north, it's also a target where an
alt-azimuth smart scope's field rotation stays high all night. Leave room around the galaxy for
cropping the edges of the stack.

## Wrapup

IC 342 is a reminder that we look at the universe from inside something. The light from this
galaxy traveled 11 million years to reach us, and the very last stretch, crossing our own
galaxy's dust, caused the greatest interference. When you find it, you're seeing two galaxies at
once: a big spiral in the background, and our own neighborhood in front of it.
