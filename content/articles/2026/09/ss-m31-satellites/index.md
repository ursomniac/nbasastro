## Introduction

{{< nbas-image src="hero.webp" fullwidth="true" caption="The Andromeda field -- M 31, M 32, M 110, NGC 147/185, and IC 10 -- generated in Stellarium." >}}

On a dark, clear Fall night, the Andromeda Galaxy is probably the most distant
object most people will ever see with their own eyes -- a smudge of
light roughly 2.5 million light-years away, more than twenty times
farther than anything else visible without help.  That's already a
strange thing to hold in your head while looking at what appears to
be a faint, unremarkable oval of light.

And that's really the first surprise about M 31: visually, it can be a bit
of a letdown. Through binoculars or a modest telescope, you mostly
get a soft glow with a brighter core -- none of the spiral structure
that makes it famous in photographs. To _really_ see this galaxy, you
need long exposures, which is exactly where a smart scope earns its keep. 

But M 31 isn't traveling alone. Like the Milky Way, it has a
retinue of smaller companion galaxies -- some bright enough to
observe directly, some so faint they're only reachable through
imaging, and one whose membership in the family is still debated.
This _A Wider View_ article is about that whole household: who's really related to
whom, what its structure tells us, and how it stacks up against our own Milky Way.

## History

{{< nbas-image src="rosse-m_31.webp" width="295" align="right" caption="Drawing of M 31 by Lord Rosse" >}}
{{< nbas-image src="Al-Sufi.webp" width="200" align="right" caption="By Albrecht Dürer CC BY-SA 4.0" >}}

Andromeda has been watched for a very long time. The Persian
astronomer Abd al-Rahman al-Sufi recorded it in his *Book of Fixed
Stars* around 964 AD, describing it simply as "a little cloud" --
the earliest known written mention of the object, though it's probably
been noticed for millennia. For nearly a thousand years after that 
it stayed a curiosity: one of many faint, fuzzy "spiral nebulae" catalogued 
by 18th- and 19th-century observers, with no way to tell whether these 
were nearby clouds of gas inside our own galaxy or something else entirely.

{{< nbas-image src="edwin-hubble.webp" width="150" align="left" caption="Edwin Hubble (1889-1953" >}}

That question -- whether these "nebulae" were part of the Milky Way
or independent galaxies of their own -- was one of the biggest open
debates in astronomy through the early 20th century. It was settled
on October 5, 1923, when Edwin Hubble identified a Cepheid variable
star in M 31 at Mt. Wilson Observatory. Cepheids pulsate at a rate tied 
directly to their true brightness (a relationship worked out by 
Henrietta Leavitt a decade earlier), so timing the pulsation tells 
you the star's real luminosity -- and comparing that to how faint it 
looks from here gives you distance. Hubble's answer put M 31 roughly a million
light-years away (later revised up to today's 2.5 million), far
outside the Milky Way by any measure. Andromeda wasn't a nebula. It
was another galaxy entirely -- an "island universe," and proof that
ours wasn't the only one.

## Galaxies and Satellites

{{< nbas-image src="local-group.webp"
caption="The three main galaxies in the Local Group and their satellites.  By Andrew Z. Colvin - Own work, CC BY-SA 4.0" >}}

The Milky Way isn't a solitary galaxy either. We have our own
retinue of satellites -- the Magellanic Clouds most famously, plus a
crowd of much fainter dwarfs -- and we have our own history of
eating smaller neighbors. Gaia-Enceladus, a galaxy roughly the size
of a Magellanic Cloud, collided with and was absorbed by the Milky
Way about 10 billion years ago, adding an estimated 50 billion solar
masses of stars, gas, and dark matter, along with at least eight
globular clusters in the process. The Sagittarius Dwarf Galaxy is
still being consumed today, in slow motion: it's made at least three
close passes, roughly 5-6 billion, 2 billion, and 1 billion years
ago, getting stripped down a little more each time.

Andromeda has the same kind of story, just easier to see from the
outside. It has its own set of satellite galaxies -- more of them
than we currently know of around the Milky Way, though some of that
gap may simply be how thoroughly each system has been surveyed -- and 
its two brightest companions, M 32 and M 110, both show signs of having 
been shaped by past run-ins with M 31 itself. Big galaxies build up 
their mass this way: by accreting the small.

## The Head of the Household: Messier 31

{{< nbas-image src="m_31-s30-20251120-41m.webp" fullwidth="true" 
caption="Bob Donahue, NBAS,  Seestar 30, 41 min" >}}

At around 220,000 light-years across, M 31 is more than double the
Milky Way's diameter, and it's often quoted at roughly a trillion
stars to our galaxy's 200-400 billion -- though that stellar count is
a rough, frequently-repeated figure rather than a tightly-measured
one, so treat it as "clearly bigger," not as a precise ratio. Total
mass (stars, gas, dust, and the dark matter that dominates the
budget) is harder to pin down for both galaxies, but recent estimates
of the mass within 300,000 light-years put the Milky Way at around
0.9 trillion solar masses and M 31 at around 1.4 trillion -- Andromeda
is the more massive of the two, though not by the lopsided margin
its size alone might suggest.

## The Primary Satellites

### Messier 32 and 110

{{< nbas-gallery style="grid" size="300px" >}}
m_32-equ-20240903-16m.webp | M 32 | eQuinox 2, 16 min
m_110-equ-20240903-23m.webp | M 110 | eQuinox 2, 23 min
{{< /nbas-gallery >}}
{{< clear >}}

**M 32** (NGC 221) is a compact elliptical galaxy -- a rare, dense type
thought in many cases to be the stripped-down remnant core of a
larger galaxy that lost its outer layers to a close encounter. M 32
sitting essentially right against M 31's disk makes it a strong
candidate for exactly that kind of history, though the details are
still debated. Visually and photographically it's a fairly
featureless, bright, compact blob -- there isn't much structure to
chase here even with a smart scope. It's still small: roughly 5,800 ly
across, a fraction of M 31's own 220,000 ly span -- though a typical
wide-field photo makes the gap look smaller than it really is. M 32's
light is compact and bright enough to show up fully in one exposure,
while most of M 31's own faint, extended outer disk doesn't register
at the same exposure depth at all.

{{< nbas-image src="m_110-dust-annotated.webp" width="300" align="right" 
caption="Bob Donahue, NBAS, eQuinox 2, 23 min" >}}

**M 110** (NGC 205) is the more interesting of the pair. It's
classified as a dwarf elliptical, but it doesn't act like a typical
one: it has visible dust lanes, unusual for this class of galaxy,
and a population of young blue stars concentrated near its center --
stars that formed in bursts as recently as roughly 25 million years
ago. That's not something a "dead," gas-free elliptical should be
doing, and the leading explanation is that it's connected to a past
interaction with M 31 itself, possibly funneling in fresh gas. It's
also considerably bigger than M 32 despite the resemblance in a
quick look -- roughly 12,300 ly across -- which makes it genuinely
worth trying to capture with a long exposure. It's one of the few
objects in this family where a smart scope _might_ reveal something
that isn't visible at all in a quick look.

### Caldwell 17 (NGC 147) and 18 (NGC 185)

{{< nbas-gallery style="grid" size="300px" >}}

c_18-equ-20240904-32m.webp | NGC 185 (C 18) | eQuinox 2, 32 min
{{< /nbas-gallery >}}
{{< clear >}}

**NGC 147** and **NGC 185** are a second pair of dwarf
companions, sitting well off to the side, over in Cassiopeia, rather 
than tucked against M 31 like M 32 -- and they turn out to be a study 
in contrast with each other, not as a matched pair. NGC 147 is close 
to the dwarf elliptical "prototype": old stars, essentially no dust, 
nothing to chase beyond overall glow, a genuine patience game.  NGC 185 
is the exception -- much like M 110, it carries real, documented dust
clouds and a pocket of star formation confined to its central few
hundred parsecs, active as recently as roughly 100 million years
ago. That's the mottled, patchy darkening visible near its core
rather than a smooth glow -- a real feature of the galaxy, not a
foreground dark nebula or an imaging artifact. Why NGC 185 held onto
star-forming gas and dust while its near-twin NGC 147 apparently
didn't is still an open question.
NGC 185 is, in fact, the larger of the two -- roughly 7,600 ly across,
against NGC 147's roughly 6,300 ly -- so size isn't what set their
different histories apart either.

Even though they sit near each other in the sky, there's no evidence they share a past, 
or have ever had any encounters with each other -- which makes their
very different evolution (one dead and dust-free, one still holding
onto star-forming material) even more of an open question rather
than a shared-history explanation.

### IC 10

{{< nbas-image src="ic_10-equ-20240825-21m.webp" width="300" align="right"
caption="Bob Donahue, NBAS, eQuinox 2, 21 min" >}}

**IC 10** is the wild card of this family. It's a dwarf
irregular galaxy -- and specifically a *starburst* galaxy, the only
confirmed one in the entire Local Group, meaning it's forming stars
at an unusually high rate for its size. Whether it's actually a
gravitationally bound satellite of M 31, though, is genuinely an
open question. Based on distance and position it's typically grouped with the
"M 31 subgroup" (which also includes M 33), but nobody has
demonstrated it's actually bound in orbit around Andromeda the way
M 32, M 110, or NGC 147/185 are. The honest framing is: maybe a member, or
just a neighbor that happens to be nearby.

It's also a genuine observing and imaging challenge, and not because
it's faint -- it sits at a low galactic latitude (-3.3°), meaning we're
looking at it through a thicker slice of our own Milky Way's dust
and stars than most of the rest of this family, which dims and
reddens it. At roughly 5,100 ly across -- the smallest of this
primary group -- that starburst activity is spread across the whole
galaxy rather than concentrated the way M 110's young core is.

### Even Smaller Faint Satellites!

Beyond these five, M 31 has a whole population of much fainter dwarf
spheroidal satellites, cataloged with Roman numerals (Andromeda I,
II, III, and on up past XXX at this point). None of these are
"observing" targets in any traditional sense -- they're imaging-only,
and even then, some are a serious stretch.

And II is the brightest of the group by far, and probably the best
first target to attempt. One fun wrinkle: And IX looks like the
closest of the six to M 31 on the sky (2.7°), but it's actually one
of the more physically distant in 3D, at 182 kpc -- it just happens
to sit much nearer to us along our own line of sight, so a small
angular separation hides a real, much larger one.

{{< nbas-image src="and123.webp" 
caption="Locations of And I, II, and III" >}}

All six cluster within about 1.96 to 2.42 million light-years of
Earth, close to M 31's own 2.54 million -- exactly what you'd expect
if they're actually orbiting the same galaxy rather than just
sharing a direction in the sky.

It's also worth pausing on how much sky this "family" actually
covers. Just among the twelve objects in this article, M 31's
satellite system already reaches into Cassiopeia and Pisces, not
just the Andromeda constellation itself -- and the wider census goes
further still: Andromeda VI, for instance, sits over in Pegasus (it
even has its own name, the Pegasus Dwarf Spheroidal). Either way,
the underlying point holds: this is a genuinely nearby, physically
compact system, and the only reason it sprawls across so much of the
sky is that M 31 itself is close enough for a few hundred
kiloparsecs to translate into double-digit degrees.

## A Wider View

### Inferred Histories (and Futures)

For decades, the Milky Way and Andromeda were described as being on
a slow, near-certain collision course, expected to merge in about
4.5 billion years. New research published in June 2025 (Sawala et
al., *Nature Astronomy*) complicates that considerably. Running
thousands of simulations using updated Hubble and Gaia data -- and
critically, factoring in the gravitational pull of other Local Group
galaxies, especially M 33 and the Large Magellanic Cloud -- the study
found only about a 50% chance of a merger within the next 10 billion
years if at all, and just a 2% chance of it happening on the old
4-5-billion-year timeline.  The pull of other nearby galaxies has an effect:
M 33's gravity nudges the odds of a merger up; the LMC's orbit runs roughly 
perpendicular to the Milky Way-Andromeda approach and nudges them back down. 
If a merger does happen, the revised timing looks more like 7-8 billion years
from now.

No Certainty of a Milky Way-Andromeda Collision,\" Nature Astronomy 9, 1206-1217 -- reproduced from the arXiv preprint (arXiv:2408.00064), licensed CC BY 4.0." >}}

That's what this figure shows: the odds shift a lot depending on
which nearby galaxies get included, from 37% up to 63% across the
four scenarios tested. And even the runs that don't end in an
outright merger still bring the two galaxies past each other at some
real, close distance -- just an uncertain one, for the same reason.

Closer to home, M 31's two brightest satellites both carry
fingerprints of past interaction with the parent galaxy. M 32's
compact, stripped-down structure is widely suspected (though not
proven) to be what's left of a larger galaxy after a close pass
tore away its outer regions. M 110's young central stars point to a 
more recent and gentler nudge -- something that funneled fresh gas 
into an otherwise "quiet" dwarf elliptical and triggered a small 
burst of star formation as recently as ~25 million years ago.

### Atypical Distribution

{{< nbas-image src="kanehisa-et-al-2025-fig1.webp" width="450" align="right"
caption="M 31's satellite galaxies (circles = in the satellite plane, triangles = off it), color-coded by their angle from the Milky Way's direction as seen from Andromeda -- all but one fall within 107° of that direction. Kanehisa, K. J., Pawlowski, M. S., and Libeskind, N. 2025, \"Andromeda's asymmetric satellite system as a challenge to cold dark matter cosmology,\" Nature Astronomy 9, 692 -- reproduced from the arXiv preprint (arXiv:2504.08047), licensed CC BY 4.0." >}}

Here's a genuinely strange feature of M 31's satellite system: of
its 37 known satellites, all but one sit within 107° of the
direction toward the Milky Way -- overwhelmingly bunched onto our
"side" of Andromeda rather than spread evenly around it. A 2025 study
(Kanehisa, Pawlowski, and Libeskind, *Nature Astronomy*) checked this
against standard cosmological simulations and found that fewer than
0.3% of simulated satellite systems are ever this lopsided. Nobody
has a settled explanation for why.

It gets stranger still. A related 2013 study found that most of
M 31's satellites don't just favor one side -- 15 of 27 surveyed
satellites sit in a thin, corotating plane, all orbiting the same
direction, with roughly 1-in-800 odds of that happening by chance.

And it isn't only an Andromeda thing: the Milky Way has its own
version of this, the Vast Polar Structure, where our own brightest
satellites also cluster into a thin, corotating plane. Whatever is
producing this seems to be happening at both of the Local Group's
big spirals, not just the one we can see clearly from outside.

One caveat worth keeping for our own satellite count specifically:
the Milky Way's own disk blocks part of our view of the sky, so a
few of ours may simply be hiding behind it, undiscovered --
Andromeda doesn't have that same blind spot from our vantage point.

### Comparing Households

Andromeda currently has somewhere between 35 and 39 known dwarf
satellite galaxies. Within about 100 kiloparsecs the two systems
look similar; farther out, M 31 outnumbers the Milky Way by roughly
a factor of 2-4. That's not because the Milky Way has nothing out
there -- it has real, confirmed satellites well past 100 kpc too
(Fornax, Leo I, Leo II, and a couple of genuinely remote ultra-faint
dwarfs) -- current research suggests the Milky Way's own census that
far out is probably still incomplete, likely missing a number of
faint dwarfs that would close some of this gap. So this may be more
about what we haven't found yet than a real difference in how each
galaxy built up its family.

It's also worth stepping back from just these two galaxies. The SAGA
survey has cataloged satellite populations around 101 Milky-Way-like
galaxies, and the results cut both ways for us: the Milky Way's raw
satellite *count* turns out to be unremarkable -- comparable hosts
in our luminosity range have anywhere from 1 to 9 known satellites,
so we're well within the normal range. But our satellites' *behavior*
isn't typical: most of ours are quiescent, essentially "dead" to new
star formation, while satellites of similar galaxies elsewhere in
the survey are still actively forming stars.

### The Close Neighbor: Messier 33

{{< nbas-image src="m_33-s30-62m.webp" width="300" align="left"
caption="Bob Donahue, NBAS, Seestar S30, 62 min" >}}

For a sense of how differently this can go, M 33 -- the third
major galaxy in the Local Group -- has essentially no comparable
system at all: just two confirmed dwarf satellites (Andromeda
XXII/Triangulum I and Pisces VII/Triangulum III), against a
predicted dozen or so for a halo its size.

But why is anyone's real guess -- lower halo mass, an early close
pass that stripped satellites away, M 33 having absorbed some of its
own smaller satellites over time, or some mix of the three. Nothing
published points clearly at one over the others yet.

Three big Local Group galaxies, three very different-looking
families. Andromeda gives us the clearest view of a satellite system
simply because it's close enough to resolve in detail -- not
necessarily because it's the typical example.

## Finding and Observing

M 31 rides high in the northeast on Fall evenings and is visible to
the naked eye from a reasonably dark site -- its position is shown 
in the finder chart at the top of this article. Binoculars bring 
M 32 and M 110 into the same field of view as the main galaxy itself; 
both are genuinely visible, not just theoretical, targets with a 
modest pair of binoculars from a dark sky. Stepping up to a telescope 
gets you to Caldwell 17 and 18 (NGC 147/185) with enough aperture and 
dark skies, though they reward patience rather than technique. IC 10 
is the real test in this group -- faint to begin with, and further 
dimmed by its low position relative to our own Milky Way's disk.

The faint numbered satellites (And I, II, III, V, IX, X, and the
rest) are past the point where "observing" and "imaging" are the
same activity -- at magnitude 12-17 and fainter, spread out over
low-surface-brightness dwarf galaxies, they're not going to reward
a visual look through an eyepiece the way the brighter objects in
this article will. Probably at best they'll be a faint smudge like
you see in the field of view of other galaxies.

### Imaging with a Smart Scope

#### M 31

M 31 itself is a challenging case here, somewhat counterintuitively --
you need a very wide field of view *and* substantial exposure time
to bring out any real structure (dust lanes, spiral arms, the glow
of the disk beyond the bright core), and different rigs handle that
very differently.

Here are four images taken with different telescopes on different nights.  While M 31 comfortably fits in the FOV of the Seestar 30, to truly capture it with an S50 you need to use mosaic mode. The eQuinox 2 -- with its narrower FOV -- only images part of the galaxy (and the alt/az orientation on the night I did it was about the worst case scenario!).

#### M 32 and M 110

M 32 and M 110 are comparatively easy, in part because there's
little structure to expose in the first place -- with one notable
exception. M 110's dust lanes and young blue core are a real,
documented feature, and unlike M 32's smooth glow, that's something
a careful long exposure has an actual chance of pulling out. Worth
treating as a deliberate target rather than an afterthought next to
M 31.

#### Caldwell 17 and 18

Caldwell 17 (NGC 147) is mostly a matter of exposure time and dark
skies -- no particular technique required, just patience. Caldwell 18
(NGC 185) is worth treating more like M 110: its central dust patch
is a real, documented feature, not just a brightness threshold to
clear, so it's worth deliberately trying to pull that mottling out
rather than just stacking for overall glow.

#### IC 10

IC 10 is the genuine challenge of the set: its low galactic latitude
(-3.3°) means fighting through more of our own galaxy's dust and
stars than usual, on top of IC 10's own faintness. 

#### The Other Satellites

As for the faint numbered satellites: And I, II, and III are, per
the table above, theoretically within reach of a very long
smart-scope exposure -- but treat these as a "did I detect it at
all" challenge, not a picture. There's no structure to bring out at
that distance and surface brightness; success looks like confirming
a faint smudge in the right spot, or even just a fuzzy "star" -- not
a photogenic result.
