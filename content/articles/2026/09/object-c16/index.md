## Introduction

{{< nbas-image src="hero.webp" fullwidth="true" >}}

Caldwell 16 is a good target hiding in a bad neighborhood. At
magnitude 6.4 and spanning about two-thirds the width of the full
Moon, NGC 7243 is bright enough to hint at itself with the naked
eye under a dark sky and resolves into a loose scatter of white and
blue stars in binoculars or any small scope. It should be a staple.
Instead it sits quietly in Lacerta — a constellation most people
couldn't point to if their life depended on it — sandwiched between
Cygnus's crowded Milky Way fields and Cassiopeia's parade of showcase
clusters. Everyone's on their way to something more famous.

## A Constellation With No Story

{{< nbas-image src="hist_LAC.webp" width="500" align="right" 
caption="Lacerta from _Urania's Mirror_ c. 1825 (Sidney Hall / Jehoshaphat Aspin, public domain)" >}}

Lacerta, "the lizard," isn't ancient. Johannes Hevelius invented
it in 1687 to fill a gap between Cygnus, Cepheus, Andromeda, and
Pegasus, and unlike almost every other constellation in the sky,
it carries no mythology at all — no hero, no god, no tragic
transformation. Hevelius wanted to call it *Stellio*, after a
star-spotted lizard, but the name never caught on for the constellation
as a whole. It didn't entirely vanish, though: in 2024, the
International Astronomical Union quietly revived it as the official
proper name for Alpha (&alpha;) Lacertae, the constellation's brightest star.

Lacerta's faint zigzag shape is often described as a dimmer echo
of Cassiopeia's W right next door — which is also the easiest way
to actually find it. Trace Cassiopeia's familiar zigzag, then look
for a fainter, smaller version of the same shape nearby.

## The Cluster Itself

William Herschel logged NGC 7243 in 1788, one of hundreds of entries
in his systematic sweep of the sky. Modern surveys put its distance
at roughly 2,860 light-years and its age at around 250 million years — old 
enough that the cluster has visibly started to loosen and
spread, young enough that its hottest, most massive members are
still burning blue. That's part of what observers notice first: a
genuine contrast between white and vividly blue stars scattered
across the field, set against the duller yellow and red stars of
the background Milky Way behind them.

Astronomers classify open clusters partly by how concentrated and
rich they are — NGC 7243 falls into a middle category, neither
tightly packed nor sparse, which is a fair description of what
you'll see: a loose, granular scatter of roughly 70 stars rather
than a dense swarm.

## Observing NGC 7243

{{< nbas-image src="c_16-92S-PIXRCAGRAXGHS-square.webp"
caption="Bob Donahue, NBAS, Seestar S50, 10.3m" >}}

Binoculars pick up a handful of the brighter members against a soft,
unresolved glow; a small scope at low power starts resolving
individual stars across the full 21-arcminute span, more than most
eyepiece fields can comfortably hold at once. The smart-scope image
above, about ten minutes of integration with a Seestar S50, shows
the same loose scatter and blue-white color contrast that visual
observers have been noting for over two centuries.

At binocular scale, NGC 7243 is one bright knot among many stars
in a rich Milky Way field — you'll need to know roughly where to
look, since nothing about it jumps out as obviously "the cluster"
at first glance. Step up to a small refractor and the picture flips:
the field narrows, the background thins out, and the cluster's own
stars start to dominate the view instead of blending into the crowd
around them.

Lacerta is well placed in the evening sky through late summer and
into fall, culminating in the early-to-mid evening this time of
year. If you want to extend the session, Alpha Lacertae — the
newly-renamed Stellio — sits nearby, along with 4 Lacertae, the
double star visible at the edge of the binocular field above.

## Closing

Nothing about NGC 7243 is faint, difficult, or obscure once you're
actually looking at it — it's just parked next door to objects with
better name recognition, in a constellation nobody bothered giving
a myth. That's not a knock against it. It's the whole reason to go
find it.
