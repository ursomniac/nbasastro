# Imaging Challenge: Phoebe

## Discovery story (good narrative hook)

{{< nbas-image src="William_Henry_Pickering.jpg" width="300" align="right" caption="William Henry Pickering (1858-1938)" >}}

- Discovered March 18, 1899 by William Henry Pickering — not at the eyepiece, but on
  photographic plates that DeLisle Stewart had taken starting Aug 16, 1898 at the Boyden
  Station near Arequipa, Peru. **First moon ever discovered photographically**, rather than by
  someone looking through a telescope in real time - worth calling out explicitly, since the
  club's own imaging-challenge framing (finding something on a captured frame rather than at the
  eyepiece) is literally how this moon was found in the first place.
- Pickering also showed it orbits **retrograde** - backwards relative to Saturn's other major
  moons - the first clue that Phoebe wasn't "born" with the rest of the system.

## Physical / observational facts

{{< nbas-image src="Phoebe_cassini.jpg" width="300" align="right" caption="NASA/JPL from the Cassini spacecraft" >}}

- ~213 km mean diameter, dark and carbon-rich, heavily cratered.
- Retrograde, distant, tilted orbit -> now understood as a **captured object**, likely a former
  Centaur/trans-Neptunian body, not native to Saturn's system.
- Cassini's very first close encounter in the Saturn system (2004) - unusually well-studied for
  an irregular moon this size, specifically because it was a convenient target on the way in.
- 2009: Spitzer discovered a huge, extremely tenuous ring of dust well beyond Saturn's main
  rings, sharing Phoebe's orbit - dust knocked off Phoebe by micrometeoroid impacts. That
  material spirals inward (retrograde, like Phoebe) and slams into the leading hemisphere of
  Iapetus - which is the real explanation for Iapetus's famous two-tone "yin-yang" coloring.

## The actual challenge

- Mag ~16.44, angular separation from Saturn ranging roughly 0-35' over its ~550-day orbit (25'
  is a fair *typical*, not maximum, separation) - meaning success depends on knowing exactly
  where it is on a given night, not just pointing near Saturn.
- Framed explicitly as a stacked-exposure imaging target, not a visual one - well past
  naked-eye-at-the-eyepiece territory for most club apertures, but realistic for smart-scope
  integration.
- On the finder-chart problem: a single chart can't show both Saturn's slow multi-degree drift
  over the Fall and Phoebe's much faster apparent motion around it without becoming unreadable -
  same reason Pluto finder charts are snapshot-per-date rather than a plotted path. Plan is a
  handful of charts for specific imaging nights rather than one chart for the whole Sep-Dec span
  - ideally nights where Phoebe passes near a bright-enough-to-image field star, giving readers a
  concrete star-hop anchor instead of bare coordinates.
- You've got the Horizons ephemeris (daily, 9/1-12/31/2026) already - once it's shared I can help
  pick which specific dates are worth a chart, and rough out the "JS excerpt on the page" idea for
  showing Phoebe's offset from Saturn on any night in that window.
