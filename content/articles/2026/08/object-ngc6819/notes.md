# ACL Plan: NGC 6819, "Foxhead Cluster" (Cygnus)

Working notes only — not the article draft. Slug: `object-ngc6819`.

## Verified catalog data

- RA 19h 41m 18s, Dec +40° 11′ 12″ (J2000), Cygnus
- Visual magnitude ~7.3
- Angular size ~5′
- Distance ~7,500 ly ± ~500 ly (2.2–2.4 kpc; modern asteroseismic/photometric studies cluster near 2.3–2.4 kpc, older estimate 2.16 ± 0.57 kpc)
- Age ~2.4–2.5 Gyr (isochrone fitting, 2.5 ± 0.5 Gyr) — independently confirmed at 2.25 ± 0.20–0.30 Gyr via HST white-dwarf cooling-sequence fit. Two independent clocks agreeing this closely is itself worth a line.
- Metallicity near-solar, slightly super-solar: [Fe/H] ≈ +0.09 ± 0.03
- Trumpler class I1r; 100+ resolvable members to mag 10–12, total mass ~2,600 M☉
- Discovered by **Caroline Herschel**, 12 May 1784 — NOT William. Double-check in final draft; sources sometimes conflate the two Herschels.

## The centerpiece story: Kepler asteroseismology

NGC 6819 sat in Kepler's continuous field of view 2009–2013, one of only a few open clusters observed almost uninterrupted for 4 years at 30-min cadence (a "superstamp"). Old + solar-metallicity + dozens of red giants sharing one age/composition + sitting in Kepler's stare = ideal calibration lab: asteroseismic mass/radius from solar-like oscillations checked against independent cluster-based estimates.

Key results (Stello et al. 2011; Basu et al. 2011; Handberg et al. 2017, MNRAS 472, 979):
- Peakbagged oscillations in ~50–115 red giants
- RGB mass ~1.61 M☉ vs. red-clump mass ~1.64 M☉ → direct measurement of red-giant-branch mass loss, consistent with Reimers-law efficiency η≈0.1
- Flagged anomalously low-mass giants — e.g. **KIC 4937011** at 0.71 ± 0.08 M☉, nearly 1 M☉ lighter than clump stars of the same age, too light to have evolved normally in 2.4 Gyr
- 2024 follow-up (Brogaard et al., A&A) identifies this star specifically as a **post-common-envelope binary product** with an anomalously low-mass core-helium-burning core — i.e., a star that looks the wrong age until you realize it had a binary companion mess with its evolution

This is a strong narrative arc for the article: "a star in this cluster looked impossibly young for its age, and the explanation turned out to be a stripped-down survivor of a stellar merger/mass-transfer event." Good hook, good closer, mirrors the M54/lithium-problem "open question" style without repeating it.

## Secondary science notes (use if space allows)

- ≥53 eclipsing binaries found in Kepler photometry, plus RS CVn/BY Dra systems and ~70+ spotted/rotating stars (Kepler IRIS catalog, 9,150 stars total in field)
- ~29 blue stragglers characterized (2023 study)
- HST resolved the white-dwarf cooling sequence to its terminus — the independent age cross-check mentioned above

## Observing / finding notes

- ~8° WSW of Sadr (γ Cygni), ~2° from M29 — high overhead from ~40°N on September evenings
- Deceptively hard for its magnitude: sub-4″ scopes show only a hazy unresolved glow; 8–10″ starts resolving stars; 12–14″ under dark skies resolves two-to-three dozen+ stars and reveals the "fox head" asterism the nickname refers to
- Worth an explicit "don't expect fireworks in a small scope" framing so members aren't disappointed — set expectations the way we did with fainter globulars
