What you almost certainly saw is the SpaceX Falcon 9 that flew the Globalstar 2-R mission out of Cape Canaveral — and it's a great story for the article, because it was part of a genuine record-setter.

The launch itself. SpaceX's Falcon 9 rocket took flight from Cape Canaveral Space Force Station at 9:12 p.m. EDT carrying a batch of satellites for Globalstar, using first stage booster B1090 on its 14th flight, which flew back and landed at Landing Zone 40 about eight minutes after liftoff. All eight spacecraft separated successfully over a six-minute span beginning 56.5 minutes after liftoff.

Globalstar's constellation operates in a 1,410 km orbit inclined at 52 degrees — a mid-inclination LEO shell, not a polar or sun-synchronous orbit. From Cape Canaveral, reaching 52° inclination requires a launch azimuth of roughly northeast (~044° from true north) — essentially the same "up the coast" heading SpaceX uses for ISS resupply and many Starlink shells. That's the geometry that explains the "north" sighting: the ground track doesn't go straight up and over Massachusetts, it runs parallel to the coast well offshore and then bends further north as it climbs, so from western Massachusetts the booster/second-stage plume would appear to arc up and over toward the northern sky rather than pass overhead. This exact phenomenon has put Cape-launched rockets in New England skies before — Boston TV stations reported a similar sighting from a February Cape Canaveral Falcon 9 back in 2022.

Why it looked like a "burn" rather than a streak. At 9:12 p.m. in mid-August, ground-level observers in New England are already in twilight/darkness, but the second stage climbing to 150–200+ km altitude is still in direct sunlight. Its engine exhaust — and especially any expanding, unburned propellant venting during a coast or restart — scatters that sunlight into the classic diffuse glowing "jellyfish" cloud that can be visible from many hundreds of miles away, long after the vehicle itself is too small/dim to resolve as a point of light. If what you saw had that soft, expanding-cloud character rather than a sharp trail, that's consistent with either the ~T+9 min ascent burn (while still over the Virginia/Carolina coast) or, more likely given a purely northern bearing, the second-stage restart for the circularization/deployment sequence around T+56 min, by which point the vehicle would have been well up over the Gulf of Maine.

The red herring worth mentioning in the article anyway. This Globalstar flight was actually one half of a record: the USSF-366 liftoff from Vandenberg Space Force Base came just 38 minutes and 31 seconds later, at 9:50 p.m. EDT — SpaceX's shortest turnaround between two orbital launches. It's tempting to credit that one instead, and it may even be the more "polar" of the two (Vandenberg missions typically fly south toward higher-inclination or sun-synchronous orbits, and USSF-366's booster drop zones reportedly matched prior high-inclination Starlink shells). But California is about 2,400 miles from Cummington — well beyond the geometric horizon for anything at LEO altitude, so it's essentially impossible that's what you saw from Arunah Hill. Worth a line in the piece as the "twin launch that wasn't visible," though.

It stayed level, then started sinking toward the NE end. That's consistent with an object that was never overhead — it passed well to your north the whole time — and by the end of your view it was receding, dropping back toward the horizon as it continued on its track away from you. That fits an object continuing outbound to the northeast, generally the same direction the Globalstar stage's ground track would be heading if it launched from Florida on a 52°-inclination shot.

It stayed high rather than doing a classic fast rise-peak-set. Lower objects (a few hundred km, like a typical Starlink deorbit) sweep the sky quickly with a pronounced curved arc because they're close. Something moving more slowly and staying at a fairly constant, generous altitude over several minutes is more consistent with a considerably higher orbit — which actually fits the Globalstar target altitude (~1,400 km) better than a routine low-LEO Starlink disposal.

One genuinely useful thing I turned up while checking: SpaceX now disposes of the overwhelming majority of its Falcon 9 second stages immediately — only 3 of 165 upper stages launched in 2025 were left in orbit after payload deployment, rather than being deorbited right away. That's a good, citable stat for the article on its own, and it also means the odds strongly favor this being a same-night burn from one of the two vehicles that flew hours earlier, rather than some older derelict stage that happened to come down that week.
