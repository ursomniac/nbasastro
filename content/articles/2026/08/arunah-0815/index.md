## Perseids, Saturn, and a Packed Hill

{{< nbas-image src="Kevin_Marsh/IMG_4399.jpeg" fullwidth="true"
caption="Arunah Hill - one of the darkest sky locations in Massachusetts - Kevin Marsh, NBAS" >}}

This was planned as our monthly opportunity for members to get together at
one of our favorite observing spots, but this time adding in a dinnertime
cookout, and some socializing, followed by stargazing.   [Arunah Hill](https://www.arunahhill.org/) is one of the darkest observing locations in all of Massachusetts.  So it's a perfect match.

The day was chosen because of an important coincidence: the tail end of the
Perseid meteor shower, and a close-to-New Moon.  So there was the possibility of dark skies and a good reason to scan the skies.

## Arunah Hill

But overall, Arunah Hill was buzzing with activity: aside from NBAS members, and the usual Arunah Hill crowd, there were several guests who'd come up both local-area residents who knew about the location, but also many newcomers from as far as the Boston metro area for the night.   It's great to see entire families coming together for sky watching.

## "What Is That?"

Not everything we saw that night was on the observing list.

{{< nbas-image src="Falcon-9-path.webp" 
caption="Approximate path of the object in the northern sky" >}}

Sometime around 11 p.m., visitor Laura Gaffney was the first to notice it: a bright point of light with a faint, expanding cloud around it, moving steadily across the northern sky — well above the horizon, drifting from the direction of Boötes over toward Cassiopeia, west to east, over the course of a few minutes. Within a minute or two, half the hill was watching and asking the same question, with several people snapping images of this strange interloper.

It took a little musing, but eventually our "UFO" became entirely identified.
Here's what we've been able to piece together:

SpaceX launched a Falcon 9 from Cape Canaveral at 9:12 p.m. EDT that night, carrying nine satellites for Globalstar's HIBLEO-4 constellation (the Globalstar 2-R Launch 1 mission). It was actually one half of a record night for SpaceX — a _second_ Falcon 9 lifted off from Vandenberg Space Force Base in California just 38 minutes and 31 seconds later, the company's shortest-ever turnaround between two orbital launches. The booster on the Globalstar flight, B1090, was flying for the 14th time and landed back at Cape Canaveral about eight minutes after liftoff.

Interestingly, there was actually an *earlier* viewing opportunity for this same rocket, if anyone happened to be looking north around 9:18 p.m. — the visibility map below, generated by the SpaceMonkey launch-tracking app, shows roughly how far up the coast the second stage's ascent would have been visible in the minutes right after liftoff.

{{< nbas-image src="spacemonkey-launch1.webp" width="400" align="right" caption="The ascent-phase visibility window for Globalstar 2-R Launch 1, via the [SpaceMonkey](https://www.spacemonkey.app) app — this covers only the first ~6.5 minutes after liftoff, a separate (and earlier) window from what we actually saw." >}}

That's not what we caught, though — this map only covers the climb to orbit, and by the time anyone on the hill noticed anything, that window had long since closed. 

None of that, on its own, explains what we saw nearly two hours later. 
The breakthrough came from a photo one of our members, Kevin Marsh, managed to catch on his phone. The image's metadata — a timestamp of 11:01:59 PM EDT, GPS coordinates confirming it was shot right from Arunah Hill, and the phone's compass heading — was enough to pinpoint exactly where in the sky the mysterious object had been.

That timestamp is the key. It's **109 minutes and 59 seconds** after the Globalstar launch — strikingly close to a full orbital period (roughly 114 minutes) at the ~1,400 km altitude where Globalstar's satellites fly. 

Our best explanation: the spent second stage, having deployed its payload about an hour earlier, coasted nearly all the way around the Earth before firing its engine one last time to dump its remaining propellant and set up a controlled reentry. That venting propellant expands and freezes into a sunlit cloud of ice crystals — even though it was fully dark on the ground, the stage itself was still high enough to be in direct sunlight — producing the same "SpaceX spiral" or "space jellyfish" effect that's been photographed over New England several times in the past year, from other Falcon 9, Vulcan, and Ariane 6 missions.

The photo that cracked the case. Full EXIF data pinned the shot to just before 11:02 p.m. — about 110 minutes after Globalstar 2-R lifted off from Cape Canaveral — with the phone pointed almost exactly north.
