## Making the Best of this Year's Summer Skies

We haven't had the best weather this summer.  With ground-soaking thunderstorms, smoke from wildfires, and high humidity,
getting out and doing imaging has been _challenging_!

## Jeffrey the eQuinox 2

The eQuinox 2 is the largest smart scope in "The Fleet" (114mm aperture) with a 34' x 47' FOV.
So, it's capable of looking at smaller objects than the Seestars.


### Two Northern Galaxies 

{{< member-image src="ngc_6015-equ-260725-45m.webp"
   title="NGC 6015 in Draco"
   author="Bob Donahue"
   telescope="eQuinox 2"
   exposure_time="45 min" 
   frames="676 x 4s"
>}}
This galaxy in Draco doesn't have a nickname; most people will have never heard of it.
It's medium-sized:  5.8'x2.6' which makes it a good fit for the eQuinox 2.
It's 55.1 Mly away - about the same distance as the Virgo Cluster - but in an 
entirely different direction.

The wide spiral arms (it's type SAc - similar to M 33 in Triangulum) 
show definte star-forming activity along one spiral arm and some warping, but
this might be intrinsic to the galaxy and not tidal distortion from a nearby
galaxy, since it appears to be somewhat isolated.
{{< /member-image >}}

{{< member-image src="ngc_6140-equ-260816-54m.webp"
   title="NGC 6140 in Cepheus"
   author="Bob Donahue" 
   telescope="eQuinox 2"
   exposure_time="54 min" 
   frames="817 x 4s"
>}}
Another anonymous galaxy - this time in Cepheus - a constellation not known 
for having many bright galaxies.  Here the challenge is showing any 
structure because it's relatively small (2.1'x1.8') but not so small that
the eQuinox 2 can't capture any detail.

Here two spiral arms extend from the central bar (it's type SBc) and 
there's some mottling within the disk, and apparently over the central 
nucleus.   It's just a little further out from the previous galaxy - about 58 Mly.
{{< /member-image >}}

{{< member-image src="c_4-equ-260816-140m.webp"
   title="Caldwell 4: The Iris Nebula"
   author="Bob Donahue" telescope="eQuinox 2"
   exposure_time="140 min" frames="2,106 x 4s"
>}}
This is an object I've been meaning to get back to: it's genuinely beautiful, 
and one where increasing aperture (or imaging exposure time) just rewards
you with more and more detail.  Although this is a reflection nebula, the
nebulosity is sclupted by star formation: this nebula (also cataloged as
NGC 7023) hosts an embedded young star cluster, with young stellar objects
hidden in the surrounding clouds of material - invisible to us but visible
at infrared wavelengths.

The central stars are illuminating the nebula but they're not hot enough 
to cause the bright H II regions we see in other emission nebulae - at least
not in full force: longer exposures do show hints of H&alpha; emission on the
walls of the dust clouds.  Those stars are able to carve out the surrounding 
molecular cloud giving us the mix of voids and defined clouds.
{{< /member-image >}}

### Ellen and Elusippe: the Seestar S50s

This year I've been obsessed with getting long exposures (several of the 
images presented here are from that effort).   To make a bit of a "cheat" I
bought a second Seestar S50 (Ellen) with the idea that they'd both observe
the same target at the same time, thus doubling the exposure times.

I'm very pleased with the results - it's making it possible to get a few
hours of time on a target on one night.

{{< member-image src="ic_10-s50-260809-272m.webp"
   title="IC 10: The Starburst Galaxy"
   author="Bob Donahue" telescope="Seestar S50"
   exposure_time="272 min" frames="1,633 x 10s"
>}}
272 minutes = 4h 32m - a new record for me in terms of long exposures
(there are other Seestar owners who show images taken over 20+ hours 
over several nights - I clearly have a long way to go).

This seemingly unremarkable galaxy was an important target because
despite its inconvenient location (it's only 3° from the plane of the
Milky Way so there's a **lot** of galactic obscuration in the way - which is
also why it seems much redder than most galaxies), it's actually very close
to us (only 2.6 Mly) and has an interesting location within our Local Group,
and perhaps also a relationship with the Andromeda Galaxy.

(Andromeda's "Family" is the topic of an upcoming [A Wider View](/series/wider-view) article - stay tuned!)
{{< /member-image >}}

{{< member-image src="sh2_136-s50-2600816-204m.webp"
   title="The Ghosts of Cepheus" author="Bob Donahue" 
   telescope="Seestar S50" exposure_time="204 min" frames="1,224 x 10s"
>}}
Another sneak-peek preview!   

This is one of my favorite objects because it's so **weird**.  It's in a
part of the sky rife with "integrated flux nebulosity" clouds that don't 
glow because of a nearby hot star, but reflect the light of the entire 
galactic plane, because these clouds are high above the disk. This one, 
though, is a bit different: here we're seeing the glow of stars inside a 
Bok globule, illuminating it from within.

This nebula is one of several that will be featured in another 
[A Wider View](/series/wider-view) article in October on "Spooky Astronomy".
{{< /member-image >}}

### Ernestine the Seestar S30

The smallest member of "The Fleet" - Ernestine - is a Seestar S30.
It tends to be tasked with objects of larger angular size - generally over
20'.   

{{< member-image src="b_68-72_s30-260712-78m.webp"
   title="The Ink Spot and Snake Dark Nebulas"
   author="Bob Donahue" telescope="Seestar S30"
   exposure_time="78 min" frames="469 x 10s"
>}}
I think that dark nebulae tend to be overlooked and forgotten by many people
and that's a missed opportunity.   Especially the ones that are set against
the background Milky Way, showing a great contrast.   These molecular clouds
are so dense they block out the light of stars behind them; I also like how
many have very elaborate structures.   

This complex is somewhat nearby - only about 400-600 ly, but with the Milky Way stars in the background densely filling the entire frame.  It's about 0.5 ly across with a total mass of about twice the Sun's.   
{{< /member-image >}}

{{< member-image src="m_51-s30-260725-54m.webp"
   title="Messier 51: The Whirlpool Galaxy"
   telescope="Seestar S30" exposure_time="54 min" frames="321 x 10s"
>}}
This interacting pair is on just about everyone's target list, and I've
observed it a few times with all the telescopes in the fleet.   This time,
mostly for comparison, it was Ernestine's turn.   

What I love is not only seeing the effects of galaxy interaction, but also
the contrast in color:  The spiral arms of M 51 are blue with star formation
(and blowing up the image shows amazing detail and structure of the spiral
arms and dust lanes) with the older, redder stellar population seen 
in NGC 5195.
{{< /member-image >}}

{{< member-image src="c_14-s30-260809-65m.webp"
   title="The Perseus Double Cluster"
   telescope="Seestar S30" exposure_time="65 min" frames="390 x 10s"
>}}
The Double Cluster is one of the few objects in the sky that delights
no matter how you observe it:  even hunting for it with the naked eye
looking for that dim patch midway between the 'W' of Cassiopeia and the
constellation of Perseus is a fun exercise.

The two clusters: NGC 869 and NGC 884 are not as "together" as one might 
think - they're actually at slightly different distances (a few hundred light 
years separate them).   But that doesn't mean they don't share a 
history - they're both part of the Perseus OB 1 association and have similar
ages.

But even slightly separated, each other's presence would be overwhelming
from an observer in one of the clusters looking at the other:  it'd take 
up several degrees in the sky with the brightest stars rivaling the brightness
of Jupiter or Venus.
{{< /member-image >}}
