## 🔭 Saturn at Opposition, a Vanishing Jupiter

{{< nbas-image src="saturn.webp" 
caption="Astrobond, CC BY-SA 4.0, via Wikimedia Commons">}}

Saturn reaches opposition on **October 4** — its biggest, brightest, and
best night of the year to observe, with the rings once again on full
display.  If you missed it, check out our [Saturn Returns!](/articles/2026/09/saturn-returns/) article!   At opposition, Saturn shines at mag +0.3, at a distance of 8.43 AU (783.7 million miles); the light you see took 70.2 minutes to reach us!  Its angular size is also at maximum: 19.7" (45.9" if you include the rings).

{{< nbas-image src="jupiter-occultation-path_hu_ce8321787a314942.webp" fullwidth="true" >}}

Early risers should also mark **October 6**: the Moon passes directly in
front of Jupiter in a lunar occultation, with Jupiter disappearing behind
the Moon's limb at approximately **4:23 AM** and reappearing around
**5:28 AM** Eastern time.  Additional details are in the [Planet Watch](/articles/2026/09/planet-watch-jupiter-meets-the-moon-mars-crosses-the-beehive/) article.

## 📸 This Month's Image
{{< nbas-image src="ngc_7380-s50-lp-96m.webp"
caption="NGC 7380, Bob Donahue, NBAS, Two Seestar S50s, 96 min" >}}

NGC 7380, discovered by Caroline Herschel in 1787, is a young open cluster
wrapped in the glowing nebulosity that gives it its nickname: the Wizard
Nebula. Sitting roughly 8,500 light-years away in Cepheus, its silhouette
is carved by radiation and stellar winds from a pair of massive young
stars at its heart. It's a rewarding imaging target, though its faint
nebulosity is a tough visual catch even in a telescope — usually needing
dark skies and a narrowband filter to see directly.

Here's another case where I "doubled my fun" using two Seestar S50s (Ellen and Elusippe) on the same target at the same time.   Being able to image for over an hour really improves the contrast of the fainter outer parts against the background sky.
