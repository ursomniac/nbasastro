## Introduction

With autumn in force, longer nights, crisper air, comes Halloween, and there are plenty of "treats" to find in the skies above.   Coincidentally, **13** objects (enough to cause triskaidekaphobes to shudder) have nicknames that align with these "spooky" times.

{{< nbas-image src="banner.webp" fullwidth="true" 
caption="Sweeping from Aquila to Orion, our selections provide an entire evening of observing!" >}}

Every one of the 13 objects below has picked up a nickname somewhere along the way -- ghosts,
a witch (twice, different body parts), a wizard, a skull, a bat, a widow's web. None of that reflects
anything they actually have in common astrophysically; a planetary nebula, a supernova remnant, an
open cluster, and a whole separate galaxy have about as little in common as objects can. The only
real thread here is that somebody, at some point, looked at each of these and thought of something
appropriately unsettling for the season. That's reason enough. All thirteen happen to be above the
horizon at midnight on Halloween, which is either a coincidence or the sky trying to make our job
easier.

### 1. NGC 6781 - the Ghost of the Moon

{{< nbas-image src="ngc_6781-equ-21m.webp" width="400" align="left"
caption="Bob Donahue, NBAS, eQuinox 2, 21 min" >}}

William Herschel found this one on July 30, 1788, and it's a genuine ghost in the sense that
matters most for a planetary nebula: it's the exposed remains of a star's outer layers, ejected as
the star itself collapsed toward its white dwarf future. It sits about 1,500 light-years away in
Aquila, and the "Moon" in its nickname isn't just poetic -- the shell is actually barrel-shaped, but
we happen to be viewing it almost exactly pole-on, which flattens the barrel into the round, softly
glowing disc that gives it a lunar look through the eyepiece. Also nicknamed the Snowglobe Nebula
for the same reason.

{{< clear >}}
### 2. NGC 6939 - the Ghost Bush Cluster

{{< nbas-image src="ngc_6939-s50-10m.webp" width="400" align="right"
caption="Bob Donahue, NBAS, Seestar S50, 10 min" >}}

Discovered by William Herschel in 1798, this open cluster in Cepheus is genuinely old for its
type -- estimates run from 1.6 to over 2 billion years, well beyond the typical lifespan of a
loose star cluster before it disperses. The nickname is one of several competing "fanciful" names
observers have given it over the years (Flying Geese Cluster and Silk Fan Cluster are two others),
with no single origin story that's won out. It sits conveniently close to the Fireworks Galaxy
(NGC 6946), making the two a natural pair in the same field of view.

### 3. NGC 6960 / Caldwell 34 - the Witch's Broom

{{< nbas-image src="c_34-S30-70m.webp" width="500" align="left"
caption="Bob Donahue, NBAS, crop of the image above." >}}

William Herschel discovered this in September 1784, though the "Witch's Broom" nickname is entirely
modern -- it's simply what the western arc of the Veil Nebula looks like once you know to look for
a broom in it. The whole Veil complex is the expanding wreckage of a single supernova, its light
having reached Earth somewhere between 10,000 and 20,000 years ago, and the Broom itself is the
easiest, brightest piece of that wreckage to find -- the traditional starting point for exploring
the rest of the Veil.

{{< clear >}}
### 4. NGC 6995 / IC 1340 - the Bat Nebula

{{< nbas-image src="ngc_6995-s30-70m.webp" width="400" align="right"
caption="Bob Donahue, NBAS, crop of the image above." >}}

The southern tip of the Eastern Veil, on the opposite side of the same supernova remnant from the
Witch's Broom. The specific "bat" silhouette that gives this piece its nickname is usually pinned
more precisely to IC 1340, a designation sitting right alongside NGC 6995 in the same patch of sky
-- the two labels essentially describe the same real estate. It's become a recurring subject of
NASA's own Astronomy Picture of the Day around Halloween, appearing there in 2021, 2024, and again
in 2025.   (To see the bat in the image, turn it almost upside-down.)

{{< clear >}}
### 5. Sh 2-136 - the Ghost of Cepheus

{{< nbas-image src="sh2_136-s50-204m.webp" fullwidth="true"
caption="Bob Donahue, NBAS, Seestar S50, 204 min" >}}
{{< clear >}}

Not a Herschel object at all -- this one comes from Stewart Sharpless's mid-20th-century survey of
emission nebulae, catalogued in 1959. It's a Bok globule (a dense, cold pocket of gas and dust
actively collapsing to form new stars) sitting at the edge of the Cepheus Flare star-forming
region, roughly 1,200-1,500 light-years away and only about 2 light-years across. Several young,
embedded stars illuminate it from within, giving it a brownish-gold glow rather than the blue tint
most reflection nebulae show -- and the wispy, upraised shapes in that glow are exactly what earned
it the name.

{{< clear >}}
### 6. NGC 7380 - the Wizard Nebula

{{< nbas-image src="ngc_7380-s50-LP-26m-wide.webp" fullwidth="true"
caption="Bob Donahue, NBAS, Seestar S50, LP filter, 26 min" >}}

Another Caroline Herschel discovery -- she found this open cluster on August 7, 1787, with the
same small 4.2-inch sweeper she used throughout her career, and her brother William catalogued it
as H VIII.77. The nebula wrapped around it (Sh2-142) is roughly 7,000-8,500 light-years away and
about 100 light-years across, sculpted into its wizard-like shape by the radiation and winds of the
young, massive stars still forming inside it -- among them DH Cephei, a close binary of two O-type
stars that does most of the work illuminating the whole region.

{{< clear >}}
### 7. NGC 7790 - the Widow's Web Cluster

{{< nbas-image src="ngc_7790-s50-9m.webp" width="400" align="right"
caption="Bob Donahue, NBAS, Seestar S50, 21 min.  Neighboring cluster NGC 7788 is at upper-left." >}}

This one earns its keep scientifically, not just visually. NGC 7790 hosts three classical Cepheid
variable stars -- CE Cas A, CE Cas B, and CF Cas -- more than any other known cluster in the Milky
Way, which makes it a genuine anchor point for calibrating the cosmic distance scale: Cepheids are
the standard candles astronomers use to measure distances to other galaxies, and a cluster with
three of them in the same place, at the same known distance, is a rare chance to cross-check the
method against itself. The cluster itself sits around 10,800 light-years away in Cassiopeia.

{{< clear >}}
### 8. IC 63 - the Ghost of Cassiopeia

{{< nbas-image src="ic_63-equ1-yann-michel-niquet.webp" width="380" align="left"
caption="Courtesy Yann-Michel Niquet, eQuinox 1, Saint-Martin-le-Vinoux, France, Bortle 7/8, over four nights (over 12k frames with different filters)." >}}
{{< nbas-image src="ic_63-s50-LP-34m.webp" width="380" align="left"
caption="Bob Donahue, NBAS, Seestar S50, LP Filter, 34 min" >}}

{{< clear >}}
About 550 light-years away, and genuinely being erased as we watch it. IC 63 sits only 3-4
light-years from Gamma Cassiopeiae, an extreme blue-white star roughly 19 times the Sun's mass and
tens of thousands of times its luminosity, spinning so fast (over 200 times the Sun's rotation
rate) that it's visibly flattened at its poles. That star's radiation is slowly stripping the
nebula apart, hydrogen atom by hydrogen atom -- the same ultraviolet bombardment that makes IC 63
glow red in hydrogen-alpha light is also, gradually, what's dissolving it. ESA/Hubble released a
detailed image of it in 2018 explicitly timed for Halloween.

{{< clear >}}
### 9. NGC 246/Caldwell 56 - the Skull Nebula

{{< nbas-image src="c_56-equ2-34m.webp" width="320" align="right"
caption="Bob Donahue, NBAS, eQuinox 2, 34m" >}}
{{< nbas-image src="ngc_246-equ1-40m-phil-norms.webp" width="430" align="right"
caption="Courtesy Phil Normandon, eQuinox 1, 40 min, outside Grenoble, France, 28 Aug 2024" >}}
{{< clear >}}
William Herschel found this one in 1785, and its central "eye" turns out to be more crowded than
it looks: the star at the heart of the Skull is actually a confirmed hierarchical triple system,
HIP 3678, anchored by an exceptionally hot pre-white-dwarf star (surface temperature around
150,000 K) with a fainter, wider companion pair orbiting alongside it. The nebula itself sits
roughly 1,600 light-years away in Cetus, and its skull-like look comes from an irregular, patchy
shell rather than the cleaner rings most planetary nebulae show.

{{< clear >}}
### 10. NGC 404 - Mirach's Ghost

{{< nbas-image src="ngc_404-equ-15m.webp" width="400" align="left"
caption="Bob Donahue, NBAS, eQuinox 2, 15 min, with &beta; Amdromedae (Mirach) just out of frame." >}}

William Herschel discovered this one in 1784, and its nickname comes from pure bad luck of
placement: it sits only 7 arcminutes from Mirach (Beta Andromedae), a star bright enough to drown
the galaxy in glare and difficult enough that some older star atlases simply didn't plot it. NGC
404 is about 10 million light-years away, just outside the Local Group and not bound to it -- and
it's stranger than it looks. As a lenticular galaxy it should be quiet, essentially done forming
stars, but it's caught actively forming new ones in a ring, evidence of a collision with a smaller
galaxy roughly a billion years ago. Its nucleus is also one of the better nearby candidates for a
genuine intermediate-mass black hole, weighing in somewhere under half a million solar masses --
a size category that sits awkwardly between ordinary stellar-mass black holes and the supermassive
kind, and one astronomers don't have many confirmed examples of.

{{< clear >}}
### 11. NGC 457/Caldwell 13 - the Owl (or E.T.) Cluster

{{< nbas-image src="c_13-s50-10m.webp" width="400" align="right"
caption="Bob Donahue, NBAS, Seestar S50, 10 min.  The \"owl\" is swooping down the image, eyes in front with outstretched wings and a tail above." >}}

William Herschel found this one in 1780. Unlike most of the nicknames on this list, this one has a
specific, known origin: an Astronomy magazine contributor observing in the 1970s noticed the owl
shape -- two bright "eye" stars, a body, wings, feet -- and the name simply stuck. Those two eyes
are Phi Cassiopeiae and the fainter HD 7902, and Phi Cas carries its own open question: nobody is
completely certain it's actually a member of the cluster rather than a foreground star along the
same line of sight. If it is a genuine member, at the cluster's roughly 7,900-light-year distance,
it would be one of the most luminous stars known -- brighter than Rigel.

{{< clear >}}
### 12. IC 2118/NGC 1909 - the Witch's Head Nebula

{{< nbas-image src="witch_head-jim-wimmer-18h.webp" fullwidth="true"
caption="The Witch's Head with Rigel.  Credit: Jim Wimmer SW Virginia, 18+ hours, Seestar S50 and S30 mosaic images." >}}

Officially IC 2118, discovered photographically by Max Wolf in 1891 -- though it also carries the
older designation NGC 1909, from a visual sighting by William Herschel in 1786 whose recorded
position turned out to sit about 22 arcminutes off from where the nebula actually is, likely a
simple transcription error. Either way, it's the same object: a huge, extremely faint reflection
nebula about 900-1,000 light-years away, lit by the reflected light of Rigel some 2.6 degrees away
in neighboring Orion. The profile that gives it its name only really reads clearly with the image
rotated -- most photographers turn it about 90 degrees from its natural orientation to bring out
the witch's face.

The image above comes from Jim Wimmer in SW Virginia.   This is a great example of what a **really dedicated imaging effort** can accomplish.   Over 2600 20-second and 30-second frames were used to create this image under Bortle 4 skies (about the same we have here) all using free post-processing software (Siril, GraXpert, Gimp, and Astrosharp).  

Jim has a [YouTube channel](https://www.youtube.com/@PhotonRookie-vk7ge) "Photon Rookie" where he discusses his processing techniques using images taken with his Seestars.   He's imaged many of the most popular nebulae we know and love and his images are simply outstanding.   Definitely worth checking out, if you want to think about upping your image processing game, or even just to be amazed at his imaging.

### 13. Messier 78 - Casper the Ghost Nebula

{{< nbas-image src="m_78-equ-30m.webp" width="400" align="right"
caption="Bob Donahue, NBAS, eQuinox 2, 30 min" >}}

Discovered by Pierre Méchain in early 1780 and added to Messier's own catalog that December --
one of several Messier objects that Messier himself never actually found. It's the brightest
reflection nebula in the sky, part of the same vast Orion molecular cloud complex as the Orion
Nebula itself, about 1,300-1,350 light-years away. Its most memorable recent moment came in
February 2004, when amateur astronomer Jay McNeil imaged a genuinely new nebula -- now called
McNeil's Nebula -- in the same field, one that simply wasn't present in any earlier image of the
region going back decades. It was a real discovery, made with amateur equipment, in one of the
most photographed patches of sky there is.

## Observing

### Binoculars

Three of these are genuinely reachable: **NGC 457** (bright and easy, a fine binocular cluster in
its own right), **NGC 6939**, and **NGC 7790**, which shows as a small hazy patch under reasonably
dark skies. Everything else on this list either needs more aperture or more exposure time than
binoculars can give you.

### Telescopes

**NGC 457, NGC 6939, NGC 7790, NGC 246, M 78,** and the cluster (though not the surrounding nebula)
of **NGC 7380** are all solid visual targets in a modest amateur scope. **NGC 404** is a genuine
classic telescopic challenge -- doable, but Mirach's glare takes real technique (high magnification,
averted vision, nudging the star just out of the field) to overcome. The Veil segments
(**NGC 6960/6995**) reward a scope equipped with an O III filter and a dark site. **IC 63** and
**NGC 6781** are visible in a telescope but faint enough that a beginner shouldn't expect an easy
win on the first try.

### Best with Imagers

**Sh2-136**, **IC 2118**, and the fainter Veil detail all genuinely need long exposures to show
anything close to what the images on this page show -- these are not objects where the eyepiece and
the photograph tell the same story. IC 63 in particular transforms with narrowband or Hα data,
revealing structure that's essentially invisible without it. If you only have time or equipment for
a handful of these thirteen, the imaging-friendly ones are where the "spooky" shapes actually
become unmistakable rather than a matter of imagination.
