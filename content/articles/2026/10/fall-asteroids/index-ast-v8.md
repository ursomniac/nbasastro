## Introduction

{{< nbas-image src="hero.png" fullwidth="true" >}}

Between Mars and Jupiter, in a belt of rubble left over from the solar system's earliest days, there are millions of asteroids. 
Only a small handful are ever bright enough for
a backyard telescope, fewer still for binoculars, and exactly one -- Vesta, at its very best -- can
occasionally be teased out with the naked eye. This fall, four of the brighter ones happen to line
up within a few weeks of each other: 4 Vesta, 2 Pallas, 9 Metis, and 192 Nausikaa. None of them
will look like anything more than a point of light. That's not a knock against them -- it's kind
of the whole point of this challenge.

## What's Out There?

{{< nbas-image src="kirkwood_gap.webp" width="400" align="right"
caption="Distribution of asteroids in the Main Belt, based on plot by Alan Chamberlain, JPL/Caltech, Public domain, via Wikimedia Commons.  The gaps where there are fewer asteroids are due to resonances with the other planets." >}}
"Asteroid belt" tends to get used as a stand-in for "everything small in the solar system," and
it isn't. The Main Belt -- home to all four of tonight's targets, sitting roughly 2 to 4 AU from
the Sun -- is just one neighborhood. Trojans share a planet's orbit outright, clustered 60 degrees
ahead of or behind it at stable gravitational parking spots called Lagrange points; Jupiter's are
the biggest population by far, but Mars, Neptune, and even Earth have a few of their own.
Near-Earth objects are asteroids whose orbits happen to swing in close to us, regardless of where
they originally formed. And well past Neptune, in the cold, is the Kuiper Belt -- a completely
different reservoir, icy rather than rocky, and the source of most short-period comets. It's not
a bigger, farther-out version of the Main Belt. It's a different neighborhood entirely.

As for how the Main Belt itself came to be: you'll actually meet the *first* attempt at an answer
in Pallas's own history below -- Heinrich Olbers thought it was the wreckage of a single exploded
planet. That idea is dead. Different asteroid families have chemically distinct compositions that
no single shattered parent body could produce. The version most people learn instead is the
"failed planet" story: material that should have accreted into a fifth rocky world, prevented from
finishing by Jupiter's gravity stirring the neighborhood into a shooting gallery instead of a
nursery. That's still roughly right, but the current research is more unsettled than the textbook
version suggests. Some recent modeling goes further and asks whether the region was ever
particularly full of *local* material to begin with -- whether much of today's belt is actually
implanted debris, scattered in from the growing giant planets and the inner disk rather than
survivors of a dense population that grew up right here. Which version turns out to be closer to
true is genuinely still being argued.

One thing does seem settled: the timing. Whichever mechanism did the stirring -- the Grand Tack's
sweep of a migrating Jupiter, or the chaotic gravitational tug-of-war as Jupiter and Saturn crossed
an early orbital resonance -- it happened fast, within the first five to ten million years of the
solar system's existence, tangled up with the same process that was still actively building the
terrestrial planets. The Main Belt wasn't an already-finished neighborhood that later got hit by
chaos. The chaos was part of how it ended up looking the way it does at all.

Here's how tonight's four compare, side by side:

| Asteroid | Diameter | Semi-major axis | Eccentricity | Inclination | Orbital Period | Albedo |
| -------- | -------- | ---------------- | ------------- | ------------ | -------------- | ------ |
| 9 Metis      | ~190 km | 2.39 AU | 0.12 |  5.6° | 3.69 yr | 0.118 |
| 192 Nausikaa | ~100 km | 2.40 AU | 0.25 |  6.8° | 3.72 yr | 0.260 |
| 2 Pallas     | ~512 km | 2.77 AU | 0.23 | 34.9° | 4.62 yr | 0.159 |
| 4 Vesta      | ~525 km | 2.36 AU | 0.09 |  7.1° | 3.63 yr | 0.423 |

2 Pallas and 4 Vesta are the second and third largest Main Belt asteroids (behind 1 Ceres).  9 Metis and 192 Nausikaa
are much smaller (about 35th for Metis, and in the 160s for Nausikaa).   

So how do they get so bright at opposition to be good observing targets?   
Aside from size, there are two other things:  orbital eccentricity, and albedo.
If an asteroid has a higher-than-average eccentricity (as do Pallas and Nausikaa), then depending on where it
is in its orbit at/near opposition, the distance to Earth might be closer, making the asteroid brighter.
Similarly a higher albedo (reflectivity) makes an asteroid brighter;  many of the larger carbon-rich asteroids
(like 1 Ceres) have low albedos lower than 0.10 (for comparison, the Moon is 0.12, and asteroid 87 Sylvia is close to pitch black), 
and so asteroids like Vesta and Nausikaa stand out.

While most of the Main Belt asteroids stick to the Ecliptic plane, Pallas's inclination is an extreme outlier (another large 
asteroid, 15 Euphrosine is similar in size and inclination as Pallas).

## Why Now?

Asteroids are at their best at opposition -- the point in their orbit where Earth passes between
them and the Sun, putting them closest to us and on the meridian -- their highest point in the sky
-- at local midnight. This particular stretch of fall gives us four oppositions in about seven
weeks, working from earliest to latest:

| Asteroid          | 2026 Opp. |  Mag.  | Constellation |    |
| ----------------- | --------- | ------ | ------------- | -- |
| **9 Metis**       |  Aug. 28  |   9.2  | Aquarius      | Now mag ~10.0 |
| **192 Nausikaa**  |  Sep. 30  |   8.4  | Pisces        |    |
| **2 Pallas**      |  Oct.  4  |   8.2  | Cetus         |    |
| **4 Vesta**       |  Oct. 13  |   6.5  | Cetus         |    |

As of early October, each target sits in a different part of its own story. Nausikaa is only a
couple of days past its own opposition and still close to its best. Pallas is right on the verge
of opposition. Vesta is still brightening toward its own. Metis is the outlier -- a full five weeks
past its late-August peak and noticeably fainter for it, which makes it the real challenge of the
four rather than a warm-up. Pallas and Vesta are the main event: both at or near their best, and
both conveniently parked in the same constellation a few weeks apart.

## The Targets

### 9 Metis: A Small Telescope's Discovery

{{< nbas-image src="hero-metis.png" width="500" align="right"
caption="9 Metis shape based on lightcurve inversion.  Astronomical Institute of the Charles University: Josef Ďurech, Vojtěch Sidorin, CC BY 4.0, via Wikimedia Commons" >}}

By the 1840s, the pace of asteroid discovery had gone quiet for decades. Vesta, found in 1807, was
the fourth and -- for 38 years -- the last: nobody found a fifth until 5 Astraea in 1845. Then the
floodgates opened. Hebe, Iris, and Flora all turned up in 1847 alone, and right behind them, on
April 25, 1848, came Metis -- found by Andrew Graham, an assistant at Markree Observatory in County
Sligo, Ireland.

{{< clear >}}
{{< nbas-image src="andrew-graham.jpg" width="200" align="right" 
caption="Andrew Graham (1815-1908), assistant at Markree Observatory in Sligo, Ireland" >}}

Markree was, for a few years around 1834, home to the largest refracting telescope in the world --
a 13.3-inch Cauchoix lens that was the observatory's pride. Graham didn't use it. He found Metis
with a much smaller, purpose-built 4-inch "comet seeker" -- a wide-field instrument specifically
suited to scanning for something faint and slow-moving against a big patch of sky, which is exactly
the kind of hunting this whole article is about. It was the first asteroid ever discovered from
Ireland, and remarkably, the *only* one for the next 160 years, until amateur astronomer Dave
McDonald, observing from Celbridge, found 2008 TM9 (later named 281507 Johnell) in 2008. It was
also Graham's only asteroid discovery -- he went on to a long career at Cambridge Observatory, but
never found a second.

#### Why It's Worth the Chase

Metis is made of silicate rock and metallic nickel-iron -- a mix that, together with an unusually
compact, dense shape, has led to the idea that it might be the intact core remnant of a much larger
body (some estimates put the original at 300-600 km across, Vesta-sized) shattered in an ancient
collision. Metis was once thought to anchor its own small asteroid family, but that grouping hasn't
held up under closer study; the more interesting lead is a spectral resemblance to 113 Amalthea,
hinting the two might be fragments of the same shattered parent rather than unrelated neighbors.
It spins briskly, once every 5.08 hours -- close to Vesta's pace -- and its orbit is fairly
ordinary as these things go, an inclination of just 5.6 degrees, nothing like the dramatic tilt
we'll get to with Pallas. A quiet, small example of the same violent history that shaped Vesta,
just seen from the debris side rather than the survivor's side.

### 192 Nausikaa: Named for a Princess Who Helped a Shipwrecked Hero

{{< nbas-image src="johann-palisa.jpg" width="200" align="right"
caption="Johann Palisa (1848-1925), astronomer at the Austrian Naval and Vienna Observatories" >}}

Nausikaa was discovered on February 17, 1879, by Johann Palisa at Pola Observatory (in what's now
Pula, Croatia). Palisa is worth knowing on his own merits -- he's the most prolific visual asteroid
discoverer in history, finding 122 of them by eye before photography took over the job entirely.
The name comes from Homer's *Odyssey*: Nausicaä was the princess who found Odysseus shipwrecked
and half-drowned on her island's shore and helped him find his way home, one of the gentler
episodes in a poem mostly about monsters and revenge.

{{< clear >}}

#### Why It's Worth the Chase

{{< nbas-image src="hero-nausikaa.png" width="500" align="right"
caption="192 Nausikaa shape based on lightcurve inversion.  Astronomical Institute of the Charles University: Josef Ďurech, Vojtěch Sidorin, CC BY 4.0, via Wikimedia Commons" >}}

Nausikaa is an S-type asteroid -- stony, rather than the carbon-rich material that dominates the
belt's outer reaches -- and a genuinely elongated one: roughly 103 by 90 kilometers, tumbling
through a slow 13.6-hour rotation, more than twice as long as Metis's day. Its orbit is moderately
eccentric (about 0.245 -- noticeably more elongated than Metis's 0.12), which is part of why its
opposition brightness varies more from apparition to apparition than a more circular orbit would
allow. There's also a small, unresolved mystery attached to it: lightcurve observations in 1985
hinted at a possible companion, tentatively orbiting every 4.6 days at a distance of roughly
1,100 km, but nothing has ever confirmed it. As far as anyone knows today, it's still just
Nausikaa out there -- but "as far as anyone knows" is doing some real work in that sentence.

### 2 Pallas: Lurking in the Outer Reaches of the Main Belt

{{< nbas-image src="heinrich-wilhelm-olbers.jpg" width="200" align="right"
caption="Heinrich Wilhelm Olbers (1758-1840)" >}}

Pallas's discovery only makes sense with a bit of backstory. In 1800, a group of German astronomers
who'd nicknamed themselves the "Celestial Police" organized a systematic hunt for a planet that was
supposed to exist between Mars and Jupiter -- predicted by the Titius-Bode law, the same rough
formula that had correctly called Uranus's distance in 1781. They divided the zodiac into 24
fifteen-degree zones and assigned one to each astronomer. Giuseppe Piazzi wasn't even a member yet
when he beat all of them to it, spotting Ceres by chance on New Year's Day 1801 while compiling an
unrelated star catalog.

Ceres fit the predicted distance, but was disappointingly small and faint for a "planet." Heinrich
Wilhelm Olbers -- observing from a rooftop observatory at his own home in Bremen -- was tracking
Ceres to refine its orbit when, on March 28, 1802, he noticed a second object nearby: Pallas. Two
bodies sharing roughly the same orbit undercut the whole premise of a single missing planet, and
it was this discovery that pushed William Herschel to coin a new word for them: "asteroid."

{{< nbas-image src="the-orbits.jpg" width="500" align="left"
caption="Some 19th century books included the first four asteroids as planets: Moon, F. G.J. & G. Pickett, Public domain, via Wikimedia Commons" >}}
It's worth pausing on that word. At the time, none of the first four discoveries -- Ceres,
Pallas, Juno, and Vesta -- were considered anything other than planets; Herschel's coinage didn't
catch on right away, and it took until the 1850s, once dozens more objects had turned up in the
same stretch of sky, for astronomers to quietly reclassify all four down to asteroids. Pluto's
demotion in 2006 got the headlines and the public outrage. It wasn't actually the first time a
"planet" got voted off the list -- just the most recent, and the one anyone alive today actually
watched happen.

Olbers had his own explanation, and it's a good one even though it turned out to be wrong: he
proposed that Ceres and Pallas were fragments of a single, larger planet, shattered by an ancient
collision with a comet. If that were true, he reasoned, more fragments should turn up near the
points where the two objects' orbits crossed -- and searching exactly there is how he found Vesta
himself, five years later, in 1807. (As the overview above lays out, we know now that the asteroid
belt isn't the wreckage of a planet that broke apart -- it's material that never finished
*building* one, or possibly material swept in from elsewhere entirely. Wrong theory, right
discovery either way.)

Olbers wasn't finished making a mark on astronomy, either -- he's also the namesake of Olbers'
Paradox: the question of why the night sky is dark at all, if the universe is infinite and eternally
full of stars, since every line of sight should eventually land on one. (The short answer is that
the universe isn't infinitely old -- there's a horizon set by the finite speed of light and the
finite time since everything began, so most of that starlight simply hasn't reached us, and much of
what has is redshifted out of visibility.)

Pallas itself is the third-largest asteroid by both mass and volume, behind only Ceres (now
classified as a dwarf planet) and Vesta, and like Ceres it's thought to be a genuine leftover
protoplanet -- a building block that never finished building.

#### Why It's Worth the Chase

{{< nbas-image src="pallas-sphere-2.jpg" align="right" width="450"
caption="ESO/M. Marsset et al./MISTRAL algorithm (ONERA/CNRS), CC BY-SA 4.0 <https://creativecommons.org/licenses/by-sa/4.0>, via Wikimedia Commons" >}}
The real story here is the orbit. Most asteroids in the main belt keep close to the same flat plane
the planets orbit in -- inclinations of a few degrees are typical. Pallas is tilted 34.9 degrees to
that plane, by far the most inclined orbit of any large asteroid, and exactly *why* is still an open
question. The general explanation for the belt's messy orbits is Jupiter's own early migration --
the "Grand Tack," in which a young Jupiter swept inward and then back out, scattering material onto
wildly different paths -- but pinning that broad picture to Pallas's specific, extreme tilt is still
active research, not a settled answer. That steep tilt is also a practical problem: matching
Pallas's orbit costs far more fuel than almost anything else in the belt, and no mission has ever
gone there. Pallas also rotates in under eight hours -- slower than Vesta's brisk 5.3-hour day, but
faster than Ceres's 9-hour one -- with its axis tipped so far over (about 84 degrees) that it spins
almost on its side, giving it seasons more extreme than anything else in the belt.

### 4 Vesta: the Brightest Main-Belt Asteroid

{{< nbas-image src="Vesta_Rotation.gif" width="400" align="left"
caption="Vesta rotation: NASA/JPL-Caltech/UCLA/MPS/DLR/IDA; Little Mountain 5, Public domain, via Wikimedia Commons" >}}
Vesta's discoverer was already a familiar name by 1807: Heinrich Wilhelm Olbers, the same person
behind Pallas five years earlier, still searching for fragments of his hypothesized shattered
planet. He didn't name this one himself, either -- by then it had become something of a tradition
to let a distinguished colleague do the honors, so he handed the privilege to Carl Friedrich Gauss,
who picked Vesta, the Roman goddess of the hearth.

{{< clear >}}
#### Why It's Worth the Chase

{{< nbas-image src="vesta-meteor.jpg" width="350" align="right"
caption="R. Kempton (New England Meteoritical Services), Public domain, via Wikimedia Commons" >}}
Vesta's story just changed. For years, the settled picture -- built on Dawn spacecraft data from
2011-2012 -- was that Vesta had genuinely differentiated like a small planet: a metallic core about
220 kilometers across, a rocky mantle, a basaltic crust, the whole terrestrial-planet package in
miniature. Then in April 2025, a reanalysis of Dawn's gravity data (Park et al., published in
*Nature Astronomy*) found little to no evidence of that core after all. Nobody's sure yet what that
means. One possibility is that Vesta's melting simply never finished -- it started down the path to
a layered interior and stalled partway. The more startling possibility, raised by MSU's Seth
Jacobson, is that Vesta isn't really a complete protoplanet at all, but a broken-off piece of a
larger, growing world elsewhere in the early solar system -- meaning the famous HED meteorites on
Earth (Howardites, Eucrites, and Diogenites, all confirmed fragments of Vesta) might be samples of
a planet that no longer exists in any recognizable form. Nobody knows which planet that would have
been. It's a genuinely open question, less than two years old as of this writing.

{{< nbas-image src="Rheasilvia_and_Veneneia.jpg" width="400" align="left"
caption="NASA/JPL-Caltech/UCLA/MPS/DLR/IDA/PSI, Public domain, via Wikimedia Commons" >}}
What Dawn *did* confirm without controversy is the scale of Vesta's damage: an enormous impact
basin near its south pole, Rheasilvia, roughly 500 kilometers across on a body only about 525
kilometers in diameter, with a central peak that rivals the tallest mountains in the solar system.
That's almost certainly the impact -- or one of two, alongside the older Veneneia basin -- that
blasted loose the material that eventually fell to Earth as those same HED meteorites. So there's a
real, non-zero chance a piece of the object you're looking at is sitting in someone's meteorite
collection right now, whatever turns out to be true about what Vesta actually is.

Vesta's own orbit, for what it's worth, is comparatively unremarkable next to Pallas's -- a modest
7.1-degree inclination, well within the range most of its neighbors share.

## Observing

### Binoculars

{{< nbas-image src="vesta-261013-0200-15x70.png" width="400" align="right"
caption="Vesta, 13-Oct at 10 PM with 15x70 binoculars" >}}

Vesta alone is worth pulling out a pair of 10x50s for. At magnitude 6.5 and brightening toward
opposition, it's within reach of ordinary binoculars from a reasonably dark sky -- no telescope
required to actually see it, just patience for the star-hop and the same night-to-night comparison
trick described below to confirm you've really got it. Pallas, brightness-wise, is technically in
range too, but it's a noticeably harder catch: fainter, and a less forgiving star-hop from
anywhere with real light pollution. Metis and Nausikaa, both fading past magnitude 8.5, are
telescope targets.

### Telescope

All four targets are within reach of a modest telescope from a reasonably dark sky -- nothing here
needs serious aperture. Vesta and Pallas, both comfortably above magnitude 8.5, are the easiest;
Metis and Nausikaa, fading toward magnitude 9-10, reward a slightly darker site and a bit more
patience.

### Smart Scopes

This is close to ideal smart-scope territory: point, stack a short sequence, and let the software
do the work of pulling a faint, star-like point out of the background. None of these four need
narrowband filtering or long individual exposures -- they're not nebulae, just faint points that
benefit from a bit of integration time.

### Confirming What You're Looking At

An asteroid doesn't look any different from a star in a single image -- a good "old school" way to
be sure you've actually got it is to prove it moved. Note the field, or better, take a quick image,
then come back a night or two later and compare. Every star will have stayed exactly where it was.
The asteroid won't have. (Of course you can also check the field with planetarium apps like
Stellarium.)

## Wrapup

Four points of light, four completely different stories: a possibly-not-quite-a-protoplanet whose
own core just became a genuine mystery, the most steeply tilted orbit of any large asteroid, a
fragment found with the *small* telescope at an observatory famous for its giant one, and a body
named for a moment of kindness in a three-thousand-year-old poem. None of that is visible in the
eyepiece. All of it is true anyway, and once you know it, that point of light doesn't look quite so
plain the next time you find it.
