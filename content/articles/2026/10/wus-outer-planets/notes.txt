## 1. Opening hook

- Saturn and Eris are in the **same constellation** (Cetus) at the same time this year
- Saturn mag 0.3, Eris mag 18.7 — an 18.4-magnitude spread, which works out to roughly a
  **23-million-fold difference in brightness**, sitting in the same patch of sky
- Good framing device for the whole piece: "outer planets" spans from the easiest object in the
  sky to one of the hardest amateur targets that exists, and this year you can point at both from
  the same spot

## 2. Saturn — the anchor

- Opposition Oct 4; still excellent by Oct 23 (three weeks past peak, still bright, still up all
  evening)
- **Naked eye:** yes, easy, unmistakable
- **Binoculars:** yes
- **Visual telescope:** rings resolved easily even in modest scopes; with a long focal length,
  real aperture, and good seeing, the Cassini Division, moons, and storm systems are all in play —
  this is real optical-telescope territory, not a smart-scope claim
- **Smart scope:** rings *are* achievable with the right settings — short single exposures rather
  than the usual long-stack deep-sky mode, since Saturn is bright enough that stacking mostly just
  blurs or blows it out. Worth framing as a "wait, really?" moment, since smart scopes are usually
  marketed/understood as deep-sky instruments, not planetary ones

---

## 3. Neptune

- Opposition was Sep 25/26; still well-placed all autumn
- **Naked eye / binoculars:** no — too faint, starlike even at best
- **Visual telescope:** tiny blue disk, needs a real scope to resolve as non-stellar at all
- **Smart scope:** Neptune + Triton is a genuine, legitimate catch — faint-companion detection is
  closer to a smart scope's actual strength (deep-sky sensitivity) than a resolution challenge
- **Aside — Nereid:** Neptune's third-largest moon, mag ~18.7–19.2, essentially the *same
  difficulty tier as Eris*. A documented amateur capture exists (a Cloudy Nights planetary imager
  recorded its trail at mag 18.69). Worth a callout paired with the Eris section later — same
  difficulty, different object type (moon vs. dwarf planet)

---

## 4. Uranus

- Opposition Nov 25, but brightest window is **Oct 24–Dec** — meaning it becomes prime literally
  the day after this article publishes
- **Naked eye:** borderline — mag ~5.7, achievable only under genuinely dark skies
- **Binoculars:** yes, easily, as a "star" (won't show a disk)
- **Visual telescope:** small disk, hint of blue-green color
- **Smart scope:** Uranus + 5 moons is a real, personal catch worth featuring — arguably the best
  "smart scopes aren't supposed to do this" moment in the whole piece
- **Correction/note:** Uranus's rings are NOT an amateur target at any level — don't imply
  otherwise. Even JWST reportedly shows them only as thin slivers; this is professional/space
  telescope territory only

## 5. Pluto — farewell cameo

- Opposition was back on Jul 27; by Oct 23 it's three months past peak and setting notably
  earlier each night
- Gone from convenient evening visibility by mid-November
- Frame as a "catch it now" nod tied back to the existing dedicated Pluto article, not a full
  section — a paragraph, not a subhead's worth of new material
- **Naked eye / binoculars:** no
- **Visual telescope:** needs real aperture even to confirm as a point source
- **Smart scope:** feasible as a faint point; more "confirm the dot is there and has moved between
  sessions" than a satisfying image
