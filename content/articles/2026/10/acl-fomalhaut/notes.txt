Low in the south on October evenings, sitting almost alone in a stretch of sky with little else bright nearby, is Fomalhaut — nicknamed "the Loneliest Star" for exactly that reason. It's worth finding on its own merits, but it's also at the center of one of the more satisfying astronomical mysteries of the last twenty years, one that just got a dramatic update.

## Finding It

Fomalhaut, the brightest star in Piscis Austrinus (the Southern Fish), is easy to identify once you know where to look, precisely because there's so little competition around it. From the Great Square of Pegasus, follow the western edge of the Square south, past Cetus, and continue down toward the horizon — Fomalhaut is the lone 1st-magnitude point sitting by itself low in the south. At our latitude it never climbs very high (it tops out around 18° above the horizon), so a clear, unobstructed view to the south is essential — this is one to try from the open parts of the hill, not from anywhere with trees or buildings in that direction.

## A Star With Real Baggage

Fomalhaut is about 25 light-years away and roughly 440 million years old — young by stellar standards, still surrounded by a substantial ring of dusty debris left over from planet formation. That debris ring is what made Fomalhaut famous: in 2008, a team using the Hubble Space Telescope announced they'd directly imaged a planet within it, "Fomalhaut b" — one of the first exoplanets ever seen in an actual photograph, rather than inferred from a star's wobble or a transiting shadow.

It didn't hold up. Follow-up observations found Fomalhaut b behaving strangely for a planet: it kept fading, and by 2020 astronomers concluded it was expanding rather than staying put — consistent with an enormous cloud of debris from a collision between two large icy bodies, not a solid world at all.

## The New Twist

That's where things sat for a few years — a demoted "planet," probably just an expanding dust cloud, with the case not quite closed. Then, late in 2025, a team led by Paul Kalas (the same astronomer behind the original 2008 discovery) went back through Hubble's archival images of the system and found something nobody expected: a **second** compact source, appearing in 2023 images, in a different part of the debris ring — looking exactly like Fomalhaut b did when it was first spotted twenty years earlier.

The team's interpretation, published in *Science* in January 2026: this is a second planetesimal collision, independent of the first, caught essentially in the act. Seeing two such events in the same system within twenty years is itself surprising — these collisions are thought to be rare — but it also does something important for the original mystery: it strongly reinforces the idea that Fomalhaut b (now referred to as "cs1") was never a planet either, just the debris cloud from an earlier, similar collision that we happened to catch a couple of decades before this new one.

In other words: Fomalhaut didn't lose a planet. It turned out to be a system where planet-sized objects are running into each other with some regularity — arguably a more interesting result than a single directly-imaged world would have been, and a useful warning for astronomers hunting for real exoplanets elsewhere: a dust cloud from a big collision can masquerade as a planet for years before it gives itself away.

## Why It Matters Beyond Fomalhaut

The research team plans to keep watching the system with both Hubble and JWST over the next few years, hoping to catch the debris clouds evolving — or catch a third collision outright. For anyone interested in how planetary systems actually form, Fomalhaut has become an unplanned but genuinely valuable laboratory: violent collisions like this were almost certainly common in our own solar system's youth, and we rarely get to watch the process happen anywhere in real time.
