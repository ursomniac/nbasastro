## Introduction

{{< nbas-image src="banner.webp" fullwidth="true" >}}

Fomalhaut's name comes from Arabic, *fum al-hut* -- "the mouth of the fish" -- describing its
position marking the mouth of Piscis Austrinus, the Southern Fish, in Ptolemy's original
constellation figure. The fish itself has a couple of different stories attached to it depending
on which ancient source you read: Eratosthenes described it as the larger fish drinking the water
Aquarius pours out, keeping the heavens from flooding; Hyginus told a different version, in which
a fish carried Aphrodite and her son Eros to safety across the Euphrates while they fled the
monster Typhon. Long before either story, Persian astronomers had already singled the star out on
its own merits: as Hastorang, it was one of the four "Royal Stars" -- along with Aldebaran, Regulus,
and Antares -- marking the sky's cardinal points around 3000 BCE, with Fomalhaut standing watch
over the winter solstice.

{{< nbas-image src="f-photo.webp" width="300" align="right"
caption="NASA, ESA, and the Digitized Sky Survey 2. Acknowledgment: Davide De Martin (ESA/Hubble), CC BY 4.0, via Wikimedia Commons" >}}

It's picked up a more modern nickname too: the Loneliest Star, for sitting nearly by itself in a
wide, dim stretch of autumn sky. That nickname is about what your eyes see. It's not quite true --
and the rest of this piece is largely about the family Fomalhaut turns out to actually have.

## Similar to Vega

{{< nbas-image src="stellar-shapes-corrected.webp" width="500" align="right"
caption="Measured rotation and true shape, Sun vs. Fomalhaut A vs. Vega -- Vega's apparent slow spin is a viewing-angle illusion." >}}

Fomalhaut and Vega make an oddly well-matched pair. Both are A-type main-sequence stars -- Vega a
slightly hotter, slightly more massive A0V, Fomalhaut an A3V -- and both sit almost exactly the
same distance from us, close to 25 light-years, essentially neighbors on the galactic scale. Their
ages line up too: Fomalhaut at 440 ± 40 million years, Vega generally put at somewhere around 450
million, though -- much like Fomalhaut's own age -- that number has moved around over the years as
better methods came along, from an early estimate of 350 million up past 450 million and higher
still in more recent interferometric work.

They share something more specific than coincidence, though. In 1983, the IRAS satellite was using
Vega -- already a standard reference star for calibrating other observations -- for a routine
calibration check, and found something nobody expected: far more infrared light than a plain star
should give off. That excess turned out to be heat radiating from a disk of cold dust, and it was
completely unanticipated; nobody had gone looking for it. The find was significant enough that it
got its own name, the "Vega phenomenon," and astronomers immediately started checking other bright,
nearby stars for the same signature. Fomalhaut was one of the first three found to share it, along
with Beta Pictoris and Epsilon Eridani -- the founding members of what's now an entire field of
study: debris disks around ordinary stars.

Where they actually part ways is shape, though not quite along the lines you'd expect. Vega is a
genuinely extreme case: CHARA array interferometry revealed in 2006 that it's spinning at somewhere
around 91-93 percent of the speed that would tear it apart, distorting it into a visibly egg-shaped
ellipsoid roughly a fifth wider at its equator than at its poles. That same rotation drags the
effective gravity down enough at the equator to cool it by more than 2,000 K relative to the poles
-- gravity darkening -- so Vega is genuinely two different temperatures depending on where on its
surface you're looking. We don't see any of this by eye, or even in most images: Vega happens to be
oriented almost exactly pole-on from Earth, so what actually reaches us is its hot, bright polar
face, with the distorted, cooler equator hidden around the back. It took interferometry, not a
telescope image, to catch it. Fomalhaut isn't the calm, spherical contrast you might expect,
though -- its own measured rotation (about 93 km/s) is comparable to, if not faster than, Vega's
directly measured value of just 20 km/s, and interferometry has picked up a small oblateness of its
own, on the order of 2 percent rather than 20. The real difference isn't fast versus slow. It's
that Vega's low apparent rotation is a disguise -- we're seeing it end-on -- while its true spin is
running far closer to its own breaking point than Fomalhaut's ever gets.

Neither star has all that long left, relatively speaking. An A-type star this size burns through its
fuel fast and has a total main-sequence lifetime of a bit over a billion years, well under a tenth
of the Sun's. Fomalhaut and Vega are both already more than a third of the way through their entire
lives as stars.

## Debris Disk

{{< nbas-image src="webb-dust-cloud.webp" width="500" align="right"
caption="JWST MIRI image of Fomalhaut's debris disk, NASA, ESA, CSA, A. Pagan (STScI), A. Gáspár (University of Arizona), CC BY 4.0" >}}

Fomalhaut's disk isn't one simple ring -- JWST's MIRI instrument imaged the system in 2023 and
resolved three nested belts: a warm asteroid-belt analog close to the star, a newly discovered
intermediate belt around 78-90 AU out that turned out to be misaligned with everything else and
looks like it's being shepherded by an unseen planet, and a cold, broad outer belt roughly 133 AU
out and about 25 AU wide, sometimes informally called "Fomalhaut's Kuiper belt." Hubble's 2008
coronagraph image of the outer ring picked up a striking, off-center glow that earned the whole
system an unofficial nickname among astronomers: the Eye of Sauron. More recent ALMA observations,
published in 2025, found an eccentricity gradient across the disk and other structural hints
consistent with unseen planets gravitationally sculpting it from the inside -- and a separate 2025
study found the disk isn't dominated by large, Pluto-sized bodies the way some early models
predicted. Mark Wyatt's own estimate puts something like 300 million planetesimals in orbit out
there, a debris field vastly larger than our own Kuiper belt's.

## Exoplanets?

{{< nbas-image src="disk_ring_and_b_2008.webp" width="500" align="right"
caption="NASA, ESA, P. Kalas, J. Graham, E. Chiang, E. Kite (University of California, Berkeley), M. Clampin (NASA Goddard Space Flight Center), M. Fitzgerald (Lawrence Livermore National Laboratory), and K. Stapelfeldt and J. Krist (NASA Jet Propulsion Laboratory), Public domain, via Wikimedia Commons" >}}

In 2008, a team led by Paul Kalas announced they'd directly imaged a planet inside that outer ring
-- Fomalhaut b, one of the first exoplanets anyone had ever actually photographed, rather than
inferred from a star's wobble or a transiting shadow. It got an official name a few years later
through the IAU's public NameExoWorlds process: **Dagon**, after the ancient Mesopotamian deity
often depicted as half man, half fish -- a fitting choice for something found in the mouth of the
Southern Fish.

{{< nbas-image src="hst-cs1-cs2.webp" width="500" align="left"
caption="ESA/Hubble, CC BY 4.0 <https://creativecommons.org/licenses/by/4.0>, via Wikimedia Commons" >}}

It didn't hold up as a planet. Follow-up Hubble monitoring through the 2010s found Dagon behaving
all wrong for a solid world: it kept fading, and by 2014 it had grown too faint to detect at all,
while also visibly expanding -- the signature of an enormous, dispersing cloud of debris from a
collision between two large icy bodies, not a planet in a stable orbit. Then, in late 2025, Kalas's
team went back through the archive and found a second, independent compact source in a different
part of the ring, in 2023 images -- looking exactly like the original Dagon detection had looked
twenty years earlier. Published in *Science* in January 2026, the interpretation is that this is a
second planetesimal collision caught in the act, and that its resemblance to the first all but
confirms Dagon itself was never a planet either -- just the debris cloud from an earlier, similar
collision that happened to get caught by Hubble right as it was forming.

So there's an officially IAU-named exoplanet, Dagon, that in all likelihood isn't a planet at all
-- named back when the 2008 detection still looked solid, the name simply never got walked back
once the science moved on. It's a good caution for anyone hunting for real exoplanets elsewhere: a
big enough collision can look exactly like a world for years before it gives itself away.

## Companions

{{< nbas-image src="fomalhaut-system.webp" width="450" align="right" >}}
"Loneliest star" turns out to undersell it. Fomalhaut is the anchor of a genuinely wide triple
system, just one spread across so much sky that the connection went unnoticed for well over a
century.

**TW Piscis Austrini** (Fomalhaut B) sits about 2 degrees away in the sky -- roughly four full
moons' width -- and around 0.91 light-years off in real distance, orbiting the system's barycenter
on a period estimated at 5.8 million years. It's a K4 orange dwarf, noticeably smaller and cooler
than Fomalhaut A, and a genuinely active one: it's a BY Draconis-type variable, its brightness
flickering between magnitude 6.44 and 6.49 on a 10.3-day cycle as starspots rotate in and out of
view, with occasional flares on top of that. It was first suspected as a companion back in 1938,
but not confirmed as genuinely bound to Fomalhaut until 2012. There's even a hint, from 2019
astrometric data, that TW PsA might have a planet of its own -- something in the neighborhood of
1.2 Jupiter masses -- though the evidence is thin and the orbit poorly constrained.

**LP 876-10** (Fomalhaut C) is stranger still: a red dwarf about 2.5 light-years from Fomalhaut A
and roughly 5 degrees away across the sky -- far enough that it technically sits in the
neighboring constellation, Aquarius, while Fomalhaut A and B share Piscis Austrinus. At magnitude
12.6, it needs a real telescope to see at all. It wasn't confirmed as part of the system until
2013. And it has its own debris disk -- a genuine rarity around a star this small and faint, which
makes the Fomalhaut system one of the few known multi-star, multi-disk arrangements anywhere
nearby.

{{< nbas-image src="f-system-diagram.webp" fullwidth="true" >}}
Taken together, the three stars span several degrees of sky -- among the widest confirmed stellar
systems known. Fomalhaut isn't lonely. It just keeps its family at an unusual distance.

## Observing

{{< nbas-image src="finder-naked-eye.webp" width="500" align="right" >}}

Fomalhaut is a naked-eye target, and an easy one to identify once you know where to look, precisely
because there's so little competition around it. From the Great Square of Pegasus, follow the
western edge of the Square south, past Cetus, and continue down toward the horizon -- Fomalhaut is
the lone 1st-magnitude point sitting by itself low in the south. At our latitude it never climbs
very high, topping out around 18 degrees above the horizon, so a clear, unobstructed view to the
south is essential -- this is one to try from the open parts of the hill, not from anywhere with
trees or buildings in that direction.

If you want to chase the family resemblance, TW Piscis Austrini (Fomalhaut B) is technically a
naked-eye object too, at magnitude 6.44-6.49 -- right at the edge of visibility from a genuinely
dark sky, an easy binocular catch otherwise, and only 2 degrees from Fomalhaut itself. LP 876-10
(Fomalhaut C), at magnitude 12.6 and 5 degrees further out in Aquarius, is a real telescope
target -- six inches of aperture or more, and a night when you're willing to do some careful
star-hopping for a genuinely obscure catch.

{{< clear >}}

Next clear night with a good southern horizon, take a look -- you're looking at a very ordinary
point of light with a family spread across a fifth of the sky and a genuinely strange story behind
the ring of debris surrounding it.
