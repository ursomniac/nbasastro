## Introduction

{{< nbas-image src="banner.webp" fullwidth="true" >}}

{{< object-specs align="right" >}}

Stephan's Quintet is one of the most photographed galaxy groups in the sky, and for
good reason: it's compact, it's dramatic, and it comes with a genuinely good story
attached. Five galaxies appear crowded into a patch of sky only a few arcminutes across.
Only four of them are actually there together. The fifth is
a bystander that happens to be sitting in the foreground, unrelated to the other
four, caught in the frame by pure chance of alignment. 

This is also the first compact group of galaxies ever recognized as such — found
in 1877, well before anyone had a coherent picture of what a "galaxy" even was.
Everything we now know about it — the collision in progress, the black hole
quietly feeding in one of its members, the foreground galaxy hiding in plain
sight — has been layered on top of that original 1877 sighting by generation
after generation of better instruments. That's the story worth carrying with you
when you go find it yourself.

## Discovery

{{< nbas-image src="edouard-stephan.jpg" width="200" align="right"
caption="Édouard Stephan (1837-1923)" >}}

Édouard Stephan discovered this group in 1877 while directing the Marseille
Observatory in France, using an 80 cm Foucault reflector — a genuinely serious
instrument for its era, built around one of Léon Foucault's silvered-glass
mirrors. Stephan cataloged five relatively bright objects packed into a tight
knot near the bright spiral NGC 7331, in Pegasus. He had no way of knowing that
one of the five didn't belong, or that the other four were in the middle of
tearing each other apart. Compact groups of galaxies weren't even a recognized
category of object yet; Stephan's Quintet effectively became the founding
example of one, formally cataloged over a century later as Hickson Compact
Group 92 when Canadian astronomer Paul Hickson surveyed groups like it in 1982.

Look at the distance column above and the plot twist is right there in the
numbers: NGC 7320 sits at roughly 40 million light-years, while everything
else in the group is out at 289-304 million. That's not a rounding error — it's
a critical part of this object's story.

While each galaxy has an NGC catalog designation, the group collectively is
Hickson Compact Group 92.  The image shows both the NGC numbers and the Hickson Group letters.

### NGC 7320 (HCG 92a): the "Photobomber"

{{< nbas-image src="Geoffrey_Burbidge.jpg" width="195" align="right"
caption="Geoffrey Brubidge (1925-2010)" >}}
{{< nbas-image src="Margaret_Burbidge.jpg" width="200" align="right"
caption="Margaret Burbidge (1919-2020)" >}}

In 1961, husband-and-wife astronomers Geoffrey and Margaret Burbidge took
spectra of all five galaxies and found that four of them were receding at
roughly 6,000–6,600 km/s, consistent with each other and with membership in a
single, genuinely bound group. The fifth, NGC 7320, was receding at only about
800 km/s. Under Hubble's law — recession velocity scales with distance — that
low a redshift put NGC 7320 seven times closer than its neighbors: a small,
nearby foreground galaxy that happens to line up with a much more distant group
almost perfectly from our vantage point.

{{< nbas-image src="NGC7320-SDSS-DR14.jpg" width="300" align="left"
caption="DSS image of NGC 7320" >}}

That explanation is the accepted one today, though it wasn't always
uncontested — Halton Arp catalogued the group as Arp 319 in his 1966 *Atlas of
Peculiar Galaxies* and pointed to it for years as a challenge to using redshift
as a distance measure at all. (That controversy, and how it eventually got
resolved, is a good story on its own — one we may come back to in a future
article.)

Looking at all the images and comparing them to the eQuinox 2 image, NGC 7320
is definitely bluer than the other galaxies in the group.  Even without space-based 
imaging, the oval shape is distinct, with a hint of its spiral arm structure: the disk
is clearly unevenly bright with some mottling across it. 

### NGC 7318 A and B (HCG 92b and 92d): The Main Event

{{< nbas-image src="ngc7318.png" width="400" align="right"
caption="N.A.Sharp/NOIRLab/NSF/AURA, CC BY 4.0, via Wikimedia Commons" >}}

When Stephan first catalogued this group in 1877, he recorded four nebulae —
what later received the designations NGC 7317, 7318, 7319, and 7320 in the New
General Catalogue of 1888. What eventually became NGC 7318A and NGC 7318B was,
at the time, just that single entry, NGC 7318; only later, higher-resolution
observations split it into two distinct, genuinely colliding galaxies. That
split is the accidental reason this is a "quintet" of five at all: one of
Stephan's four turned out to be two galaxies, and another (NGC 7320, see
above) turned out not to belong to the group in the first place.

In Hubble's 2009 WFC3 portrait, the pair appears as a single blob with two
bright cores, ringed by young blue star clusters and pink clouds of glowing
hydrogen — active stellar nurseries. Those clusters are less than 10 million
years old, young enough that they haven't yet cleared away the gas they were
born from. Even more striking: off to one side, in a patch of space that
isn't part of either galaxy's disk, more star clusters are forming in
mid-air — stars being born directly out of the tidal wreckage between
galaxies, with no parent galaxy underneath them at all.

Even modest smart-scope imaging shows the two galaxies' spiral arms pulled
visibly out of shape by the encounter. Look closer at NGC 7318B specifically
and one of its arms appears to have come loose from the galaxy entirely — a
separate strand of material trailing away and ending in a distinct, blobby
knot rather than fading out smoothly. That's a real, specifically studied
feature: astronomers have identified several "tidal dwarf galaxy" candidates
in this group, dense clumps of gas and stars stripped out during these
encounters that may be compact enough to be gravitationally self-bound —
essentially small, new galaxies condensing out of the wreckage. A couple of
the strongest candidates are tied to tails associated with NGC 7318B
specifically, and the most famous one in the whole group sits in NGC 7319's
own long tidal tail. Nothing about this is settled science yet —
"candidate" is doing real work in that phrase — but it's a legitimate live
question, not just an odd-looking smudge.

### NGC 7319 (HCG 92c): The Tail Keeper

{{< nbas-image src="ngc_7319-hst.jpg" width="400" align="left"
caption="NASA, Jayanne English (University of Manitoba), Sally Hunsberger (PSU), Zolt Levay (STSci), Sarah Gallagher (PSU), and Jane Charlton (PSU), Public domain, via Wikimedia Commons" >}}

NGC 7319 shows the clearest scars of everything else in this group. It's a
barred spiral galaxy with a type 2 Seyfert nucleus — an actively feeding
supermassive black hole — and it trails two long, roughly parallel tidal tails
that curve away toward NGC 7320C. Those two tails appear to record two
separate close passages by the same intruder: an older, fainter tail from an
encounter several hundred million years ago, and a younger, more distinct
tail from a second pass roughly 200 million years later.

In the Hubble image, NGC 7319's spiral arms sweep almost all the way around,
nearly 180 degrees back to the galaxy's central bar. Blue specks along the
upper arm and red dots near the core are both star clusters — the blue ones
young, the red ones older or more dust-reddened — each one a grouping of many
thousands of stars. Some of those clusters are younger than either known
passage of the intruder, suggesting the interaction is still touching off new
star formation well after the flyby itself ended.

This isn't a detail that needs a space telescope to catch, either — even a
smart-scope stack starts showing the group's tidal tail and what
look like three separate detached strands of spiral arm pulling away from
the core. Real tidal structure, not just something confirmed on paper by
infrared or X-ray data.

### NGC 7317 (HCG 92e): The Quiet One

{{< nbas-image src="NGC7317-SDSS_DR14.jpg" width="400" align="right"
caption="NGC 7317 SDSS DR14 image" >}}

NGC 7317 is the smallest member of the group and, at first glance, the least
interesting: Hubble's own description of it is a plain, normal-looking
elliptical, visibly less affected by the interactions than its neighbors. No
spiral arms, no obvious tidal tail, no dramatic distortion of its own light
— most models of the group's collision history leave it out entirely, for
lack of anything to explain.

(You may notice what looks like a thin faint edge-on galaxy on the periphery of
NGC 7317, showing up as a faint "smudge" in the smart-scope image.   Strangely,
this object doesn't appear to have a designation anywhere, despite how apparent
it is especially in the HST and JWST images, which show a large number of even
fainter galaxies in the background.)

### NGC 7320 C: Disruptor?

{{< nbas-image src="NGC_7320C.jpg" width="200" align="left"
caption="Jschulman555, CC BY-SA 3.0, via Wikimedia Commons" >}}

The group's real headcount is stranger than "four instead of five." NGC 7320C
sits just outside the frame of most classic images of the Quintet, wasn't part
of Stephan's original five, and yet its redshift (~6,000 km/s) matches the true
group almost exactly — it belongs, even though it isn't named in the group's
own title. The evidence suggests that a few hundred million years ago, NGC
7320C plunged through the group, 
stripping out gas and stars as it passed and leaving behind long tidal tails
still visible in deep images today. It's the drive-by member: not one of the
"quintet" by name, but very much a participant in the collision that shaped
everything else you see here.

## How They Are Interacting

{{< nbas-image src="hst.jpg" fullwidth="true"
caption="NASA, ESA, and the Hubble SM4 ERO Team, Public domain, via Wikimedia Commons" >}}

Take NGC 7320 out of the picture and what's left is four galaxies genuinely
caught up in a slow-motion collision. Three of them — NGC 7317, NGC 7318A, and
NGC 7319 — share very nearly the same redshift and a small internal velocity
spread, meaning they're gravitationally bound to each other and likely to
eventually merge. The fourth, NGC 7318B, is the group's most recent arrival:
it's plowing into the group at roughly 800–1,000 km/s — almost two million
miles per hour — relative to the others, smashing through gas that an earlier
flyby by NGC 7320C already stripped out into the space between the galaxies.

Hubble's WFC3 camera, which took the portrait above during the telescope's
2009 servicing mission, was able to show this collision in more than one
light at once: visible and near-infrared together, plus a filter tuned to
glowing ionized hydrogen. The colors in the image trace the ages of different
stellar populations directly — younger stars show up bluer, older ones
redder — and the infrared channel sees straight through curtains of dust that
would otherwise hide entire clusters of stars from a purely visible-light
view. The three distant, actively interacting galaxies read distinctly redder
than the foreground NGC 7320, both because their stars are on average older
and because dust stirred up by the collision itself reddens their light
further.

That collision between NGC 7318B and the group's gas is also generating a
shock wave larger than the entire Milky Way, heating gas to millions of
degrees — one of the most energetic intergalactic shock fronts known. NASA's
Spitzer Space Telescope first revealed it in mid-infrared light in 2006; the
Chandra X-ray Observatory has since imaged the hot gas it produces directly.
When the James Webb Space Telescope imaged the group in July 2022 — Stephan's
Quintet was one of the five targets chosen for Webb's very first released
images — its MIRI instrument captured the shock front and the surrounding
tidal debris in extraordinary detail, along with pockets of star formation
triggered by the ongoing interaction. In the composite image below, the
yellow and orange tones mark MIRI's mid-infrared view of hot dust and the
shock-heated regions around the colliding pair, while the blue and white
tones are stars picked up by NIRCam at near-infrared wavelengths — so the
collision itself is quite literally shown in a different color than the
galaxies producing it.

Look closely at that MIRI feature and it appears to extend well past NGC
7318B, reaching up into NGC 7319 itself — and that's not an illusion, but
it's also not one single thing. Published analysis of the JWST data
identifies a genuine shocked filament roughly 40,000 light-years long
running between NGC 7319 and NGC 7318B, produced by NGC 7318B's high-speed
plow through the group's stripped gas. But NGC 7319 also happens to host its
own separately bright feature at almost the same spot: a supermassive black
hole, around 24 million times the Sun's mass, actively feeding and normally
hidden behind dust — exactly the kind of thing MIRI's infrared vision is
built to see straight through. What reads as one continuous sparkly smear
connecting the two galaxies is more likely two real, physically distinct
phenomena sitting right next to each other in the frame: an intergalactic
shock front, and a dust-shrouded black hole finally showing its face.

{{< nbas-image src="Stephan's_Quintet_taken_by_James_Webb_Space_Telescope.jpg" fullwidth="true"
caption="NASA/ESA/CSA James Webb Space Telescope composite (NIRCam + MIRI), released July 2022." >}}

Compact groups like this one are thought to have been far more common in the
early universe, where similar collisions may have helped fuel the very
energetic black holes we see as distant quasars. Stephan's Quintet gives us
a nearby, present-day laboratory for a process that mostly played out
billions of years ago, everywhere else.

## What You're Looking At

{{< nbas-image src="hcg_92-equ-37m.jpg" fullwidth="true"
caption="Bob Donahue, NBAS -- eQuinox 2, 37 min" >}}

Through modest amateur equipment, Stephan's Quintet reads through the eyepiece as a tight, faint
knot of smudges rather than a dramatic collision — a testament to how much
context those Hubble and Webb images are quietly supplying. NGC 7320, the
foreground interloper, is usually the easiest member to pick out even in
smaller scopes, simply because it's marginally brighter and slightly larger in angular size. 
The other four are individually faint (magnitude 13–15.5) and smaller, which is exactly
why the group's _combined_ light adds up to a deceptively bright-sounding
integrated magnitude of 11.1 while any single galaxy in it is a real challenge.
Dark skies and some aperture matter here — this is not a target that gives up
much to a quick glance from a light-polluted backyard.

Smart scopes narrow that gap considerably. Stacking even a modest integration
time — the eQuinox 2 image above is 37 minutes — starts separating the
individual galaxies and hinting at their shapes in a way that's genuinely hard
to achieve visually without a large-aperture Dobsonian and a very dark site.
But whatever you're using, the galaxies in the frame are the same ones Stephan
picked out with an 80 cm mirror in 1877, the same ones the Burbidges measured
in 1961, that Hubble resolved around 2000, and now Webb in 2022. 
Each generation of instrument told us something the last one couldn't. 
Yours will show you something too.

## Finding and Observing It

{{< finder-chart-set >}}

Stephan's Quintet sits about half a degree southwest of NGC 7331, a bright,
easy-to-find unbarred spiral in Pegasus — star-hop to NGC 7331 first (it's a
rewarding target in its own right), then nudge half a degree to pick up the
Quintet's tight little knot nearby. Because the group is so compact, higher
magnification helps more than usual once you've located the field; at low
power the whole group can look like a single fuzzy smudge that's easy to
dismiss as nothing. Give it aperture, give it time, and give it magnification,
and it starts resolving into the individual pieces of the story above.

## Wrapup

Stephan's Quintet is a good reminder of what "a closer look" is really about.
The raw view — five smudges of light in a small patch of Pegasus — hasn't
changed since 1877. What's changed is everything we've learned to see within
it: an accidental foreground galaxy that briefly threatened to upend redshift
cosmology, a genuine sixth member hiding just outside the frame, and a
present-day collision generating a shock wave bigger than our own galaxy. None
of that is visible at the eyepiece. All of it is true anyway, and it's waiting
in the same spot of sky whether you're pointing an 80 cm reflector at it in
1877 or a smart scope at it tonight.
