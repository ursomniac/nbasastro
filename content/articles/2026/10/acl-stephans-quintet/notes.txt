## 2. What you're actually looking at

- The five visually grouped galaxies: NGC 7317, 7318A, 7318B, 7319, 7320 — plus a probable sixth,
  NGC 7320C, dangling off to the east on an old tidal tail
- Discovered 1877 by Édouard Stephan at the Marseille Observatory — decades *before* anyone knew
  galaxies were separate objects from our own Milky Way; he had no framework for what he'd actually
  found
- Located in Pegasus, ~270 million light-years away (for the true group)

**Visual ideas:**
- [ ] Hero image: Hubble WFC3 portrait (2009, "Galactic Wreckage" release) or the JWST composite —
      both well-documented NASA/ESA public releases

## 3. First reveal — NGC 7320 isn't really there

- NGC 7320 is a foreground galaxy roughly 7x closer than the other four — a chance line-of-sight
  alignment, not a real companion
- Confirmed via redshift by the Burbidges in 1961
- Hubble's own press materials call the name "Stephan's Quintet" itself "a bit of a misnomer"
- Nice beat: even NASA/ESA acknowledge the name doesn't quite hold up

**Visual ideas:**
- [ ] Simple distance comparison diagram — NGC 7320 in the foreground, the true group far behind it,
      showing why they only *look* grouped from Earth's vantage point

## 4. Second reveal — the group itself is mid-collision (or two)

- NGC 7318B is a genuine intruder, currently ramming through the group core at ~800 km/s
  (roughly 1.8 million mph) — a first-time passage, still ongoing
- But there's evidence of an *earlier* intruder too: NGC 7320C, off to the east, appears to have
  already punched through once before, stripping gas from the others and leaving two long parallel
  tidal tails of very different ages (~100 million years vs. up to a billion years old)
- So this isn't witnessing one collision — it's wreckage from at least two, staggered across time
- This is probably the single best "I did not know that" beat in the whole piece — give it room

## 5. The shockwave — and a possible new galaxy being born

- The NGC 7318B collision has produced one of the largest shockwaves ever observed — several times
  the size of the Milky Way, heating intergalactic hydrogen gas to millions of degrees, first
  detected via radio (1972), later confirmed in X-ray and infrared
- Counter to the usual script (collisions like this normally trigger a firestorm of new star
  formation), recent ALMA/JWST work found something stranger: the shock is shredding a giant gas
  cloud, possibly slamming two others together, and appears to be assembling the raw material for
  a brand-new dwarf galaxy out of the wreckage
- Strong "wonder" peak for the piece — the wreck is building something, not just destroying it

**Visual ideas:**
- [ ] Chandra X-ray + optical composite showing the shock (well-documented public NASA/CXC image)
- [ ] JWST MIRI image specifically, since that's the instrument that revealed the dust-shrouded
      detail — good pairing with a visible-light image to show what MIRI added that wasn't visible

## 6. The mechanics aside — the discordant-redshift controversy

- When Geoffrey and Margaret Burbidge measured NGC 7320's redshift in 1961 and found it wildly
  different from its apparent neighbors, it became a founding piece of evidence in a real (if now
  largely abandoned) cosmological controversy
- Halton Arp used cases like this to argue redshift might not reliably indicate distance at all —
  briefly a serious challenge to the redshift-distance relation underpinning the expanding universe
- Frame as a "how do we actually know" mechanics section, placed after the wonder has already
  landed, per the object-led structure

**Visual ideas:**
- [ ] Simple table: each galaxy's measured recession velocity, side by side, making the

## 7. Legacy — first of its kind

- First compact group of galaxies ever discovered — decades before Hubble established that spiral
  nebulae were separate galaxies at all
- Became the prototype for an entire class of object: Paul Hickson's 1982 catalog of compact
  groups (Stephan's Quintet = Hickson Compact Group 92) is directly descended from this one
  discovery
- Selected by NASA as one of the five targets for JWST's first official science image release —
  a 145-year-old discovery still doing first-of-its-kind duty in 2022
