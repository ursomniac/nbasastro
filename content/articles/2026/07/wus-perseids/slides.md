## Slide 2 — What Is a Meteor Shower?

**Title:** Earth Crosses a River of Debris

**Slide content:**
- Every year in August, Earth passes through a stream of particles shed by a comet
- Particles (mostly sand-grain to pea-sized) hit the atmosphere at ~59 km/s
- Friction heats them to thousands of degrees — they glow, then burn up ~100 km up
- All traces appear to radiate from one point in the sky: the **radiant**
- These are NOT falling stars — they never reach the ground

**Layout:** Split — diagram on right (Earth orbit crossing debris trail), bullets on left
**Image:** Diagram of Earth's orbit intersecting comet debris stream (NASA/ESA style)

**Speaker notes:**
> What actually happens during a meteor shower? Once a year, Earth's orbit crosses a band of debris left behind by a comet — essentially a river of tiny particles orbiting the Sun along the same path. When one of those particles hits our atmosphere, the friction heats it to thousands of degrees in a fraction of a second. It glows, ablates, and burns up completely about 100 kilometers above your head. Most of the particles are smaller than a marble. We call them meteors — or falling stars — but they never fall all the way. What you're seeing is the air glowing around the particle, not the particle itself.

## Slide 3 — Meet the Parent: Comet 109P/Swift-Tuttle

**Title:** The Source: Comet 109P/Swift-Tuttle

**Slide content:**
- Discovered 1862 (Lewis Swift and Horace Tuttle, independently)
- Orbital period: ~133 years; nucleus ~26 km across — one of the largest known
- Last appeared: 1992 | Next return: ~2125
- Sheds debris every orbit; trail has spread around the entire path
- Earth hits this trail every August — the same debris, year after year

**Layout:** Image of comet or artist's render + bullets
**Image:** 1992 photo of 109P/Swift-Tuttle or artist's illustration; orbit diagram as inset

**Speaker notes:**
> The Perseids come from Comet 109P/Swift-Tuttle, independently discovered by Lewis Swift and Horace Tuttle in 1862. It orbits the Sun about every 133 years — we last saw it return in 1992, and it won't be back until approximately 2125. But every time it passes, it sheds material — dust, ice, and rock fragments — which spread out along its entire orbital path. Earth crosses that path every August, which is why the Perseids happen like clockwork, year after year. The nucleus is roughly 26 kilometers across, making it one of the largest known short-periodic comets — considerably bigger than Halley's Comet.

## Slide 5 — When to Watch

**Title:** Mark Your Calendar

**Slide content:**
- **Active period:** July 14 – September 1 (background rates only near start/end)
- **Good nights:** August 10–14 (above half the peak rate)
- **Peak:** Night of August 12–13; predicted maximum ~14:53 UTC August 13
- **Best viewing window:** After midnight through astronomical twilight (~5 am)
- Radiant rises in the NE around 10–11 pm — rates pick up after midnight
- Don't wait until the maximum — Aug 11-12 is also excellent

**Layout:** Simple horizontal timeline graphic with dates marked; "PEAK" highlighted; "GOOD" range indicated
**Image:** Custom timeline graphic; clock showing midnight–dawn highlighted

**Speaker notes:**
> The Perseids are active from mid-July all the way to September 1st, but rates are low near the boundaries. You want to be out August 10th through the 14th for above-average activity. The predicted peak is around 3 AM UTC on August 13th — that translates to late night on the 12th for North America and early morning the 13th for Europe. The best window any night is after midnight, when the radiant is higher in the sky and you're on the leading face of the Earth sweeping into the debris stream. Rates before midnight can still be worth watching, but they're maybe a quarter to a third of what you'll see before dawn.

## Slide 6 — Finding the Radiant

**Title:** Where Do They Come From?

**Slide content:**
- Radiant: Perseus constellation — near the Double Cluster (h and χ Persei)
- Rises in the NE around 10 pm; climbs through the night; highest at dawn
- **Don't stare at the radiant** — meteors appear shorter near it
- Look 40–90° away from Perseus; scan broadly across the sky
- All Perseids trace back to the same point if you follow their tails

**Layout:** Star chart showing Perseus with radiant marked; arrow to "look here instead" region
**Image:** Wide-field star chart of Perseus region; ideally a fisheye all-sky shot with traces converging on radiant

**Speaker notes:**
> All Perseid meteors appear to radiate from a single point in Perseus — the radiant. It's near the famous Double Cluster, which is easy to find in binoculars. The radiant rises in the northeast around 10 or 11 pm and climbs higher through the night until it's nearly overhead by dawn — which is one reason rates keep improving as the night goes on. Here's a common mistake: people think you should stare at the radiant. Actually you want to look away from it, 40 to 90 degrees off. Meteors near the radiant appear very short because they're coming almost straight toward you. The long, dramatic streaks happen away from Perseus, where the meteors cross your field of view. So face the northeast, let the radiant sit in your peripheral vision, and scan broadly.

## Slide 7 — What You'll See

**Title:** Fast, Bright, and Colorful

**Slide content:**
- **Rate:** ~90/hour under ideal conditions (ZHR); 40–60/hour from a dark site
- **Speed:** 59 km/s — among the fastest of all major showers
- High speed = brilliant, short-lived streaks; frequent persistent trains (glowing smoke trails)
- **Fireballs:** Perseids produce more fireballs than almost any other shower
- **Colors:** white core, yellow or green wings, orange/red terminal flares
- Some will leave a glowing smoke train that persists for seconds or even minutes

**Layout:** Full-frame Perseid fireball photo; bullet list overlay or adjacent column
**Image:** Spectacular fireball with persistent train; ideally showing color

**Speaker notes:**
> At peak, under a truly dark sky — no light pollution, no Moon — you can expect around 90 meteors per hour. From a suburban dark site, 40 to 60 is more realistic. Don't count them obsessively; just lie back and enjoy the show. The Perseids are fast — 59 kilometers per second — which is part of what makes them so visually dramatic. Fast meteors tend to be bright and to leave persistent trains: glowing ionized trails that can hang in the sky for seconds, sometimes minutes, twisting slowly in the upper atmosphere as high-altitude winds pull them apart. The Perseids are also among the most prolific fireball producers of any shower, so on a good night you'll likely see at least one or two that make you gasp. Watch for colors: a brilliant white core, sometimes green or yellow wings, and often an orange or red flash at the end.

## Slide 8 — How to Watch

**Title:** You Don't Need a Telescope

**Slide content:**
- **Equipment:** None — naked eye only; binoculars and telescopes actually hurt (too narrow)
- **Most important factor:** Dark sky, away from city lights
- Allow **20+ minutes** for dark adaptation — no phone screens during this time!
- Recline in a lawn chair or lie on a blanket; comfortable neck = more meteors
- Dress **warmer than you expect** — August nights can be surprisingly cool after midnight
- Meteors come in bursts, not steadily — be patient through quiet stretches

**Layout:** Photo of person reclining in a lawn chair under a dark sky; bullets alongside
**Image:** Person lying under stars with a meteor overhead; warm clothing visible

**Speaker notes:**
> This is the easiest observation in astronomy — you need absolutely nothing. A telescope or binoculars will actually hurt you, because they narrow your field of view. You want to see as much sky as possible. The single most important factor is darkness. Drive away from city lights if you can — even 30 to 45 minutes from a city makes a significant difference. Give your eyes at least 20 minutes to adapt to the dark, and resist the urge to check your phone during that time: even a glance at a bright screen resets your dark adaptation clock. Recline — a lawn chair or sleeping bag on the ground is ideal. You don't want to be craning your neck all night. And layer up. August nights feel warm at sunset, but it drops off after midnight, especially at altitude or in rural areas.

## Slide 9 — Photographing the Perseids

**Title:** Capturing Meteors on Camera

**Slide content:**
- **Lens:** Wide angle — 14–24mm ideal; the wider, the better
- **Aperture:** f/2.8 or faster (f/1.8, f/1.4 if you have it)
- **ISO:** 1600–3200; experiment with your specific camera
- **Exposure:** 15–25 seconds (keeps stars as points; avoids saturation)
- Point toward Perseus — or 45–90° away for longer streaks
- Remote shutter / interval timer essential (shoot continuously all night)
- Expect to delete ~95% of frames; one keeper per hour is a good night

**Layout:** Split — example Perseid photo on left; settings summary as simple graphic on right
**Image:** Perseid trail across Milky Way; inset of camera settings or DSLR on tripod in foreground

**Speaker notes:**
> If you want to try photography, the Perseids are a forgiving subject. You don't need exotic gear — any DSLR or mirrorless camera with a manual mode and a wide lens will do. Shoot at f/2.8 or faster if you can, ISO 1600 to 3200, 15 to 25 second exposures. Set up a remote shutter or interval timer and shoot continuously — meteors are unpredictable and you want the camera running all night. Point toward Perseus or 45 to 90 degrees away from it for longer trails. You'll delete most frames, but a good Perseid photo — a bright fireball cutting across the Milky Way — is absolutely worth the patience. The new moon in 2026 means no lunar glow to wash out your shots, which is ideal.
