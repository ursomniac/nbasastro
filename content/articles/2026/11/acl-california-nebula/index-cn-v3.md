## Introduction

{{< nbas-image src="banner.png" fullwidth="true" >}}

{{< object-specs align="right" >}}
The California Nebula is one of the largest emission nebulae in the northern sky -- about two and a
half degrees long, five full Moons laid end to end -- and one of the easiest to photograph. A smart
scope will show its red glow in a few minutes. It is also in the wrong place. The well-known
emission nebulae crowd along the band of the Milky Way: Orion, the Rosette, the Heart and Soul, the
North America, the Eagle, and the Lagoon.  This one sits in Perseus, twelve degrees off the galactic plane, with no bright
cluster inside it and no obvious reason to be glowing at all.

The reason turns out to be a single star, and the star is not from around here.

## Discovery

{{< nbas-image src="ee-barnard.jpg" width="250" align="right"
caption="E.E. Barnard (1857–1923), Public domain, via Wikimedia Commons"
alt="Headshot of E.E. Barnard, astronomer." >}}
Edward Emerson Barnard found the nebula in the mid-1880s from Nashville, Tennessee, using a 6-inch
refractor at Vanderbilt University. He found it by eye. That is worth a second look, because the
California Nebula has a reputation today as a hard visual object: large, diffuse, and so low in
surface brightness that many observers with far bigger telescopes never see it.

The trouble is the color of its light. The nebula shines mostly in hydrogen-alpha, a deep red at
656 nanometers, and dark-adapted eyes respond poorly to red. A camera sensor has no such weakness,
which is why long-exposure photographs later showed the full outline -- and why the nebula got its
name from photographs, where its shape resembles the state's coastline. What Barnard saw with a
6-inch lens was the faintest part of what your camera records easily.

## One Star, Passing Through

Most emission nebulae are lit from within. A cloud collapses, a cluster of hot young stars forms,
and their ultraviolet light makes the leftover hydrogen glow. The Orion Nebula works this way, with
the Trapezium at its heart.

The California Nebula has no Trapezium. Its light comes from Xi Persei, also called Menkib, the
fourth-magnitude star just off the nebula's edge. Xi Persei is an O-type giant with a surface
temperature near 35,000 K, among the hottest stars visible to the unaided eye. Its ultraviolet
output strips electrons from hydrogen atoms across the face of the cloud. When the electrons are
recaptured, the atoms emit light at a few exact wavelengths, the red hydrogen-alpha line strongest
among them.

Xi Persei does not appear to have formed in this cloud. It has an unusually high velocity and lies
well away from the Perseus OB2 association, the nearest group of stars of its kind, and it may be a
runaway -- a star thrown out of its birthplace, either by a close encounter in a crowded young
cluster or by the supernova of a companion. If so, the nebula is a matter of timing. A star
powerful enough to light a cloud is passing a cloud, and for a while the cloud glows.

It would not be the only such case. AE Aurigae, a runaway ejected from the Orion Nebula region
about 2.5 million years ago, now lights the Flaming Star Nebula in Auriga, a short hop to the east.

Nor will it remain the nebula's beacon for very long: at ~30 M☉ its
whole lifetime is only about 7 million years, and it has already
used most of that. Within the next million years or so it will reach
the end. It may explode as a supernova, though stars this massive
sometimes collapse into a black hole with no explosion at all. If
it does explode, it would peak near magnitude –9: about 50 times
brighter than Venus, and about 1/30th the brightness of the Full
Moon.

## The Sleeping Giant Behind It

The glowing gas is only a skin. In 2009 a team mapping dust across this part of the sky found that
the dark clouds behind the nebula form one enormous structure, about 260 light-years long and
holding roughly 100,000 times the mass of the Sun. They named it the California Molecular Cloud,
after the nebula on its edge.

That makes it a match for the Orion A cloud, the most famous star factory in our part of the
galaxy, in both size and mass. Yet it contains more than ten times fewer young stars. Two clouds of
the same mass, at comparable distances, and one is busy while the other is almost idle. The
difference the team found was in how the gas is packed: the California cloud has only about a tenth
as much dense material as Orion, and dense gas is where stars form.

So the nebula's odd location has an answer. It sits away from the other bright nebulae because it
isn't one of their kind. Those mark places where a cloud has made massive stars. This marks a cloud
that mostly hasn't, caught in the light of a star from somewhere else. And it lies off the Milky
Way's band because it is close: a cloud only a few hundred light-years below the galactic plane
looks well out of the plane when it is this near to us.

How near is not settled. The cloud is measured at about 1,470 light-years. Xi Persei's older
parallax puts the star nearer 1,240, and many references give the nebula as 1,000. At those
distances its length works out to somewhere between 45 and 65 light-years.

## What You're Looking At

{{< nbas-image src="ngc_1499-s50-35m.jpg" fullwidth="true"
caption="Bob Donahue, NBAS, Seestar S50, 35 min, LP filter"
alt="Image of the California Nebula stretching across the entire image." >}}

The first thing to know is that you are not seeing all of it. A Seestar S50 frame covers roughly
0.7 by 1.2 degrees, and the nebula is 2.5 degrees long, so a single frame holds about half its
length. The red fills the image from edge to edge because the object is bigger than the window.

What the red traces is the surface of the molecular cloud where Xi Persei's light reaches it. The
glow is a thin lit layer, with the bulk of the cloud dark and unseen behind it. The streaks and
ridges are real structure in that surface: denser folds of gas that catch more of the star's light.

### A Galaxy Behind the Curtain

Near the western end of the nebula, about 1.2 degrees from its center, there is a faint smudge that
doesn't belong to it. IC 2005 is a galaxy of about 15th magnitude, a third of an arcminute across,
lying roughly 280 million light-years away. Its light has crossed all that distance and then passed
through the nebula's gas on the last thousand-odd light-years of the trip.

It is a hard catch: small, faint, and seen against a red background. In a smart-scope image it
would be a soft dot barely larger than a star. If your image covers the western end, it is worth a
look at full zoom.

## Finding and Observing It

{{< nbas-image src="xi-per.png" width="400" align="right" 
caption="Locating the nebula's location in Perseus between Mirfak and the Pleiades."
alt="A star map of the constellation Perseus with nearby bright stars and the Pleiades.  The nebula's illuminating star Xi Persei is marked and the position of the California Nebula." >}}

Start with Xi Persei, a fourth-magnitude star about 12 degrees north of the Pleiades. The nebula
runs along the star's northern side, about a degree away.   To locate the field, follow Perseus
from its brightest star, Mirfak along a slight arc to the Pleiades: Xi Per (Menkib) is just beyond
the half-way point.

From our latitude it passes almost overhead, reaching about 84 degrees of altitude, which is as
good as it gets for cutting through haze. It crosses the meridian around midnight in late November
and about two hours earlier each month after that, so by late January it is at its best in the
early evening.

### By Eye

Be ready for this one to beat you. The nebula is big and its light
is spread thin, and a telescope makes that worse: magnify a 2.5-degree
object and it overflows the field while each patch of it gets dimmer.
Barnard managed with a 6-inch refractor, under a sky darker than
most of us will ever stand beneath.

What helps is the reverse of the usual advice. Use the lowest power
and widest field you have, binoculars included, from the darkest
site you can reach. Observers who do see it mostly use a hydrogen-beta
filter, which passes the nebula's blue-green light at 486 nanometers
and blocks nearly everything else. Don't buy one for this alone.
If your club has one, borrow it.

If you see nothing, you're in good company, and the camera will show you what you missed.

### Smart Scopes

The nebula is easy to record and awkward to frame. Mosaic or framing mode on a Seestar S50 covers
about 1.4 by 2.5 degrees, enough for the whole outline. A filter that passes hydrogen-alpha helps a
great deal from light-polluted sites, since nearly all of the nebula's light is in that one line.

## Wrapup

The California Nebula looks like the other red clouds of the winter sky, and it is a different
thing. No cluster sits inside it. The cloud behind it is as massive as Orion's and has made a
fraction of the stars. Its light is on loan from one hot star that may simply be passing by.
Barnard saw it faintly with six inches of glass. A camera the size of a lunchbox will show you the
color he couldn't see -- and, with luck, a galaxy far behind it.
