## Introduction

{{< nbas-image src="banner.webp" fullwidth="true" >}}

Mars and Jupiter spend all of November working their way toward each other against the stars of Cancer and Leo, reaching their closest approach between **November 14 and 16**, when they close to as little as **1.1°–1.2°** apart.

## The View in the Solar System

{{< nbas-image src="orbits.webp" >}}

This diagram shows why the view from Earth can be deceiving: Mars and Jupiter only *look* close together because they happen to line up along nearly the same sightline this month. In reality they're roughly 350 million miles apart in space — Jupiter sits far beyond Mars in its own, much slower orbit. What we're seeing is a trick of perspective, not a close pass between the two planets themselves.

Because of the time of year, the pair is also a late-night-into-morning object rather than an early-evening one — look for them rising after midnight and climbing higher as dawn approaches, rather than expecting to spot them right after sunset.

## What You'll See

### Naked Eye

Both planets are easy naked-eye targets all month, and watching their positions shift night to night is half the fun. Mars starts November to Jupiter's west, then swings north of it around mid-month at closest approach, and finishes the month having pulled ahead to Jupiter's east. The two won't be done with each other after this, either: heading into 2027, both planets turn retrograde, and Mars very nearly catches back up to Jupiter again in late March.

Watch for a striking brightness contrast, too. Both planets brighten slightly over the course of the month — Mars from magnitude 0.85 to 0.51, Jupiter from -2.00 to -2.19 — but the gap between them stays dramatic throughout: on average, Jupiter outshines Mars by about 2.7–2.8 magnitudes, which works out to roughly **12 to 13 times brighter**, even sitting right next to each other in the sky.

### Binoculars

{{< nbas-image src="binoc-7x35.webp" 
caption="For most of the month, both planets will be in the same FOV with standard 7x35 binoculars." >}}

A pair of ordinary binoculars is all you need to hold both planets in the same field of view for most of November — no telescope required to appreciate how close they've drawn together.

### Small Telescopes

{{< nbas-image src="eyepiece-80mm-32mm.webp" 
caption="FOV with an 80mm telescope and a 32mm eyepiece" >}}

Even a small telescope at low power will comfortably frame both planets side by side for much of the month, making this a good target for a first look through a new scope.

{{< clear >}}
### Features on Mars

Don't expect much surface detail on Mars this time around — it's a small target all month, growing only from about 6.6″ to 7.9″ in apparent diameter between the start and end of November. At that size, Mars is best appreciated as a small, distinctly reddish disk rather than a source of visible surface features; save the detailed Mars observing for when it's larger and closer to Earth.

### Jupiter and the Galilean Satellites

Jupiter, by contrast, rewards a closer look even at modest magnification — its dark cloud bands are easy to pick out, and its four Galilean moons shift position from night to night. ### Jupiter and the Moon

As a bonus for early risers, the waning crescent Moon passes just **1°18′ south of Jupiter** on the morning of **November 30**, offering one more good photo opportunity to close out the month.
