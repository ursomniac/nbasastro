## 🪐 Mars and Jupiter Close In

{{< nbas-image src="eyepiece-80mm-32mm_hu_f330ded6f12109ba.webp" 
caption="Simulated FOV with an 80mm telescope and a 32mm eyepiece">}}

All month, Mars and Jupiter draw together in the pre-dawn sky in Leo, reaching their closest
approach on **November 15–16**, just **1.2°** apart -- easily in the same binocular field.
They're not actually close: Jupiter is roughly 350 million miles beyond Mars, and the pairing is
pure line-of-sight. The pair rises around 11 PM and is best placed in the hours before dawn. Late
in the month, Mars slides past Regulus, Leo's brightest star, and the Moon joins Jupiter on the
morning of **November 30**. The full story is in our
**[Planet Watch: Mars and Jupiter Dance](/articles/2026/11/planet-watch-mars-and-jupiter-dance/)**.

## 🔵 Uranus at Opposition, November 25

{{< nbas-image src="uranus-hyades-pleiades-finder.webp"
caption="Finding Uranus between the Hyades and Pleiades." >}}

Uranus reaches opposition on the evening of **November 25** (5:41 PM EST), in Taurus, up all
night. At 18.44 AU (about 1.71 billion miles), the sunlight bouncing off it to your eye
left Uranus about 2 hours and 33 minutes earlier. Its disk is just 3.8" across, but even
a small telescope at high power shows it as a tiny, pale blue-green dot that doesn't twinkle the
way the stars around it do. For finding it -- and the rest of the outer planets -- see
**[What's Up: Outer Planets](/articles/2026/10/whats-up-outer-planets/)**.

## 🌠 Leonid Meteor Shower - Night of November 17–18

{{< nbas-image src="leonids-1118-06utc.webp" fullwidth="true"
caption="Leonids radiant, 1:00 AM EST Nov 18 (06:00 UTC). Looking East." >}}

The Leonids peak on the night of **November 17–18**. Rates are modest in a typical year (around
10–15 per hour under dark skies), but this is the shower behind the great meteor storm of 1833.
The first-quarter Moon sets around 11:30 PM, leaving the best hours, after midnight until dawn,
dark, with Leo climbing in the east. More in our
**[Fall 2026: A Season of Meteor Showers](/articles/2026/09/fall-2026-a-season-of-meteor-showers/)**.

The **Taurids** are also active all month, with a weak, broad northern peak around November 12 --
low rates, but known for slow, bright fireballs, and with New Moon on the 9th, the sky will be dark.

## 🌅 For Early Risers

- **Nov 7:** A thin crescent Moon sits near Venus low in the east before dawn.
- **Nov 9:** Venus passes close to Spica.
- **Nov 17–25:** Mercury's best morning appearance of the year for Northern observers -- look low in
  the east-southeast about 45 minutes before sunrise. Greatest elongation is November 21.

## 📸 This Month's Image
{{< nbas-image src="pgc_143-s50-98m-square.webp" 
caption="PGC 143 the Wolf-Lundmark-Melotte Object, Bob Donahue, NBAS, Two Seestar S50s, 98 min" >}}

This galaxy doesn't make any of the popular amateur catalogs, which is a shame, because it's a very
interesting object (it's one of the six in our [Dwarf Galaxies](/articles/2026/11/dwarf-galaxies/)
article at the end of this month). WLM sits about 3.2 million light-years away, out on the fringe
of the Local Group -- not a satellite of the Milky Way or of M 31, but a member in its own right.
The clumpy brightness comes from star formation, and not star formation triggered by interactions
with other galaxies. Basically it's just "doing its own thing": nearby, somewhat faint, but still
reachable with smaller smart scopes -- if you have the patience, and if you like your galaxies
"irregular and lumpy".
