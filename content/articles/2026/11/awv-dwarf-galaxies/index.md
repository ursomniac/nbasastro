## Introduction

{{< nbas-image src="banner.webp" fullwidth="true" >}}

Point a smart scope at IC 1613 in Cetus and you won't see a pinwheel. You'll get a broad, faint
haze about half the width of the full Moon, with a scatter of brighter knots that don't line up
into arms or anything else. Point it at NGC 147 in Cassiopeia and you get the opposite: a smooth,
featureless oval, as tidy as a galaxy gets.

Both are small. Both are close, as galaxies go, at around two and a half million light-years.
So why does one look like something spilled across the sky, and the other like an egg?

The short answer is gas. In a small galaxy, the handful of places where gas is turning into stars
dominate the light, and wherever they happen to be is what you see. That's the ragged look. Take
the gas away, and the raggedness goes with it.

This article walks through seven dwarf galaxies in that order: three quiet ones, two in the middle
of a burst of star formation, and two that have almost nothing left to make stars.

---

## What Are Dwarf Galaxies?

### Small Is a Size, Ragged Is a Shape

"Dwarf" and "irregular" get used to describe galaxies as if they mean the same thing. They don't. "Dwarf" is about
size and brightness. "Irregular" is about shape. The Large Magellanic Cloud is irregular but big
enough that calling it a dwarf is a stretch. NGC 147 is unquestionably a dwarf, and there's
nothing irregular about it.

Nor is there a sharp line between a dwarf and a regular galaxy. The most-used definition, from
Gustav Tammann in 1994, is simply a brightness cut: a galaxy fainter than a set absolute
magnitude, and more spread out than a globular cluster, counts as a dwarf. Others draw the line by
how fast the galaxy rotates. Either way, it's a convention. A 2009 review of the Local Group's
dwarfs by Eline Tolstoy, Vanessa Hill and Monica Tosi puts it plainly: "There is no clear break
which distinguishes a dwarf from a larger galaxy."[^tolstoy] A galaxy just over the line, like the
Large Magellanic Cloud, can share nearly everything with the dwarfs except the label.

### Where the Tuning Fork Runs Out

{{< nbas-image src="fig-tuning-fork-dwarf.svg" fullwidth="true"
alt="Hubble's tuning fork diagram extended to the right: Sa, Sb, Sc and their barred versions, then Sd, Sm and Im added by de Vaucouleurs, fading out. WLM, IC 1613, NGC 1156, IC 10 and NGC 1569 are all marked at the barred irregular end, IBm. NGC 147 and NGC 185 sit in a separate box below S0, labeled dwarf spheroidal or dwarf elliptical, with no slot on Hubble's fork."
caption="Hubble's tuning fork, extended. Five of this article's galaxies share one label at the far end; the other two have no slot at all. Schematic: the shapes are not to scale." >}}

Our [Peculiar Galaxies](/articles/2026/11/peculiar-galaxies/) article looked at Hubble's tuning fork and the galaxies it
couldn't hold because something had happened to them. Dwarfs fall off the fork for a different
reason: it simply runs out.

Hubble's spirals ended at Sc, loosely wound arms around a small center. Gérard de Vaucouleurs
later extended the sequence: Sd, with fragmentary arms; Sm, with only a hint of spiral structure,
like the Large Magellanic Cloud; and Im, with no obvious structure at all, like the Small
Magellanic Cloud. The "m" stands for Magellanic. There's no gap between a ragged spiral and an
irregular galaxy, just a fade.

Five of our seven sit at that faint end: WLM, IC 1613 and NGC 1156 are Magellanic barred
irregulars, and IC 10 and NGC 1569 are listed as barred or blue compact irregulars. One label covers a galaxy in an extreme burst of star
formation and one that's barely ticking over. The classification records the shape. It says
nothing about what's going on inside.

The word "irregular" is also confusing. Hubble had two kinds.
Irr I were the Magellanic types, the ones here. Irr II were smooth, dusty, chaotic-looking
galaxies, many of which turned out to be collisions in progress, and they belong to the other
article.

### The Smooth Ones

The other two, NGC 147 and NGC 185, carry labels like "dSph/dE": dwarf spheroidal, or dwarf
elliptical. Authors use the two terms for overlapping groups, and you'll see both. The name
"dwarf elliptical" suggests a scaled-down version of a giant elliptical like M87, but the
astronomer John Kormendy has argued for decades that they aren't. Their structure says they are
"related to disks," continuous with the faded disks of S0 galaxies, rather than small
ellipticals. His case rests mostly on structure: plot size against surface brightness and total
brightness, and these galaxies line up with disks, not with ellipticals.

Their motions point the same way. In 2010, measurements of the stars in NGC 147 and NGC 185 found
that both rotate, at up to 17 and 15 kilometers per second, while the stars' random motions within
the galaxies run about 16 and 24 km/s. That's not far from IC 1613, whose gas rotates at up to 
about 25 kilometers per second. The researchers suggested that galaxies like these are transformed 
or stripped versions of gas-rich, rotating galaxies.

That fits the story here. They look like the end of a process, not a different kind of object.

## Why Small Galaxies Look Ragged

A big spiral is a well-organized machine. Its gravity is strong enough to hold a thin, rotating
disk, and spiral density waves sweep through that disk and line up star formation along the arms.

A dwarf has much less gravity to work with, and it shows. Star formation happens where it
happens, in a few big clumps, and in a galaxy this small, a few clumps of young blue stars are
most of the light. That gives it the ragged look.

It isn't for lack of order underneath, though. WLM's gas is rotating, and astronomers can model it as a disk
tilted about 74 degrees to our line of sight. IC 1613's gas rotates too, slowly. These aren't
shapeless blobs. They're really small disks being knocked around from the inside.

They are _thick_ disks, though, and the reason is worth a moment. The gas in any galaxy is doing two
things at once: sweeping around the center with the whole disk, and jostling about at random,
cloud against cloud. The jostling turns out to be about the same in big galaxies and small ones,
roughly 5 to 15 kilometers per second. What differs is the sweep. A big spiral like the Milky Way
rotates at more than 200 kilometers per second, so the jostling is a small wobble on a fast,
orderly orbit, and the disk settles thin, like spinning a ball of dough into a pizza.  A faint dwarf rotates at a
few tens of kilometers per second, so the jostling inside it is a big share of all its motion, and
the disk can't settle: it stays puffed up. Measured, the gas disks of faint dwarfs come out about
60 percent as thick as they are wide, compared to about 20 percent for the star disks of large spirals.[^thick]

{{< nbas-image src="fig-disk-thickness.svg" fullwidth="true"

Right, a faint dwarf: a short arrow labeled rotation a few tens of kilometers per second, the same jostling marker, and a thick, puffy disk."
caption="Why dwarfs can't keep thin disks. The random jostling of gas is about the same everywhere; the rotation isn't. Schematic: drawn at the same width, though the real galaxies differ greatly in size." >}}

That's the _shape_ of the disk, set by motion. The ragged texture is a separate thing, set by star
formation.

And the knocking is visible. In IC 1613, radio maps of the hydrogen show it riddled with shells
and holes, 200 to 300 parsecs across and expanding at 10 to 20 kilometers per second, blown by
the winds and supernovae of young stars. There are giant holes several hundred parsecs across,
and one roughly a kiloparsec wide that is too large for the star formation observed nearby to
explain. In a big spiral, those blasts are local events in a much heavier disk. In a dwarf, they
can rearrange the galaxy entirely.

At some point, however, the gas runs out, or gets taken from it through stripping from encounters with other galaxies. 
Without gas there's no new star formation, the blue clumps fade over time as the stars evolve, and what's left is 
an evenly spread population of old stars. Smooth. Quiet. That's NGC 147.

The famous examples, the Large and Small Magellanic Clouds, are out of reach from New England.
Barnard's Galaxy in Sagittarius is the summer one. The seven here are the ones up on late-autumn
evenings.

All but two of these galaxies are members of the Local Group, and that's no accident. Dwarfs give
off little light in total, so even nearby ones are hard targets; at ten times the distance, most
would be out of reach. (Surface brightness, oddly, doesn't fade with distance. A galaxy twice as
far away is a quarter as bright, but it also covers a quarter of the sky area, so each square
arcminute looks the same. What shrinks is the total light and the size on the sky.)

Within the Local Group, they sort into three kinds of neighborhood. NGC 147 and NGC 185 are
satellites of the Andromeda Galaxy, which we covered in the
[Andromeda family article](/articles/2026/09/the-andromeda-family-of-galaxies/). IC 10 is 18
degrees from Andromeda on the sky but only about 800,000 light-years from it in space, close
enough that it's often counted among Andromeda's satellites too -- at least in terms of proximity;
it's still up in the air if it is a physical satellite to Andromeda, or just a next-door neighbor. 
IC 1613 and WLM belong to neither big galaxy. They're out on their own, members of the Local Group in their own right.

### Quiet -- WLM: Out on the Edge

{{< nbas-image src="pgc_143-s50-98m-square.webp" width="300" align="right"
caption="Wolf-Lundmark-Melotte (PGC 143), Bob Donahue, NBAS, Seestar S50, 98 min"
alt="Smart scope image of the Wolf-Lundmark-Melotte (PGC 143) in Cetus" >}}

The Wolf-Lundmark-Melotte galaxy carries three names because it took three people to figure it
out. Max Wolf found it on a photographic plate in 1909. Its nature as a separate galaxy was
established in 1926, and credited to Knut Lundmark and Philibert Melotte.

WLM sits on the outer edge of the Local Group, about a million parsecs (3.3 million light-years)
from both the Milky Way and Andromeda. Its nearest neighbor, the small Cetus dwarf, is about
250,000 parsecs away. If any galaxy in the Local Group should be undisturbed, it's this one. It doesn't appear to have had 
any mergers to speak of, and contains one old globular cluster.

So it's the baseline. WLM is patchy for no other reason than that's how dwarfs make stars on their
own: no collision, no crowd. Its long, narrow shape is the tilted disk mentioned above, seen at a steep
angle, and the knots along it are its star-forming regions, scattered with no arms to line them up.
You'd never call it a disk from the picture. The evidence is in radio measurements of its
hydrogen: one end is moving toward us and the other away, the signature of a disk turning.

Even here, though, there's a twist. A 2026 study found that WLM is losing gas, pushed out by the
thin intergalactic gas it's moving through. Isolated, it turns out, isn't the same as untouched.

{{< clear >}}

### Quiet -- NGC 1156: Alone in Aries

{{< nbas-image src="ngc_1156-equ-11m-square.webp" width="300" align="right"
caption="NGC 1156, Bob Donahue, NBAS, eQuinox 2, 11 min"
alt="Smart scope image of NGC 1156 in Aries" >}}

NGC 1156 makes WLM look sociable. It's a Magellanic-type irregular in Aries, about 25 million
light-years away (7.6 megaparsecs), well outside the Local Group, and its nearest sizable
neighbors are more than 10 degrees away on the sky. At its distance, that puts them at least
4 million light-years off. When a radio survey mapped the hydrogen around it looking for company,
it found just one: a tiny dwarf with about a thousandth of NGC 1156's gas and star formation.

And yet it's busy. Its outline is boxy, its brightness uneven, and its bright blue patches mark
active star formation, with no neighbor around to set it off. If WLM is the baseline, NGC 1156 is
the proof that a dwarf left entirely to itself still turns out ragged.

There's order underneath here too: its stars and gas rotate together, about the same axis. Oddly,
that axis doesn't line up with the bar that dominates its light. William Herschel found it in 1786.

{{< clear >}}

### Quiet -- IC 1613: Blowing Holes in Itself

{{< nbas-image src="c_51-s50-square-92m.webp" width="300" align="right"
caption="IC 1613 (C 51), Bob Donahue, NBAS, Seestar S50, 92 min"
alt="Smart scope image of Caldwell 51 in Cetus" >}}

IC 1613, also found by Max Wolf, in 1906, is the largest of the seven on the sky, about 16
arcminutes across. It's also the faintest per square arcminute, which is the number that
matters for a diffuse object. Large doesn't always mean easy to observe.

At about 2.4 million light-years (730,000 parsecs), it's a near neighbor of ours, though nowhere
near Andromeda: it sits off on its own in Cetus. It has an unusually small amount of dust, both in the
galaxy and along our line of sight to it. That's made it a favorite of astronomers measuring distances: 
with little dust to dim its stars, its Cepheid variables give a clean reading, and IC 1613 has been 
one of the galaxies used to calibrate the Cepheid distance scale.

It's also the clearest example of the mechanism in this article. Those holes and shells in its
hydrogen are the work of its own young stars: the galaxy is rearranging itself from the inside.

{{< clear >}}

{{< nbas-image src="ic1613-halpha-ne-crop.webp" width="350" align="left"
caption="The northeast part of IC 1613 in the red light of glowing hydrogen (H-alpha), with the light of ordinary stars subtracted. North is up, east is left.  Credit: LITTLE THINGS survey (Hunter et al. 2012), via NRAO."
alt="Black-and-white image of glowing gas in IC 1613: a cluster of bright, overlapping rings and bubbles, some complete loops and some broken arcs, with a few bright knots." >}}
The radio maps need a radio telescope, but you can see the same process in visible light by
looking through a filter that passes only the deep-red glow of hydrogen gas lit up by hot young
stars, called hydrogen-alpha. In that light, IC 1613 is full of bubbles. One survey of its glowing
gas picked out 17 of these superbubbles, 110 to about 300 parsecs across, nearly every one with a
group of young stars inside. The stars' winds, and probably a supernova or two, blew them out, and
some are still expanding at more than 40 kilometers per second. The busiest region is the
northeast, shown here. The bright knots and arcs on the left side of our smart-scope image are
the same region.

{{< clear >}}

### Boiling -- IC 10: The Starburst Behind Our Own Dust

{{< nbas-image src="ic_10-s50-272m-square.webp" width="300" align="right"
caption="IC 10, Bob Donahue, NBAS, Seestar S50, 272 min"
alt="Smart scope image of IC 10 in Cassiopeia" >}}

IC 10 is the nearest starburst galaxy to us, and the only one in the Local Group. It's forming
stars hard enough that it has a higher density of Wolf-Rayet stars, massive stars in a short and
violent late stage, than either the LMC or SMC.

It's also one of the hardest galaxies in this list to see, but that has nothing to do with the
galaxy itself. IC 10 sits only about three degrees from the plane of the Milky Way, so we're looking 
at it through a thick layer of our own galaxy's dust. That dust dims and reddens it, and for decades it
made IC 10's distance uncertain enough that even its membership in the Local Group was in question.
Current measurements put it about 715,000 parsecs away, 2.3 million light-years, firmly a member.

Lewis Swift found it in 1887. It took 272 minutes, using two Seestar S50 telescopes, to give up
what's in this image. IC 10 also appeared in our [Andromeda family article](/articles/2026/09/the-andromeda-family-of-galaxies/).

{{< clear >}}

### Boiling -- NGC 1569: The Extreme One, and Not Alone

{{< nbas-image src="ngc_1569-equ-26m-square.webp" width="300" align="right"
caption="NGC 1569, Bob Donahue, NBAS, eQuinox 2, 26 min"
alt="Smart scope image of NGC 1569 in Camelopardalis" >}}

NGC 1569 is well beyond the Local Group too: about 11 million light-years, more than four times
farther than the Local Group members. It's here because it's the most extreme example of a dwarf in a burst.
Over the last hundred million years it has been forming stars at roughly a hundred times the
Milky Way's rate.

At its heart are two super star clusters, each with a mass comparable to a globular cluster in
our own galaxy. One of them, cluster A, turns out to be two clusters close together, one young
and one older.

The distance was a story of its own. For years NGC 1569 was assumed to be about 2.2 megaparsecs
away. In 2008, Hubble images of its brightest red giant stars put it at 3.36 megaparsecs, which
made it an even more extreme starburst than anyone had thought, and moved it into the IC 342
group of galaxies. It isn't isolated after all, and interactions with that group are thought to
have set off the burst.

It's also the easy one to observe: compact and bright for its size (and circumpolar from New England), 
it came through in only 26 minutes on the eQuinox 2.

{{< clear >}}

### Spent -- NGC 185: Almost Out

{{< nbas-image src="c_18-equ-32m-square.webp" width="300" align="right"
caption="NGC 185 (C 18), Bob Donahue, NBAS, eQuinox 2, 32 min"
alt="Smart scope image of NGC 185 (Caldwell 18) in Cassiopeia, a satellite of the Andromeda Galaxy" >}}

NGC 185 and NGC 147 are satellites of the Andromeda Galaxy, close to each other on the sky and
in space, and both turned up in our
[Andromeda family article](/articles/2026/09/the-andromeda-family-of-galaxies/). Here they're the
end of the line.

NGC 185 formed most of its stars early: about 70 percent of them are more than 12.5 billion years
old. It still holds a little gas, about 250,000 times the mass of the Sun, and some dust. In the
last few hundred million years it has made new stars, but only near its center. When Walter Baade
noticed "young blue objects" in it in 1951, they turned out to be star clusters.

That dust shows in the image: the small dark patch just off its center. Compare NGC 147 next,
where there's nothing of the kind.

So it's not quite empty. But it's smooth, because the little star formation it has left is too
small and too central to break up the even glow of billions of old stars.

{{< clear >}}

### Spent -- NGC 147: Empty

{{< nbas-image src="c_17-equ-32m-square.webp" width="300" align="right"
caption="NGC 147 (C 17), Bob Donahue, NBAS, eQuinox 2, 32 min"
alt="Smart scope image of NGC 147 (Caldwell 17) in Cassiopeia, a satellite of the Andromeda Galaxy" >}}

NGC 147 is what's left when the gas is gone. No hydrogen has been detected in it, and no dust.
Its star formation in the regions studied ended at least three billion years ago.

Oddly, it was the later bloomer of the pair. Only about 40 percent of its stars were in place
12.5 billion years ago, against 70 percent in NGC 185. The researchers who measured this read it
as a difference in when each galaxy fell in toward Andromeda: NGC 185 earlier, NGC 147 later, and
NGC 147 stripped harder once it got there.

Compare this image with IC 1613's. Similar size, similar distance. One is a scatter of knots in a
haze, the other an even oval. The difference is about a billion years of having nothing left to
make stars with.

## Finding and Observing These Galaxies

Most of these cover more sky than the galaxies in our last article, because they're so much
closer; physically, they're far smaller. And most of them are faint per square arcminute, which
is what matters for a diffuse object. A smart scope will go to each by name. If you're
star-hopping or using setting circles:

In late November, the sky is fully dark a little after 6 PM. Start with WLM: it's the most southern
of the set, peaking at 32 degrees around 7:30 PM, so catch it early. IC 10, NGC 147, NGC 185,
and IC 1613 all cross the meridian between about 7:50 and 8:35 PM, high and well placed. NGC 1156
follows around 10:30 PM, high in Aries. NGC 1569 is the latest, highest around midnight, but it's far enough north that it never sets from here.
Neither do IC 10, NGC 147, or NGC 185.

### At the Eyepiece

With typical amateur telescopes, just seeing a faint glow above the background is about as much
of a success as you can expect. The usual rules apply, dark skies and large aperture, and low
magnification and a wide field of view help too. Light pollution is the real killer: it washes
out low-surface-brightness objects more than anything else. If your
observing site has light domes in certain directions, try to make your observations when the objects are
as far away from them as possible.

Even with a large Dobsonian, most of these are challenging objects. That's not to say they're out
of reach for smaller telescopes, but they'll take a great deal of patience and all of your
observing skills.

**NGC 1569**: This is the one fortunate exception -- it is bright enough to be seen in almost all medium-aperture
scopes (down to 3-4"). With larger apertures more details become apparent: clumpings, and different parts of
the overall structure are revealed.

**NGC 185 (C 18)**: This is a nice challenge for the smaller scopes (about 3-4") -- it won't be _easy_ but a great test
of patience at the eyepiece.   At 6" you can get a sense of the overall form/shape, becoming more distinct with larger scopes.

**NGC 147 (C 17)**: This is the harder of the pair. It can be difficult even with 8-10" or larger,
even though its catalog values don't look _that_ different from NGC 185's. As a pair only 1° apart
(they fit together in a very wide-field eyepiece), they make a good compare-and-contrast. Best when
nearly overhead.

**IC 10**: Might be faintly visible with a 6" as an elongated patch, SE to NW. With 12" you might start to see
some graininess.

**WLM (PGC 143)**: Technically, this is reachable with 8-10".   Realistically, it might require 12-15", especially
considering its low declination -- even on the meridian, you're looking through almost twice as much atmosphere
than overhead, and that's attenuating the dim galaxy even more.

**IC 1613 (C 51)**: This might be the most challenging object on our list. It may take
apertures in the 15"+ range to see even a faint haze.

**NGC 1156**: Small but fairly bright for its size. It might be dim and diffuse in an
8", elongated SSW-NNE, with a roughly rectangular outline and uneven surface brightness. 
A magnitude 11.5 star sits just under an arcminute north of its center.

### Smart Scopes

Surface brightness is the thing to watch. NGC 1569 is compact and comes up fast. IC 1613 and WLM
are spread thin and reward long sessions. IC 10 needs the most patience of all, because our own
galaxy's dust is in the way: this galaxy deserves a very long exposure.

If your scope has a dual-band or hydrogen-alpha filter, there's an experiment built into this list.
The five gas-rich galaxies should show glowing gas, the red light of hydrogen lit by young stars.
The two spent ones should show little or nothing. That's the article's whole argument, in one
filter.

What to look for in each:

**WLM**: A long, narrow glow, the tilted disk. Look for blue knots strung along its length, and
pinkish patches among them: those are clouds of gas lit up by new stars. There are no arms to
find, and that's the point.

**IC 1613**: The challenge here is the faint, wide haze; it's the largest of the seven, about 16
arcminutes across, and needs a long session to fill in. On the northeast side, look for the
cluster of knots and arcs, the region of superbubbles in the hydrogen-alpha image above. A
hydrogen-alpha filter and a lot of time could start to show the bubbles themselves.

**NGC 1156**: A small, boxy, elongated glow. Look for its bright blue patches of star formation,
and for any pinkish knots among them. Don't mistake the star just north of its center for part of
the galaxy.

**IC 10**: The field tells half the story: a crowd of orange-tinted Milky Way stars, reddened by the
same dust that dims the galaxy. In the galaxy itself, look for a mottled core with pinkish knots
of star formation.

**NGC 1569**: Small and bright, an elongated bar of light with knots along it. This is the one where
hydrogen-alpha may pay off most: the burst has blown filaments of glowing gas out of the galaxy.
Mind the bright foreground star beside it.

**NGC 185**: A smooth, nearly round glow. The thing to find is the small dark patch near the center,
its remaining dust. Nothing else in it should look blue or clumpy.

**NGC 147**: A smooth, elongated glow, fainter in its outer parts than NGC 185, with no dust patch at
all. NGC 185 lies about a degree away; on the same night with the same exposure, the pair shows the
end of the sequence side by side.

## Wherever You're Looking From

None of these is a showpiece. Most are faint smudges that take a dark sky or a patient smart
scope to bring out. But line them up in this order and they tell one of the clearer stories in
the sky: small galaxies look the way they do because of what their gas is doing, or isn't
doing anymore.

The two at the end are about seven degrees from the Andromeda Galaxy. Next time you're there, swing over
to NGC 147 and NGC 185, and then find IC 1613 in Cetus. The difference between them is the whole
story.
