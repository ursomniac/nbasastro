## Introduction

{{< nbas-image src="banner.webp" fullwidth="true" >}}

Ask almost anyone with a telescope to name two galaxies colliding and you'll get the same answer: M 51,
the Whirlpool, with its small companion NGC 5195 hanging off the end of one arm. It's an iconic
picture, happily bright enough and large enough that it made Messier's list, and since then, one of
the standard go-tos of the spring observing season. Computer models of the pair have the
companion passing through the Whirlpool's disk 500 to 600 million years ago, and again within the
last 100 million. Nobody has watched any of it happen, and nobody will.

{{< nbas-image src="m_51-s30-54m-square.webp" width="300" align="left"
caption="M 51, Bob Donahue, NBAS, Seestar S30, 54 min" 
alt="Image of Messier 51 and NGC 5195, interacting galaxies in Canes Venatici" >}}
So how do we know what comes next? The same way you'd work out how people might age without waiting
eighty years: walk through a crowd and look at everyone at once. The sky has galaxies at every
stage of an encounter. Find enough of them, put them in order, and the film assembles itself.

Five great examples are up on November evenings. Two galaxies just after a close pass. Two more tangled
into one body with both cores still intact. One finished merger. One with a scar from a meeting
long ago. And one that looks every bit as roughed-up as the others, with nobody on record as
having done it.

These aren't "fall galaxies" in any meaningful sense. They happen to sit in the part of the sky
that's up now, in Pisces, Cetus, and Aquarius. Spring has its own set, and we'll get to those
after winter.

## Relationship Status: Complicated

### The Tuning Fork Has No Slot for an Event

{{< nbas-image src="fig-tuning-fork-peculiar.webp" fullwidth="true"
alt="Hubble's tuning fork diagram: ellipticals E0 to E7 along the handle, S0 at the junction, then two prongs of spirals (Sa, Sb, Sc) and barred spirals (SBa, SBb, SBc), with Sd and SBd added later. Six galaxies from this article are marked at their catalog types. NGC 520 sits in a separate box, off the fork entirely."
caption="Hubble's tuning fork, with this article's galaxies placed by their catalog types. Not to scale; the shapes are schematic." >}}

Edwin Hubble sorted galaxies by shape in 1926, and in 1936 drew his sorting as the diagram
everyone now calls the tuning fork: smooth ellipticals along the handle, then two prongs of
spirals, with and without a central bar. It's a filing system, and a very good one. Most bright
galaxies drop into one of its slots without argument.

What the fork doesn't have is a place for history. It records what a galaxy looks like today and
says nothing about how it got that way. Hubble knew it. He called the left end "early" and the
right end "late," and then warned his readers that "temporal connotations are made at one's
peril." It's tempting to read the diagram left to right as a life story, with ellipticals
flattening into spirals and some of them picking up a bar along the way. It isn't one.

Galaxies that had been through something landed in a catch-all bin at the end, the irregulars.
NGC 520, one of our five, was filed there for decades, under a subclass called "Irr II" that was
kept for smooth, dusty, chaotic-looking objects. The leading explanation for a long time didn't involve a
collision at all. It was an explosion in the core.

A word of warning about that word, because it trips people up. "Irregular" is also the name for
small, ragged, perfectly healthy galaxies like the Magellanic Clouds, which were never disturbed
by anything. Those are a different story, and they get their own article later this month.

### Arp's Atlas

In 1966 Halton Arp went the other way. Where Hubble had built his system from the galaxies that
fit, Arp collected 338 that didn't, and published them as the *Atlas of Peculiar Galaxies*. His
reasoning was that the misfits were where the physics was showing.

Three of our five are in it. NGC 520 is Arp 157. The pair NGC 7714 and NGC 7715 is Arp 284.
NGC 7252 is Arp 226, which Arp described as "Loops, filaments at various angles." NGC 660 and
NGC 337 aren't in the atlas at all, which is worth remembering when we get to them. (M 51 is: it's
Arp 85.)

### Lining Them Up

An atlas of odd galaxies is still just a pile. The idea that turned it into a story came from two
brothers, Alar and Juri Toomre. In 1972 they ran simple computer models of two disk galaxies
passing each other, and the models grew the same long tails and bridges that Arp had
photographed. Gravity alone was enough. No explosions needed.

{{< nbas-image src="Alar_Toomre.webp" width="150" align="right"
caption="Alar Toomre. AIP Emilio Segrè Visual Archives, John Irwin Slide Collection, CC BY-SA 4.0, via Wikimedia Commons" 
alt="Headshot of astronomer Alar Toomre." >}}
Five years later Alar Toomre took the next step. He picked eleven galaxies from the NGC catalog,
arranged them in order of how far along each collision seemed to be, and proposed what came at
the end: two spirals in, one elliptical out.

That list is now called the Toomre sequence. It opens with the Antennae and the Mice, two spring
pairs with long tails and their galaxies still clearly separate. NGC 520 sits seventh, in the
middle. NGC 7252 is eleventh and last.

Two of our five are in his list. The others aren't, but we can borrow his method and put ours in
the same kind of order, earliest stage first. One caution before we do. The order is a judgment
made from still pictures. We see each of these from one direction only, and Toomre's eleven are
thought to span more than a billion years between them.

### Do Galaxies Really Change Type?

Nobody has seen one do it. Everything here is pieced together from snapshots, from simulations,
and from comparing nearby galaxies with far more distant ones, whose light left them when the
universe was younger.

The best single piece of evidence is one of our five. In 1982 François Schweizer measured how the
light of NGC 7252 falls off from its center outward, and found that it follows the same curve
elliptical galaxies do. The catalogs already file it as an S0, at the junction of the fork, next to the ellipticals.
Two spiral galaxies went in. The thing that's coming out is something else.

Mergers aren't the only route. In crowded galaxy clusters, spirals plowing through the hot gas
between galaxies can have their own gas swept out of them. With nothing left to make new stars,
the arms fade, and what remains looks like an S0. That is one leading explanation for why
clusters are so full of S0s, though some S0s may be merger products too.

{{< nbas-image src="cartwheel-webb.webp" width="400" align="right"
caption="The Cartwheel Galaxy (ESO 350-40) from the James Webb Space Telescope. Credit: NASA, ESA, CSA, STScI"
alt="A ring galaxy seen almost face-on: a bright inner ring and a wide, clumpy outer ring joined by spoke-like streaks, with two small companion galaxies nearby." >}}
Other changes are temporary. The Cartwheel, a far-southern galaxy in Sculptor, took a head-on hit
from a smaller galaxy 200 to 300 million years ago and now wears a huge expanding ring. The ring
is expected to break up over the next few hundred million years and leave a spiral behind again.

And some encounters make new galaxies outright. At the end of each of NGC 7252's two tails sits a
small clump of gas and young stars with the makings of a dwarf galaxy. Both are too rich in heavy
elements to be old dwarfs that wandered in. They formed from the wreckage.

### Stage One -- NGC 7714 and NGC 7715: After the First Pass

{{< nbas-image src="ngc_7715-equ-89m-square.webp" width="300" align="right"
caption="NGC 7714 and NGC 7715 (Arp 284), Bob Donahue, NBAS, eQuinox 2, 89 min" >}}

This is the closest thing in our set to M 51: two galaxies, still clearly two, that have already
had their first meeting. Somewhere between 100 and 200 million years ago they passed close enough
to pull each other out of shape.

The larger one, NGC 7714, came away with a partial ring of older stars around one side and two
long tails. A bridge of stars runs from it to NGC 7715, the thin streak beside it. Gas is flowing
along that bridge into NGC 7714, and it's feeding a burst of star formation in the core that's
strong enough to dominate the galaxy's light. Visual observers describe the nucleus as looking
like a star.

In our image the bridge and the tails are the things to look for. They're faint, and they only
came up with a long exposure. The bright star crowding the frame is 16 Piscium, magnitude 5.7 and
about 106 light-years away. The galaxies behind it are nearly a million times farther.

### Stage Two -- NGC 520: Two Cores in One Body

{{< nbas-image src="ngc_520-equ-34m.webp" width="300" align="right"
caption="NGC 520 (Arp 157), Bob Donahue, NBAS, eQuinox 2, 34 min" >}}

Here the two galaxies have stopped being two. NGC 520 is a single lumpy body with a heavy dust
lane across the middle and a tail trailing off to one side. Arp called it the second-brightest
very disturbed galaxy in the sky.

What it actually is took a long time to settle. Was this one galaxy that had blown itself apart,
or two caught in the act? The explosion idea was still being argued in the mid-1970s. The
question closed in 1990, when the astronomers Stanford and Balcells photographed it in infrared
light, which passes through dust that blocks visible light. Sitting in the middle of the dust
lane was a second core. Two galaxies, with their centers now only about 20,000 light-years
apart, wrapped in one shared envelope of stars.

That's why Toomre put it in the middle of his list. The disks are wrecked and the cores haven't
met yet.

NGC 520 has been on this site before, in our piece on
[how a galaxy's tilt hides its features](/articles/2026/09/using-galaxy-inclination-to-detect-galactic-features/).
There the question was what our viewing angle conceals. Here it's what happened.

### Stage Three -- NGC 7252: After the Smoke Clears

{{< nbas-image src="ngc-7252-eso.webp" width="300" align="left"
caption="NGC 7252 from the MPG/ESO 2.2-metre telescope at La Silla, more than four hours of exposure. Credit: ESO." >}}

The end of Toomre's sequence. Two disk galaxies fell together here something like 600 to 700
million years ago, and there is now one core where there were two. Around it are shells and
loops of stars, and two long tails thrown off in opposite directions.

The loops gave it its nickname. In the 1950s the logo of Eisenhower's "Atoms for Peace" program
was a stylized atom with electrons looping around a nucleus, and NGC 7252 looks enough like it
that the name stuck. Schweizer, who did more than anyone to explain this galaxy, wrote that it
"has variously been compared to the Atoms-for-Peace symbol, to a fuzzball with protruding
filaments, and to a crumpled spider."

{{< nbas-image src="ngc_7252-equ-82m-square.webp" width="300" align="right"
caption="NGC 7252 (Arp 226), Bob Donahue, NBAS, eQuinox 2, 82 min" >}}

Now the honest part. This is a very hard object. The main body is less than 2 arcminutes across.
The tails stretch the whole thing to nearly 8 arcminutes, but they are so faint that in more than
an hour of exposure with an eQuinox 2 they barely separate from the sky background. The two
newborn dwarf galaxies at the tail ends aren't there at all.

Use the observatory image as the answer key, then go back to ours. The bright, slightly fuzzy
core is easy. The inner loops are there if you know where they should be. The rest is a reminder
of what four hours on a 2.2-meter telescope under a Chilean sky buys you.

### Off the Sequence -- NGC 660: A Ring at the Wrong Angle

{{< nbas-image src="ngc_660-equ-34m-square.webp" width="300" align="right"
caption="NGC 660, Bob Donahue, NBAS, eQuinox 2, 34 min" >}}

Not every encounter ends in a merger of equals. NGC 660 is a barred spiral seen nearly edge-on,
with a second structure laid across it: a broad ring of gas, dust, and young stars, tilted about
45 to 55 degrees to the main disk. The two cross and make an X.

Galaxies like this are called polar-ring galaxies, though as the tilt shows, the name describes
the class better than it describes this one. Disks don't grow rings at that angle on their own.
The material came from somewhere else, about a billion years ago, and there are two accounts of
how. In one, NGC 660 collided with another galaxy. In the other, it pulled gas off a galaxy that
was only passing by, and the stolen gas settled into orbit as a ring. The evidence hasn't picked
between them.

So NGC 660 isn't a stage in the two-spirals-make-an-elliptical story. It's a galaxy that kept
its type and gained a part.

It's also the easy one. At about 8 arcminutes long it is the largest of the five on the sky, and
the X shows up early in an exposure. This is its second appearance here too; the
[inclination article](/articles/2026/09/using-galaxy-inclination-to-detect-galactic-features/)
has the first.

### Off the Sequence -- NGC 337: The One Without a Culprit

{{< nbas-image src="ngc_337-equ-square-101m.webp" width="300" align="right"
caption="NGC 337, Bob Donahue, NBAS, eQuinox 2, 101 min" >}}

On looks alone, NGC 337 belongs in this article. It has no real central bulge. Its arms
are broken into pieces that don't lead back to the center. It's studded with bright knots of star
formation, and one side of it reaches farther out than the other. William Herschel, who found it
in 1785, called it an "irregular parallelogram."

The catalogs file it as an ordinary late-type barred spiral, SB(s)d, with no "peculiar" flag. It
isn't in Arp's atlas. It isn't in Toomre's list. People who have studied its shape have suggested
a close encounter as the cause, and then conceded that nobody can point to the galaxy that did
it. It isn't alone out there: it's the brightest member of a small group, and a faint dwarf
spiral, NGC 337A, lies about 27 arcminutes to the east. Neither fact amounts to a suspect.

It may have had an encounter we can't reconstruct. It may be a galaxy that simply forms stars in
an untidy way. We include it for that reason. A disturbed look is evidence, but it isn't proof,
and the four galaxies above earned their stories through measurements that NGC 337 doesn't have
yet.

## Finding and Observing These Galaxies

All six of these galaxies are small and faint enough that a finding chart or atlas gets
you to the right field and no further. A smart scope will go to each by its NGC number.
If you're star-hopping or using setting circles, here is where they are:

The list is in the order they cross the meridian, and that's the order to observe them in,
because NGC 7252 won't wait. It never gets higher than 23 degrees from North Adams. In
mid-November it's at its highest around 6:40 PM, half an hour after the sky is fully dark. By the
end of the month it peaks before dark and is sinking by the time you can see it. Start there.

NGC 7714 and NGC 7715 follow a little over an hour later. NGC 337, NGC 520, and NGC 660 are at
their best between 9 and 10 PM in mid-November, and about an hour earlier by month's end.

### Smart Scopes

Of course with smart-scope imaging, "longer is better" (up to a point).

The cores of all of these come up in the first few minutes. So does NGC 660's X and NGC 520's
dust lane. The faint things take patience: NGC 7714's tails and bridge, and above all NGC 7252's
loops. For those two, plan on the longest session your sky and your battery allow -- probably over
a couple of nights -- and expect the result to be subtle.

### At the Eyepiece

None of these is a showpiece, and a visual observer should go in expecting small, dim, and oddly
shaped. That oddness is the reward. In a 17.5-inch telescope, the veteran observer Steve Gottlieb
recorded NGC 7714 as a small halo around an "unusually bright 13th magnitude stellar nucleus,"
with NGC 7715 beside it "very faint, very small." He saw NGC 337 as fairly bright but
"noticeably mottled," with knots that came and went. A galaxy that looks uneven at the eyepiece
is telling you something a smooth one isn't.

## Wherever You're Looking From

None of these galaxies will look any different in your lifetime, or in the lifetime of anyone who
has ever looked at them. The collision in NGC 520 was already old when Herschel swept past it.
What changed was that people started asking what had happened to them, and found that the answer
was scattered across the sky in pieces.

That's available to anyone with a telescope, a smart scope, or a set of images and a free
evening. Start with the Whirlpool next spring if you want the famous one. Then come back to
these, in order, and watch a billion years go by.
