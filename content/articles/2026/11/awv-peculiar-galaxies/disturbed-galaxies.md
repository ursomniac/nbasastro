### Middle science section: the tuning fork has no slot for an event
Angle for THIS article (the dwarf piece takes a different angle — the fork runs out at the faint end — so the two don't repeat each other).

- The Hubble fork classifies appearance, not history. These galaxies had a normal type before the encounter, and some will have a different one after.
- Honest caveat to state outright: nobody has watched a galaxy change type. Timescales are hundreds of millions of years; every case is inferred from snapshots, simulations, and look-back to distant populations.
- Arp's 1966 atlas was compiled precisely from galaxies the fork couldn't hold. NGC 520 (Arp 157), NGC 7714/7715 (Arp 284), NGC 7252 are Arp objects. **[VERIFY NGC 7252's Arp number; check whether NGC 660 and NGC 337 are in the atlas]**
- Observed/inferred type changes besides "mergers make ellipticals" — all **[VERIFY]**:
  - Spiral → S0 by gas stripping in clusters (morphology–density relation; Butcher–Oemler effect; "jellyfish" galaxies caught mid-strip). Strongest case.
  - Collisional rings are temporary — expand and fade in a few hundred Myr. So NGC 7714 changes look twice.
  - Acquired structure — NGC 660's polar ring added to a galaxy that already had a type.
  - Tidal dwarf galaxies condensing out of tidal tails. From memory NGC 7252's tails have candidates — would be a direct link to `dwarf-galaxies`.
  - Slow internal change — bars rearranging gas and building central bulges with no collision.
- Key evidence for our own remnant: NGC 7252's light profile already follows the law ellipticals follow (Schweizer, early 1980s, from memory). **[VERIFY]**
- Hubble's "Irr II" / later "I0" class (smooth, dusty, chaotic, M82-like) is mostly disturbed/starburst galaxies — i.e. it belongs to THIS article, not the dwarf one. Same "irregular" confusion flagged in the title note. **[VERIFY]**
