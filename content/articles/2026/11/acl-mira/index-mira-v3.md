## Introduction

{{< nbas-image src="banner.png" fullwidth="true" >}}

Point a pair of binoculars at the same spot in Cetus every few weeks for a year, and you'll watch a star very nearly disappear and reappear again. That's not an exaggeration for effect — Mira genuinely swings from naked-eye bright to invisible-without-a-telescope and back, on a clock of almost exactly eleven months. It was the first star anyone documented doing this, and four centuries later it's still surprising people: as recently as 2007, a NASA satellite found it dragging a tail behind it longer than the distance to our nearest stellar neighbor.

## A Star Discovered by Accident

On August 13, 1596, a German pastor and amateur astronomer named David Fabricius was using an unremarkable third-magnitude star in Cetus as a fixed reference point — he was actually trying to track the position of Mercury. Three weeks later, the "reference star" had brightened by a full magnitude. By October it had faded below naked-eye visibility entirely, and Fabricius, reasonably, assumed he'd witnessed a nova.

{{< nbas-image src="Johannes_Phocylides_Holwarda.jpg" width="200" align="right"
caption="Johannes Holwarda (1618-1651), Frisian astronomer, physician, and philosopher." >}}
He hadn't. 
Fabricius noted it again in 1609, confirming that it wasn't a nova.
It took until 1638 for Johannes Holwarda to work out that the star's disappearing act was periodic — about eleven months — making him the discoverer of its true variable nature. The name came later still: writing to Johannes Kepler in 1609, Fabricius had called the star *res mira*, Latin for "a wonderful thing." In 1662, Johannes Hevelius formalized this into the name we still use — Mira, "the Wonderful" — in his *Historiola Mirae Stellae*.

Mira became the prototype for an entire class: there are now thousands of known "Mira variables" across the sky, all named after this one star.

But this might not be the first time in the past that the comings and goings of this star was noticed:  it's possible (but not confirmed) that the star was observed by both the Greeks (by Hipparchus) and by the Chinese in the 11th century, as well, but Holwarda's "discovery" is the only one known with certainty.

## Mira the Star

Mira is a red giant near the very end of its life — specifically an *asymptotic giant branch* (AGB) star, a stage the Sun itself will pass through in a few billion years. It's enormous: current measurements put its radius at somewhere around 300–400 times the Sun's, meaning if it replaced the Sun, it would swallow everything out to roughly the orbit of Mars.

{{< nbas-image src="mira-2000-2026.png" fullwidth="true"
caption="[AAVSO light curve](https://apps.aavso.org/v2/tools/light-curves/) for Mira, 2020-2026" >}}

The 332-day rhythm comes from pulsation: the star's outer layers expand and contract, its surface temperature rising and falling in step, which is what drives the dramatic brightness swings — a change of roughly 1,500-fold in visible light between minimum and maximum. The rise from minimum to maximum takes only about 126 days; the decline back down takes nearly twice as long, so the light curve isn't a simple sine wave — it's a fast climb and a long, slow fade.

The commonly-stated age of 6 billion years (Gyr) isn't pulled from a generic reference table — it comes from the pulsation period itself. Mira variables follow a _period–age relation_, calibrated from the kinematics of Miras in the solar neighborhood (Feast & Whitelock 2000): the underlying physics is that a Mira's pulsation period tracks the mass of its progenitor, and more massive progenitors are younger. Longer-period Miras — some run out past 400–1000 days — do come from younger, more massive stars. Mira's own period, 332 days, sits almost exactly in the middle of that relation, corresponding to an age of roughly 6–7 Gyr. It's a nice reminder that the period isn't just a clock for brightness — it's a clock for age, too.

{{< nbas-image src="mira-hr-diagram.png" fullwidth="true"
caption="Mira's place on the Hertzsprung–Russell diagram: from a hotter, more luminous young F-type star, through the giant branch, to today's cool, luminous AGB pulsator — and, schematically, where it's headed next." >}}

Mira didn't start out as the star you see today. A few billion years ago it was a hotter, brighter, unremarkable F-type main-sequence star — more massive than the Sun, and because of that, both hotter and more luminous than the Sun ever was. As it exhausted the hydrogen in its core, it swelled and cooled, climbing the giant branch and then the asymptotic giant branch to reach its current position: cool, enormous, and thousands of times more luminous than it was as a young star. It's not done yet, either — within a few hundred thousand years it will shed its outer layers entirely, briefly flare hot and blue as an exposed stellar core, and settle in as a white dwarf, the same fate awaiting the Sun.

### The tail nobody expected 

{{< nbas-image src="galex-mira-tail.jpg" width="500" align="right"
caption="NASA, GALEX image" >}}
In 2007, NASA's GALEX satellite — surveying the whole sky in ultraviolet light — noticed something nobody had ever documented around Mira: a comet-like wake of shed material stretching roughly 13 light-years across the sky. For comparison, that's more than three times the distance from the Sun to Proxima Centauri. It's invisible in ordinary light entirely — Mira is much brighter than its tail in the visible, so the tail was only picked up because GALEX happened to be looking in UV.

The mechanism: Mira is moving unusually fast for a red giant, about 130 km/s relative to its surroundings, likely from a gravitational encounter with another star at some point in its past. As it plows through the interstellar medium, the material it's constantly shedding gets left behind, fluorescing in ultraviolet as it goes. Astronomers have since also found a bow shock piling up in front of the star and detected an atomic hydrogen counterpart to the tail. The two numbers you'll see quoted for the tail's age aren't a contradiction, just two different questions. The GALEX discovery team (Martin et al. 2007) estimated the ultraviolet-bright portion of the tail traces about the last 30,000 years of mass loss — the part still warm enough to fluoresce. A follow-up hydrodynamic model of the whole bow shock and wake (Wareing et al. 2007) put the full structure's age at roughly 450,000 years, arguing the high-mass-loss phase producing it has likely been underway far longer than the UV emission alone suggests. Different slices of the same tail, different ages.

The genuinely striking part for the wrapup: Mira had been one of the most closely watched stars in the sky for over 400 years, and this had simply never been noticed — not because it's subtle, but because nobody had ever pointed a UV survey at the empty-looking space around it.

## Long-Period Variables: Mira's Whole Family

Mira lends its name to an entire class of pulsating red giants — stars in the same late-life AGB stage, pulsating over periods roughly 100 to 1,000 days, with big enough brightness swings to notice by eye. They're common enough, and bright enough (especially in infrared), that astronomers use them as tracers of old and intermediate-age stellar populations — including, in recent research, mapping out the structure and formation history of the Milky Way's central bar.

Mira variables aren't just interesting on their own terms, either — oxygen-rich Miras follow a period–luminosity relation, a dimmer echo of the famous Cepheid relation, that's been used to gauge distances to galaxies in and around the Local Group. It's a rougher tool than Cepheids, but a genuinely useful one.

### When to Look

Mira had its last maximum in early March 2026, and is expected to return to about that brightness in late January 2027 (which means there's a chance it'll have a second maximum in 2027 at the very end of December).  Mira is likely already climbing out of a minimum around Nov 8 — past its faintest, on the rise, but not yet dramatic. That's arguably a better story than either extreme: catch it now, dim but climbing, and come back in December or January to see the payoff.

### Naked Eye vs. Binoculars

At minimum, Mira drops to around 9th–10th magnitude — well beyond naked-eye range, needing a telescope or at least a good pair of binoculars from a dark site. At maximum it can rival some of the brighter stars in Cetus outright. ## Why This Matters When You Look Up

Here's the thing worth leaving the reader with: from the eyepiece, Mira in November is just a dim, unremarkable red point, easy to mistake for nothing in particular. It won't look like much yet.

But by the time you find it, you'll know it's the same star a Lutheran pastor accidentally caught while trying to track Mercury — not even looking for anything unusual. You'll know its faintness right now is temporary, a hundred-some days into an eleven-month heartbeat it's kept up for as long as anyone's been watching, and that it's due back at its brightest by January. You'll know it's not traveling alone: a companion star, still argued over in the literature as recently as this year, is quietly stealing material from it and will eventually detonate because of it. And you'll know that even after four centuries of being one of the most stared-at stars in the sky, it kept a 13-light-year secret until 2007 — because the tail was there in ultraviolet the whole time, and nobody had thought to look.

That's the payoff of "a closer look": the dim red dot doesn't change, but what you bring to looking at it does. Next time you're out and Cetus is up, Mira isn't just a variable star to check off a list — it's a discovery accident, a slow-motion heartbeat, a binary with an ongoing argument attached, and a hidden tail, all in the same spot in the sky. Go find it.
