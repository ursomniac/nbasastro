## Introduction

{{< nbas-image src="banner.webp" fullwidth="true" >}}

{{< clear >}}
{{< object-specs align="right" >}}
Point a pair of binoculars at the same spot in Cetus every few weeks for a year, and you'll watch a star very nearly disappear and reappear again. That's not an exaggeration for effect — Mira genuinely swings from naked-eye bright to invisible-without-a-telescope and back, on a clock of almost exactly eleven months. It was the first star anyone documented doing this, and four centuries later it's still surprising people: as recently as 2007, a NASA satellite found it dragging a tail behind it longer than the distance to our nearest stellar neighbor.

---
## A Star Discovered by Accident

On August 13, 1596, a German pastor and amateur astronomer named David Fabricius was using an unremarkable third-magnitude star in Cetus as a fixed reference point — he was actually trying to track the position of Mercury. Three weeks later, the "reference star" had brightened by a full magnitude. By October it had faded below naked-eye visibility entirely, and Fabricius, reasonably, assumed he'd witnessed a nova.

{{< nbas-image src="Johannes_Phocylides_Holwarda.webp" width="200" align="right"
caption="Johannes Holwarda (1618-1651), Frisian astronomer, physician, and philosopher." >}}
He hadn't. Fabricius noted it again in 1609, confirming that it wasn't a nova. It took until 1638 for Johannes Holwarda to work out that the star's disappearing act was periodic — about eleven months — making him the discoverer of its true variable nature. The name came later still: writing to Johannes Kepler in 1609, Fabricius had called the star *res mira*, Latin for "a wonderful thing." In 1662, Johannes Hevelius formalized this into the name we still use — Mira, "the Wonderful" — in his *Historiola Mirae Stellae*.

Mira became the prototype for an entire class: there are now thousands of known "Mira variables" across the sky, all named after this one star.

{{< clear >}}

### And a First!

Up until Fabricus's discovery and Holwarda's confirmation, the only variable stars noticed were supernovae with truly outstanding examples in the preceding decades:  Tycho's Star in 1572 in Cassiopeia, and Kepler's Star in 1604 in Ophiuchus.  Prior to that you have to go back to the 11th and 12th centuries in chronicles by the Arabs, China, and Japan (including the 1054 CE supernova that we now see as the Crab Nebula in Taurus).   Mira was something different: it brightened, faded, and then it came _back_ again.
Up to that point, dynamism in the sky had belonged entirely to the solar system — the planets' wandering motion, a comet flaring up and receding, a meteor's instant streak. The "fixed stars" were exactly that: fixed, permanent, unchanging, with the one narrow exception of a nova or supernova's single catastrophic flash. Mira fit neither category. It wasn't a wanderer, and it wasn't a one-time event — it was an ordinary-looking star doing something dynamic, on a predictable cycle, indefinitely. That was new: the stars themselves could be active, changing objects, not just an unmoving backdrop for the solar system's motion.

With more people using telescopes to scan the sky it wasn't long before other variable stars were noticed:

Things took off in 1784 with the rapid sequence of discoveries by Pigott and Goodricke (who sadly died soon after at only 21).  But this opened the floodgates with new variable stars being discovered regularly.

It's possible — though not confirmed — that Mira's comings and goings were noticed even earlier, by the Greeks (via Hipparchus) and by Chinese observers in the 11th century. But Holwarda's is the only discovery of its periodic nature known with certainty.

## Mira the Star

Mira is a red giant near the very end of its life — specifically an *asymptotic giant branch* (AGB) star, a stage the Sun itself will pass through in a few billion years. It's enormous: current measurements put its radius at somewhere around 300–400 times the Sun's, meaning if it replaced the Sun, it would swallow everything out to roughly the orbit of Mars.

{{< nbas-image src="mira-2000-2026.webp" fullwidth="true"
caption="[AAVSO light curve](https://apps.aavso.org/v2/tools/light-curves/) for Mira, 2020-2026" >}}

The 332-day rhythm comes from pulsation: the star's outer layers expand and contract, its surface temperature rising and falling in step, which is what drives the dramatic brightness swings — a change of roughly 1,700-fold in visible light between minimum and maximum, using the star's own quoted magnitude range of 2.0 to 10.1. The rise from minimum to maximum takes only about 126 days; the decline back down takes nearly twice as long, so the light curve isn't a simple sine wave — it's a fast climb and a long, slow fade.

The commonly-stated age of 6 billion years (Gyr) isn't pulled from a generic reference table — it comes from the pulsation period itself. Mira variables follow a *period–age relation*, calibrated from the kinematics of Miras in the solar neighborhood (Feast & Whitelock 2000): the underlying physics is that a Mira's pulsation period tracks the mass of its progenitor, and more massive progenitors are younger. Longer-period Miras — some run out past 400–1000 days — do come from younger, more massive stars. Mira's own period, 332 days, sits almost exactly in the middle of that relation, corresponding to an age of roughly 6–7 Gyr. It's a nice reminder that the period isn't just a clock for brightness — it's a clock for age, too.

{{< nbas-image src="hrd2a.webp" width="550" fullwidth="true"
caption="Vallastro, CC BY-SA 4.0, via Wikimedia Commons;  Mira's place on the Hertzsprung–Russell diagram: from a hotter, more luminous young F-type star, to today's cool, luminous AGB pulsator." >}}

Mira didn't start out as the star you see today. A few billion years ago it was a hotter, brighter, unremarkable F-type main-sequence star — just a little more massive than the Sun, and because of that, both hotter and more luminous than the Sun ever was. As it exhausted the hydrogen in its core, it swelled and cooled, climbing the giant branch and then the asymptotic giant branch to reach its current position: cool, enormous, and thousands of times more luminous than it was as a young star. It's not done yet, either — within a few hundred thousand years it will shed its outer layers entirely, briefly flare hot and blue as an exposed stellar core, and settle in as a white dwarf, the same fate awaiting the Sun.

## The Tail Nobody Expected

{{< nbas-image src="galex-mira-tail.webp" width="480" align="left"
caption="NASA, GALEX image" >}}
In 2007, NASA's GALEX satellite — surveying the whole sky in ultraviolet light — noticed something nobody had ever documented around Mira: a comet-like wake of shed material stretching roughly 13 light-years across the sky (Martin et al. 2007). For comparison, that's more than three times the distance from the Sun to Proxima Centauri. It's invisible in ordinary light entirely — Mira is much brighter than its tail in the visible, so the tail was only picked up because GALEX happened to be looking in UV.

The mechanism comes down to this: Mira is moving unusually fast for a red giant — about 130 km/s relative to its surroundings, likely from a gravitational encounter with another star at some point in its past. As it plows through the interstellar medium, the wind it's constantly shedding gets left behind, fluorescing in ultraviolet as it goes, producing both the tail and a bow shock piling up ahead of the star. 

The two numbers you'll see quoted for the tail's age aren't a contradiction, just two different questions. The GALEX discovery team (Martin et al. 2007) estimated the ultraviolet-bright portion of the tail traces about the last 30,000 years of mass loss — the part still warm enough to fluoresce. A follow-up hydrodynamic model of the whole bow shock and wake (Wareing et al. 2007) put the full structure's age at roughly 450,000 years, arguing the high-mass-loss phase producing it has likely been underway far longer than the UV emission alone suggests. Different slices of the same tail, different ages.

The genuinely striking part: Mira had been one of the most closely watched stars in the sky for over 400 years, and none of that scrutiny ever hinted at the tail — not because anyone was careless, but because the tail is essentially invisible outside ultraviolet light, a window on the universe that didn't open until space telescopes made it possible.

## The Companion: Mira B

{{< nbas-image src="hst-binary-dec-1995.webp" width="500" align="right"
caption="M. Karovska (CfA), NASA, HST FOC image, December 1995; separation is 0.6 arcsec." >}}
Mira isn't alone. In 1922, A. H. Joy and R. G. Aitken discovered a faint companion about 0.6 arcseconds away (Aitken 1923) — physically somewhere around 55–70 AU depending on which distance estimate you adopt, on a wide orbit with a preliminary period of about 498 years (Prieur et al. 2002). The companion is now known as Mira B, or VZ Ceti.

Mira B's identity was genuinely contested for decades: its low X-ray luminosity once suggested an ordinary K-type main-sequence star, while other evidence pointed to a white dwarf. That question is now settled. Rapid optical flickering detected by Sokoloski & Bildsten (2010) matches the signature of an accreting white dwarf, not a normal star, and a 2026 study (Zamanov et al. 2026) used that same flickering to pin down its mass: Mira B is a **white dwarf of spectral type DA** (a hydrogen-atmosphere white dwarf), with a mass of about **0.24 ± 0.04 M☉** if it accretes via wind Roche-lobe overflow — the favored scenario — or about 0.42 ± 0.04 M☉ under a simpler Bondi-Hoyle accretion model. Either way, it's an unusually light white dwarf; typical white dwarfs run closer to 0.6 M☉, so Mira B sits among the "extremely low-mass" white dwarfs.

Mira B accretes material from Mira A's stellar wind at a modest rate — on the order of 10<sup>-9</sup> to 10<sup>-10</sup> M☉ per year — which heats an accretion disk around it to roughly 14,000 K. ALMA observations have resolved the ionized region around that disk at about 2.4 AU across (Vlemmings et al. 2015).

Because it's confirmed to be an accreting white dwarf, Mira B will periodically undergo classical nova eruptions — brief thermonuclear flashes on its surface as accreted hydrogen ignites — with Sokoloski & Bildsten (2010) estimating a recurrence of roughly once every million years. What it will *not* do is go supernova. A Type Ia supernova requires a white dwarf to approach the Chandrasekhar limit of about 1.4 M☉; Mira B starts at roughly a sixth of that, its accretion rate is slow, and each nova eruption blows off much of what it gained in the meantime. There's no realistic path from here to detonation — only a very long string of comparatively modest nova flashes.

## Long-Period Variables: Mira's Cohort

{{< nbas-image src="mira-pl-relation.webp" width="450" align="left"
caption="The Mira period–luminosity relation (K band): M_K = 0.94 − 3.47 log₁₀P (van Leeuwen, Feast & Whitelock 1997; slope from Feast et al. 1989). Mira's own point is marked." >}}

Mira lends its name to an entire class of pulsating red giants — stars in the same late-life AGB stage, pulsating over periods roughly 100 to 1,000 days, with big enough brightness swings to notice by eye. They're common enough, and bright enough (especially in infrared), that astronomers use them as tracers of old and intermediate-age stellar populations — including, in recent research, mapping out the structure and formation history of the Milky Way's central bar.

Mira variables aren't just interesting on their own terms, either — oxygen-rich Miras follow a period–luminosity relation, a dimmer echo of the famous Cepheid relation, that's been used to gauge distances to galaxies in and around the Local Group. It's a rougher tool than Cepheids, but a genuinely useful one.

## Observing Mira

### When to Look

Mira reached its most recent minimum right around September 1, 2026. Its light curve isn't a smooth sine wave: AAVSO data from the previous cycle shows it lingers near minimum for six to eight weeks with barely any change, then climbs sharply — about 4.5 magnitudes in just five weeks — before flattening out near maximum. Applying that same real timing to this cycle: expect it to stay a telescope-only object through October, become a binoculars target in late October/early November, reach naked-eye brightness by mid-November, and hit maximum in early January 2027. The maximum magnitude varies from cycle to cycle — typically around 3.0–3.5, but some maxima run a magnitude or more brighter (let's hope this is one of those times).

Roughly, here are some target dates, worked out from the star's own actual behavior last cycle rather than a generic assumption — though as always with Mira, treat these as a guide to check against current AAVSO observations, not a fixed schedule:

{{< article-table >}}

| Date    |  Mag.   |  Observing  |
| :------------------ | :-----: | :--------------------- |
| Sept. 1, 2026 |  ~ 9.0–9.1  | at minimum, telescope only |
| early Nov. 2026 |  ~ 7.5  | possible in binoculars |
| mid Nov. 2026 |  ~ 5.0  | naked-eye in dark skies |
| mid Dec. 2026 |  ~ 3.5  | easily naked-eye, nearing maximum |
| early Jan. 2027 |  ~ 3.2–3.3   | at its brightest, sometimes almost mag 2.0 |
| mid Feb. 2027  |  ~ 5.0   | starts to disappear naked eye |
| mid Mar. 2027  |  –   | sets in evening twilight |

{{< /article-table >}}

After that, Mira is simply too close to the Sun to observe at all — no amount of aperture helps — until it re-emerges as a morning object in early-to-mid July 2027, by which point it'll be well down toward its next minimum.

While it will always look like a star, of course, what should stand out is its color – definitely redder than most other stars.  However, if you look carefully as it brightens over the weeks towards its maximum, that color should shift from decidedly "red" to more "orange" and then back again as it fades.   What you're experiencing there is a real change in surface temperature over the cycle: cooler near minimum (favoring the deep red), measurably warmer near maximum (paling toward orange) — Mira's own spectral type actually shifts between roughly M9 at minimum and M5 at maximum as this happens.

## Why This Matters When You Look Up

From the eyepiece, Mira in November is just a dim, unremarkable red point, easy to mistake for nothing in particular. It won't look like much yet.

But by the time you find it, you'll know it's the same star a Lutheran pastor accidentally caught while trying to track Mercury — not even looking for anything unusual. You'll know its faintness right now is temporary, a hundred-some days into an eleven-month heartbeat it's kept up for as long as anyone's been watching, and that it's due back at its brightest by January. You'll know it's not traveling alone: a confirmed white dwarf companion, barely a sixth of the mass it would need to ever go supernova, is quietly stealing material from it and will keep flaring — modestly, roughly once every million years — because of it. And you'll know that even after four centuries of being one of the most stared-at stars in the sky, it kept a 13-light-year secret until 2007 — because the tail was there in ultraviolet the whole time, and nobody had thought to look.

That's the payoff of "A Closer Look": the dim red dot brightens and fades right on schedule, 
but understanding why and how it's changing while seeing it happen lets you enjoy the experience.
Next time you're out and Cetus is up, Mira isn't just a variable star to check off a list — it's a discovery accident, a slow-motion heartbeat, a white dwarf companion with a settled identity and an unsettling future, and a hidden tail, all in the same spot in the sky. Go find it.
