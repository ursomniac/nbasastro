## Introduction

<!-- NOTE: normally I put the banner here, if for no other reason than to remind me to make one... -->

{{< nbas-image src="mitchell-1845-p174.jpg" width="380" align="right"
caption="\"Tell how many planets there are.\" S. Augustus Mitchell, Mitchell's Primary Geography (1845), p. 174. Public domain." 
alt="A 19th-century schoolbook engraving of the solar system: the Sun at the center, circular orbits labelled for Mercury, Venus, Earth, Mars, Vesta, Juno, Ceres, Pallas, Jupiter, Saturn and Uranus, plus a comet, with study questions printed below." >}}

In 1845, an American schoolchild working through *Mitchell's Primary Geography* would come to
page 174, find an engraving of the solar system, and be asked a simple question: *Tell how many
planets there are.*

The answer was eleven. Mercury, Venus, Earth, Mars, Jupiter, Saturn and "Herschel" (now Uranus)  — and, packed
into the gap between Mars and Jupiter, four small worlds called Vesta, Juno, Ceres and Pallas.

All four had been found in a six-year burst at the start of the century, between 1801 and 1807.
Then nothing more turned up for 38 years. For a generation, eleven planets was simply what the
solar system *was*.

How those four got onto the list is one of the better stories in astronomy. It involves an
organized search that got scooped, a discovery made by someone who wasn't looking, a theory that
was completely wrong and still told people exactly where to point their telescopes, an
observatory burned in a war, a metal sold out of a shop window, and a retired postal official
who refused to give up. And all four are still up there for you to find.

## A Hole in the Pattern

The search started with a number pattern.

In 1766, Johann Daniel Titius was translating a popular book on natural philosophy by the Swiss
naturalist Charles Bonnet. Into his German translation he quietly inserted two paragraphs of his
own — they aren't in Bonnet's original — describing a curious regularity in the planets'
distances from the Sun. Take the series 0, 3, 6, 12, 24, 48, 96, add 4 to each, and divide by
10. You get 0.4, 0.7, 1.0, 1.6, 2.8, 5.2 and 10.0 — and, in astronomical units, those are very
close to the distances of Mercury, Venus, Earth, Mars, *something*, Jupiter and Saturn.

{{< article-table align="right" >}}

| Body | Rule (AU) | Actual (AU) |
|------|-----------|-------------|
| Mercury | 0.4 | 0.39 |
| Venus | 0.7 | 0.72 |
| Earth | 1.0 | 1.00 |
| Mars | 1.6 | 1.52 |
| *gap* | **2.8** | Ceres: **2.77** |
| Jupiter | 5.2 | 5.20 |
| Saturn | 10.0 | 9.54 |
| Uranus | 19.6 | 19.19 |

{{< /article-table >}}

That *something* was the problem: nothing was known at 2.8 AU. Johann Elert Bode picked the rule
up in 1772 and put it in a footnote in his popular astronomy compendium (at first without
crediting Titius), and it has carried both their names ever since.

A curiosity became a target in 1781, when William Herschel discovered Uranus — and it turned out
to orbit at about 19.2 AU, close to the rule's next value of 19.6. Bode used that fit to urge his
colleagues to go looking for the missing planet at 2.8.

Is the rule real physics? Nobody has found a reason it should be. Most astronomers now treat it
as a coincidence of the sort that stable planetary systems produce fairly easily, and the
planetary-science journal *Icarus* no longer accepts papers proposing improved versions. But in
1800 it looked like a law of nature with a hole in it.

## The Celestial Police

On 20 September 1800, six astronomers met at Johann Hieronymus Schröter's private observatory in
Lilienthal, a village near Bremen, and set out to find it. Their formal name was the United
Astronomical Society. Everyone else called them the *Himmelspolizey* — the celestial police.

{{< nbas-image src="zach.jpg" width="220" align="left"
caption="Franz Xaver von Zach. Unknown artist, Public domain, via Wikimedia Commons" 
alt="Engraved portrait of Franz Xaver von Zach." >}}
The driving force was Baron Franz Xaver von Zach, a Hungarian-born astronomer and editor who had
been looking for the Bode planet for years. The plan was methodical: divide the zodiac — the band
of sky where the planets travel — into 24 zones, each 15° long, and assign one zone to each of 24
astronomers across Europe. Each would chart the stars in his zone carefully and watch for one
that moved.

Among the six at Lilienthal were Schröter himself, his young observer Karl Ludwig Harding, and a
Bremen physician named Wilhelm Olbers. All three matter to this story. So does a name on the list
of 24 who wasn't at the meeting: Giuseppe Piazzi, director of the observatory at Palermo, in
Sicily.

Invitations went out by post, across a continent at war. Some arrived late; some, apparently,
never arrived at all. Piazzi was on the list. He wasn't searching. And he got there first.

## The Star That Wouldn't Stay Put

{{< nbas-image src="piazzi.jpg" width="220" align="right"
caption="Giuseppe Piazzi. Credit: Costanzo Angelini, Public domain, via Wikimedia Commons." 
alt="Portrait of Giuseppe Piazzi." >}}
Piazzi was doing something much less glamorous than planet hunting. Since 1792 he had been
building a new star catalogue — he'd found errors in the best existing one, Francis Wollaston's,
and he had a better instrument to fix them with. The work meant measuring each star's position as
it crossed the meridian, night after night, with his assistant Niccolò Cacciatore writing down
the numbers.

On the night of 1 January 1801 — the first night of the new century — Piazzi measured a faint
star in Taurus. When he checked it the next night, it wasn't quite where it had been.

The story of the next few nights comes from Cacciatore, who told it to a visiting British naval
officer forty years later, so take the details as a fond memory rather than a transcript.
According to Cacciatore, Piazzi first blamed the discrepancy on a recording error — Cacciatore's
— and Cacciatore suggested the error might be in the observation instead. By the fourth night
the star had moved again, and there was no arguing with it.

Piazzi was cautious. An unsigned notice in the *Journal de Paris* announced a "new comet" near
Taurus (and misprinted the reference star's catalogue number, for good measure); the great French
astronomer Lalande learned of the discovery from the newspaper. When Piazzi wrote to Bode in
Berlin he called it a comet. But to his friend Barnaba Oriani in Milan he wrote that it might be
something better than a comet. Those letters, written on 24 January, took months to arrive:
Bode's came on 20 March, and Oriani's not until 5 April, delayed by Napoleon's occupation of
northern Italy.

By then the object was gone. Piazzi had followed it for 41 days, the last time on 11 February,
before it slipped into the evening twilight. That was 22 observations covering only a tiny piece
of its orbit, and nobody knew how to compute where it would reappear.

Piazzi named it *Ceres Ferdinandea*, after the patron goddess of Sicily and his patron, King
Ferdinand IV of Naples and Sicily. The astronomical world kept the goddess and quietly dropped the
king.

### Finding It Again

{{< nbas-image src="ceres-ephemerides.png" width="450" align="right"
caption="First observations of Ceres Ferdinandea after its discovery: positions from von Zach (Seeberg), Olbers (Bremen), Méchain (Paris), Maskelyne (Greenwich) and Oriani (Milan), December 1801 – March 1802." 
alt="A printed table headed 'Prime Osservazioni di Cerere Ferdinandea dopo la sua scoperta', listing dates, times, right ascensions and northern declinations of Ceres from December 1801 to March 1802, with the observer and observatory for each group of rows." >}}
Recovering Ceres became the problem of the year, and it was solved by a 24-year-old
mathematician in Brunswick named Carl Friedrich Gauss. Gauss worked out a new way to compute an
orbit from only a handful of observations, and von Zach published his prediction in September
1801. On 7 December, von Zach, at the Seeberg observatory near Gotha, recorded a faint star very
close to Gauss's position. Seeing it was not the same as knowing it: he needed to catch it moving,
and only on 31 December — almost exactly a year after Piazzi first saw it — was he sure. Olbers,
in Bremen, picked it up on the first two nights of 1802.

Once Gauss had shown where to look, everyone could find it. The table alongside, printed in
Italian under Piazzi's chosen name, collects positions measured over the next three months at
Gotha, Bremen, Paris, Greenwich and Milan. Its first entry is von Zach's from 7 December.

And Ceres sat at about 2.77 AU from the Sun. Right in the gap.
{{< clear >}}

{{< nbas-callout title="Did a philosopher \"prove\" there was no planet there?" >}}
A popular story says that Hegel, in his
1801 dissertation *De Orbitis Planetarum*, proved that no planet could exist between Mars and
Jupiter — just as Ceres was being found. What he actually wrote is more modest: a number series
borrowed (and altered) from Plato's *Timaeus* left no room for a planet there, *if* Plato's
series was the right one. He didn't settle the question, and he later said he was no longer
satisfied with what he'd written about the gaps.
{{< /nbas-callout >}}

## A Second Planet in the Same Neighborhood

{{< nbas-image src="olbers.jpg" width="220" align="left"
caption="Wilhelm Olbers. Credit: Rudolph Suhrlandt, Public domain, via Wikimedia Commons" 
alt="Lithograph portrait of Wilhelm Olbers." >}}
Olbers was a practicing physician in Bremen who spent his nights in an observatory on the upper
floor of his own house. On 28 March 1802, while checking the field around Ceres, he noticed
another "star" that moved.

It was a second planet. He named it Pallas, and it was strange in a way that Ceres wasn't. The
major planets all orbit within a few degrees of the same plane, like grooves on a record. Pallas's
orbit is tilted about 35° to that plane. Its orbit and Ceres' orbit are at almost the same
average distance from the Sun, but they cross each other at a steep angle — like two hoops of
nearly the same size, one tipped sharply against the other.

{{< clear >}}

Right: the same orbits edge-on, at true scale, showing Pallas's 35° tilt. Elements: JPL Small-Body Database." 
alt="Two diagrams. ## A Wrong Idea That Worked

Two planets where the rule allowed for one needed an explanation, and on 17 May 1802 Olbers
offered one in a letter to William Herschel. What if Ceres and Pallas were *pieces* of the single
missing planet, broken apart by a collision with a comet or by some internal explosion? Then there
should be more pieces out there.

The idea came with a built-in search strategy. If all the fragments started from the same place,
their orbits should all still pass through that place — or, at least, through the same two
opposite points in the sky where the orbits of Ceres and Pallas cross. Olbers worked out that
those points lay in the constellations Virgo and Cetus, and he resolved to survey the faint stars
in both regions, over and over, watching for anything that moved.

His geometry holds up. Run today's orbits for Ceres and Pallas through the same calculation and
the two crossing directions still land in Virgo and Cetus, and on the Cetus side the two orbits
pass within about 0.06 AU (9 million km) of each other.

The theory was wrong. The asteroids are not the debris of a shattered planet; the modern view is
that Jupiter's gravity kept the material in this zone from ever assembling into one. The fact that
the first orbits seemed to cross in two places was a coincidence of small numbers.

But a wrong theory that tells you where to look can still find things, and this one found two.

## Juno, and a Map Without Monsters

{{< nbas-image src="harding-1811.png" width="220" align="left"
caption="Karl Ludwig Harding - 1811. Credit: Bautsch, CC0, via Wikimedia Commons" 
alt="Portrait of Karl Ludwig Harding in 1811." >}}
Karl Ludwig Harding had come to Lilienthal in 1796 to tutor Schröter's son, and ended up running
the observatory. After Olbers' proposal, Harding set about charting the faint stars along the
paths of Ceres and Pallas — exactly the territory the broken-planet idea pointed to.

On 1 September 1804, comparing the sky with his charts, he found an eighth-magnitude star that
wasn't in the catalogues. It moved. Gauss computed its orbit, and it was named Juno.

Juno is only about 250 km across, much smaller than Ceres, but its surface is unusually
reflective for its type, and at a good opposition it reaches about magnitude 7.5. A small,
bright target, in the region someone had been told to search.

{{< clear >}}
Harding's charting turned into something lasting. His *Atlas novus coelestis*, published between
1808 and 1823, mapped about 120,000 stars — and it was the first major star atlas to leave out
the mythological figures. No bears, no hunters, no sea monsters; just stars and coordinates. It's
a small step toward the way we chart the sky today, and it came straight out of the search for
the missing planet. The style caught on: within a few years other mapmakers were drawing the sky
the same way.

{{< nbas-image src="Der_Gestirnte_Himmel.jpg" fullwidth="true"
caption="A star map of 1837 drawn \"after Harding's Atlas novus coelestis\" and other sources: stars, the Milky Way and constellation boundaries, but no mythological figures. C. F. Weiland, Der gestirnte Himmel (northern hemisphere). Leibniz-Institut für Länderkunde, CC0, via Wikimedia Commons."
alt="A round engraved map of the northern sky showing stars, the band of the Milky Way and constellation boundary lines, with no figures drawn." >}}

In 1805 Harding left for a professorship at Göttingen. His replacement as Schröter's assistant at
Lilienthal was a young clerk turned astronomer named Friedrich Wilhelm Bessel — who, thirty years
later, would measure the first [distance to a star](/articles/2026/12/parallax/).

## Vesta: The One You Can See

Olbers kept up his survey of Virgo and Cetus for years. On 29 March 1807, in Virgo, it paid off: a
fourth planet.

{{< article-table align="right" title="Planetary Symbols" >}}

| Asteroid | Symbol |
| :------- | :----: |
| 1 Ceres  | <span class="astro-sym"> ⚳ </span> | 
| 2 Pallas | <span class="astro-sym"> ⚴ </span> | 
| 3 Juno   | <span class="astro-sym"> ⚵ </span> | 
| 4 Vesta  | <span class="astro-sym"> ⚶ </span> | 

{{< /article-table >}}

He handed the honor of naming it to Gauss, who chose Vesta, the Roman goddess of the hearth, and
designed its symbol: an altar with a sacred fire burning on it. Gauss also computed its orbit in
about ten hours — a demonstration of how far orbit calculation had come since the anxious year
spent looking for Ceres.

Vesta is not the largest of the four, but it is the brightest. Its surface is unusually
reflective, and at its best it reaches about magnitude 5.1 — faintly visible to the naked eye
from a dark site. If you've ever seen an asteroid without optical help, it was almost certainly
this one.

## A Shop Window in Soho

{{< nbas-image src="wollaston.jpg" width="220" align="left"
caption="William Hyde Wollaston. Stipple engraving by W. Holl after J. Jackson. Wellcome Collection, CC BY 4.0, via Wikimedia Commons."
alt="Engraved portrait of William Hyde Wollaston." >}}
The new planets left their mark somewhere unexpected: the periodic table.

In 1802 the English chemist William Hyde Wollaston isolated a new metal from platinum ore. He
first called it *ceresium*, after the newest planet, then changed his mind and named it
*palladium*, after the even newer one.

Then he did something odd. Instead of publishing, he had the metal offered for sale,
anonymously, through Mrs. Forster's shop at 26 Gerrard Street in Soho, advertised as "Palladium,
or New Silver" — five shillings, half a guinea, or a guinea a sample. The Irish chemist Richard
Chenevix bought up the entire stock, analyzed it, and announced that it was no new element at all,
just an alloy of platinum and mercury. He published the claim and won the Royal Society's Copley
Medal.

In December 1803 an anonymous letter — almost certainly from Wollaston — offered £20 to anyone
who could make palladium from platinum and mercury in public. Nobody collected. In 1805 Wollaston
finally published his full account, and palladium was an element after all.

Ceres got an element too. In 1803 Jöns Jacob Berzelius and Wilhelm Hisinger in Sweden, and Martin
Klaproth in Germany independently, identified a new earth that became the source of **cerium**,
named for the first asteroid.

## The Observatory That Burned

{{< nbas-image src="Lilienthaler_Sternwarte.jpg" width="300" align="right"
caption="Schröter's observatory at Lilienthal, c. 1805. Unknown artist, public domain, via Wikimedia Commons."
alt="Early 19th-century engraving of Schröter's observatory buildings and large telescopes at Lilienthal." >}}
The society that started the search didn't outlast the Napoleonic Wars. In 1813, French troops
under General Dominique Vandamme destroyed Schröter's observatory at Lilienthal, along with his
books and papers. The place where the celestial police first met, where Juno was found and where
Bessel learned his trade, was gone. Schröter never recovered from the loss, and he died in 1816.

Olbers kept searching for more fragments until 1816, and then he stopped too.

## Thirty-Eight Years of Nothing

After Vesta, the sky went quiet. For 38 years, no one found a fifth.

{{< nbas-image src="Karl_Ludwig_Hencke.png" width="220" align="left"
caption="Karl Ludwig Hencke. From Himmel und Erde, reprinted in The Sidereal Messenger (1891). Public domain, via Wikimedia Commons."
alt="Drawn portrait of Karl Ludwig Hencke, an older man with white hair." >}}
It wasn't that nobody looked. Karl Ludwig Hencke, a retired Prussian postal official in the town
of Driesen, took up the search around 1831. Using the Berlin Academy's new star charts, and making
detailed charts of his own, he compared them with the sky night after night from the dormer
window of his house. For about fifteen years he found nothing.

On 8 December 1845 he found a star that moved. It was the first new asteroid since 1807, and it
was named Astraea. The King of Prussia rewarded him with a pension. Hencke found a second, Hebe,
on 1 July 1847, and Gauss — still naming planets four decades after Vesta — chose its name.

{{< clear >}}
{{< nbas-image src="fig-asteroid-count.svg" fullwidth="true"
caption="The number of known asteroids, 1800–1860: four in six years, then nothing for 38 years, then a flood. Data: IAU Minor Planet Center discovery dates."
alt="Step chart of known asteroids from 1800 to 1860. It climbs to four by 1807, stays flat at four through a shaded 38-year span, then rises from Astraea in 1845 to 15 by 1851 and 62 by 1860." >}}

{{< nbas-image src="mitchell-1854-p174.png" width="380" align="right"
caption="The same page, nine years later. S. Augustus Mitchell, Mitchell's Primary Geography (1854), p. 174. Public domain, via Google Books."
alt="The 1854 version of the same schoolbook engraving, its asteroid zone crowded with many more labelled small planets." >}}
After that, the dam broke. Better charts and more searchers brought a steady stream: by 1851
there were fifteen known asteroids, each with its own planetary symbol, and the symbols were
becoming hopeless. In 1852 the American astronomer Benjamin Apthorp Gould suggested simply
numbering them in order of discovery, inside a circle. Vesta became ④ — today's (4) Vesta.

You can watch the change happen on that schoolbook page. The 1849 edition of *Mitchell's Primary
Geography* adds Astraea, and a new outer planet labeled "Le Verrier" — Neptune, still going by
the name of the man who predicted it — and tells students that the last *ten* are exterior
planets. By 1854, the engraving's asteroid belt is crowded with names: Flora, Irene, Victoria,
Parthenope and more. Counting planets had stopped being a sensible question for a geography
lesson.

Herschel had seen this coming. In 1802 he measured Ceres and Pallas, found them far too small to
show proper disks — he estimated Ceres at about 160 miles across — and told the Royal Society
that they deserved a name of their own. He proposed *asteroids*, "star-like," because that's how
they look in a telescope. (The word itself may have come from a friend's son, the classical
scholar Charles Burney Jr.) Herschel's colleagues mostly ignored it. Herschel's size estimate was
badly low, too — Ceres is closer to 600 miles across.

The new name took decades to win. By the 1850s, with dozens of small bodies on the books, the
first four were quietly moved off the planet list and into a new category. Neptune broke the
Titius–Bode rule in the same decade — it orbits at about 30 AU, not the 38.8 the pattern
called for — and the "law" that started the whole search was demoted along with them.

Ceres, Pallas, Juno and Vesta have the odd distinction of losing planet status about 150 years
before Pluto did. And in 2006, when the International Astronomical Union created the new category
of "dwarf planet," Ceres was named as one of its first members, alongside Pluto.

## Go Look

{{< nbas-image src="4_vesta-equ2-2m.jpeg" width="500" align="right" 
caption="4 Vesta from a short smart scope image, Bob Donahue, NBAS, eQuinox 2, 2 min" 
alt="Image of the sky showing the position of Vesta overlaid with an equatorial coordinate grid." >}}

All four are still out there, and every one is within reach of a small telescope or a smart
scope. Each comes to opposition (its best showing) every 15 to 17 months. Vesta, the brightest,
reaches about magnitude 5 at a good opposition, and Juno about 7.5. To find where they are on any
night, ask [JPL Horizons](https://ssd.jpl.nasa.gov/horizons/) for an ephemeris, or turn them on in
a planetarium program such as Stellarium. Then use the trick Piazzi stumbled onto: look twice, a
night or two apart, and see which "star" has moved.

## Wrapup

The search for the missing planet was organized, international and theory-driven. It was built on
a numerical pattern that turned out to mean nothing, and a fragment theory that turned out to be
wrong. And between them, they found two of the first four asteroids — while the first was found by
a man making a star catalogue, and the fifth, decades later, by a retired postal official with
patience and a dormer window.

When you find Vesta or Ceres in your binoculars or telescope this winter, you're looking at something that, for
the schoolchild holding Mitchell's geography in 1845, was simply a planet. It's a little humbling
to think about which of our own tidy lists will look like that page in a hundred and eighty years.
