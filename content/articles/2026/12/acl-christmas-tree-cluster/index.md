## Introduction

{{< nbas-image src="banner.webp" fullwidth="true" 
caption="Locating NGC 2264 in Monoceros between Betelgeuse and Procyon. Rendered with Stellarium.[^stellarium]" >}}

{{< object-specs align="right" >}}
Between Betelgeuse and Procyon, in the dim northern corner of Monoceros, sits a cluster that
everyone calls a Christmas tree and almost nobody sees as one at first. The bright star S
Monocerotis marks the base, the tip points at a dark spike called the Cone Nebula, and the
fainter stars between them fan out into a triangle. Once you've seen the tree you can't unsee
it. The catch is that it's upside down on nearly every chart you'll ever look at.

The tree is the least of it. NGC 2264 is a cluster still sitting in the cloud that made it,
young enough that stars are forming at its southern end right now. It's also one of the places
where astronomers first worked out what a newborn star looks like.

## Two Discoveries, Twenty-Three Months Apart

{{< nbas-image src="herschel.webp" width="200" align="right"
caption="Wm. Herschel (1738-1822) painted by Lemuel Francis Abbott c. 1785, public domain"
alt="Painted portrait of William Herschel" >}}
William Herschel found the cluster on January 18, 1784, sweeping with his 18.7-inch reflector.
He filed it as H VIII.5 -- class VIII, "coarsely scattered clusters of stars" -- and noted that
15 Monocerotis (S Mon) was attended by more than thirty fairly bright stars.

The nebula took him longer. On December 26, 1785, he thought he saw a very faint milky glow
among the stars southwest of 15 Mon, and gave it a separate number, H V.27, in his class of very
large nebulae. He wasn't sure. His own note says there remained a doubt of its reality, and he
was still hedging when he looked again in 1787. At Birr Castle, Lord Rosse's observers searched
the region repeatedly and came away with nothing. What settled the question was photography: by
the 1890s Isaac Roberts and E. E. Barnard had long exposures showing extensive nebulosity all
through the region.

That history is worth keeping in mind at the eyepiece. Herschel had one of the largest
telescopes in the world and could barely convince himself the nebula was there. The New General
Catalogue later folded both of his entries into one number, which is why "NGC 2264" means the
cluster, the nebula, and the Cone, all at once.

## Which Way Is Up?

Astronomical charts put north at the top. NGC 2264's tip -- the Cone -- lies south of its base,
S Mon. So on a north-up chart the tree hangs upside down, and many published photographs show it
the same way, or lying on its side.

There's nothing wrong with any of those. The sky has no "up"; north-up is a convention, and a
useful one. But it does mean that the view in a Newtonian reflector -- or a refractor or
Schmidt-Cassegrain used without a star diagonal -- is the flattering one. Those instruments
rotate the image 180 degrees, putting south at the top, and the tree stands up on its own.

Smart scopes add their own twist. An alt-az smart scope frames the sky at whatever angle the
mount and the hour happen to give it, and the field rotates through the night. My own image of
NGC 2264 came out with the tree lying on its side. Rotated to put south on top, here it is.

## What You're Looking At

{{< nbas-image src="ngc_2264-s50-20m.webp" width="420" align="right"
caption="NGC 2264, the Christmas Tree Cluster, rotated so the tree stands upright. South is up and east is to the right. The Cone Nebula is the dark spike at the top; S Mon is the bright star near the bottom. Bob Donahue, NBAS -- Seestar S50, LP (dual-band) filter, 20 min" >}}

Read this image from the top down, and you're walking from the youngest part of NGC 2264 to the
oldest.

**The Cone.** At the top is the dark spike of the Cone Nebula -- a pillar of cold gas and dust
roughly seven light-years long, made famous by a 2002 Hubble portrait. It is what's left of a
cloud being eaten away by the ultraviolet light of hot young stars. Dense knots of gas shield the
material behind them, so as the surroundings boil away, a pillar remains, with its blunt head
facing the stars doing the eroding. In this image the Cone's head points down, toward the
cluster. Which star does most of the sculpting isn't settled; S Mon and a massive star still
buried in the dust near the Cone's tip have both been proposed.

**Below the tip.** Just beneath the Cone is the most embedded part of the region, where star
formation is still going on. You can't see the newest stars here at all -- they're inside the
dust, visible only in the infrared -- but most of the stars in this zone still carry the disks of
gas and dust they formed from.

**The tree.** Working down from the tip, the brighter stars spread out into the triangle: a
few dozen hot B-type stars, with hundreds of fainter, lower-mass members scattered among them.
None of it is a tidy line of ornaments; your eye does a lot of work here, which is part of the
fun.

**Red and blue in the same cloud.** The red glow that fills the middle of the frame is hydrogen
gas, ionized by ultraviolet starlight and shining at the wavelength called hydrogen-alpha. The
blue haze around S Mon is something else: dust close to the star, reflecting its light. Blue
light scatters off fine dust more readily than red, so reflection nebulae around hot stars look
blue. One cloud, lit two different ways. (This image was taken through a dual-band
light-pollution filter that passes hydrogen-alpha and oxygen-III light, which is why the red is
so strong.) 

**The Fox Fur.** The pale, whitish patch just above S Mon is starlight reflected off dust close
to the star. Wrapped around it, darker and redder, is the mottled, streaky dust that gives the
Fox Fur Nebula its name -- in a deep image it really does look like fur.

**S Mon.** At the bottom is the base of the tree and the brightest star in the cluster: S
Monocerotis, magnitude about 4.7, the hot massive star that lights and shapes much of what you've
just looked at. More on it below.

Star formation in NGC 2264 started here, in the north around S Mon, more than five million years
ago, and moved south toward the Cone a few million years later. That's the age gradient you
walked through from top to bottom. Around S Mon, the young stars have lost their disks faster
than anywhere else in the cluster -- possibly stripped by the star's own intense ultraviolet
light.

### The Star at the Base

S Mon goes by two names. "15 Monocerotis" is its Flamsteed number. "S" is a variable-star
letter: the letters are handed out in the order stars in a constellation are recognized as
variable, so S marks it as the second in Monoceros (the first, R Mon, is the star that lights
Hubble's Variable Nebula a degree or so to the south -- a story for another day). The letter says
nothing about how dramatic the variation is, and here it isn't: S Mon wanders between about
magnitude 4.62 and 4.68, far too little to notice by eye. I haven't been able to find out who
first flagged it as variable, or why, which is a small mystery of its own.

The star itself is anything but modest. The primary is an O7 main-sequence star, around 38,000
degrees at the surface and roughly 200,000 times as luminous as the Sun. It's so typical of its
kind that since 1943 astronomers have used its spectrum as the standard for type O7: to call
another star "O7" is to say its spectrum looks like S Mon's.

It isn't alone. A second hot star, about 1.6 magnitudes fainter, orbits it only a tenth of an
arcsecond away -- far too close for any backyard telescope. Pinning that orbit down took decades.
In the 1990s the period was thought to be about 25 years; the current estimate is about 108
years, on an orbit so elongated that the two swing from close to far apart and back. The last
close approach was around 1996, so nobody alive today will see the next one. Together the pair
holds roughly 45 times the Sun's mass.

Farther out is a companion you can see: S Mon B, magnitude 7.8, about 3 arcseconds away. It's a
three-magnitude step down from the primary, which makes it a nice test for a small telescope on a
steady night. It's very likely the companion Herschel noted in 1784, when he wrote that 15 Mon
was itself a double star, "extremely unequal"; F. G. W. Struve measured it in 1825, and it is still
catalogued as Struve 950 AB. Gaia puts B at the same distance as the cluster, which makes it at
least 2,000 times the Earth–Sun distance from A. Whether it's actually bound to A, nobody can say
yet.

For a sense of scale: S Mon is about magnitude 4.7 from 2,400 light-years away. Moved to the
distance of Sirius, the pair would shine at around magnitude −8 -- about twenty times brighter
than Venus at its best, bright enough to cast shadows.

## Too Young to Sit Still

Most of the stars in NGC 2264 aren't finished.

A star like the Sun spends a few tens of millions of years contracting before it settles onto the
main sequence and starts its long, steady life burning hydrogen. While it's contracting it's
larger and brighter than it will be as an adult, and it sits above the main sequence on an H-R
diagram (see the H-R diagram in our [winter star colors article](/articles/2026/12/the-colors-of-winter-stars/)
for what the main sequence is).

NGC 2264 is one of the places where that was first seen clearly. In 1954 George Herbig found 84
stars in the cluster showing hydrogen emission, most of them T Tauri stars -- a class of young,
irregular variables. Two years later, Merle Walker's photometry showed that NGC 2264 has a normal
main sequence for its hot, massive stars, but that its lower-mass stars consistently sit above
it. The massive stars had already arrived; the small ones were still on their way. Walker
recognized that these stars would matter a great deal for understanding the earliest stages of
stellar evolution, and he was right.

How young is young? The usual figure is about three million years, but that's a median over a
real spread of four or five million years, and it shifts with the adopted distance. (A cluster
placed farther away has to be more luminous, which makes its stars look younger.) The
trustworthy statement is "a few million years" -- less than a thousandth of the Sun's age.

### Stars That Flicker

{{< nbas-image src="spitzer-ir.webp" width="400" align="left"
caption="NGC 2264 in the infrared, seen by the Spitzer Space Telescope. The red and pink specks are newborn stars still buried in the cloud -- the 'Snowflake Cluster' -- invisible in visible light. North is up here, so the tip of the Cone is at the bottom -- the opposite of the tree image above. NASA/JPL-Caltech/P.S. Teixeira (Harvard-Smithsonian CfA)"
alt="False-color infrared image of a tall field of glowing green dust wisps scattered with blue foreground stars, with a cluster of small red and pink points near the center marking embedded protostars." >}}
Young stars are restless. They're still pulling in gas from their disks, they have hot spots where
that gas lands, and the disks themselves are lumpy. Over thirty continuous days, an international campaign called
CSI 2264 (the Coordinated Synoptic Investigation of NGC 2264) watched more than a thousand
members at once, with the CoRoT and Spitzer space telescopes and a network of ground-based
telescopes. The results were published in 2014.

The biggest single group among the disk-bearing stars studied in detail -- more than a fifth of
them -- were what the team called "dippers": stars that fade abruptly for a day to five days,
then recover. The most likely explanation is clumps of dust in the star's own disk passing in
front of it. You're looking at stars dimmed by the raw material of their own planetary systems.

## Neighbors and Cousins

The winter sky is full of young clusters still wrapped in gas. Three of them make a good
comparison with NGC 2264:

It's tempting to read this as a sequence -- buried, emerging, carving, cleared -- and in a loose
way it is. But the ages are too close and too uncertain to line the four up in order. How a
cloud looks depends on how massive its stars are and how the cloud is shaped as much as on how
long the stars have been shining. The Rosette's two biggest stars are hotter than anything in
NGC 2264, and one of them, HD 46223, sits off at the edge of the cavity -- a Gaia study suggests
it was thrown out of the cluster's center about 1.7 million years ago.

{{< nbas-image src="ngc_2024-equ-34m.webp" width="300" align="right"

The Flame has a story of its own. Its ionizing star was hidden so well behind the dust lane that
nobody could say which star it was until 2003, when near-infrared observations picked out IRS
2b.

One thing the table hides: the Rosette sits in the sky only about five degrees from NGC 2264, and on a
wide photograph they look like neighbors. They aren't. NGC 2264 belongs to the Mon OB1
association, about 2,400 light-years away; the Rosette's cluster belongs to Mon OB2, roughly twice
as far. They're lined up by chance.

## Finding and Observing It

This one doesn't need a finder chart. Draw a line from Bellatrix to Betelgeuse, across Orion's
shoulders, and keep going about one and a half times as far again: you land within a degree or so
of S Mon, at the base of the Unicorn's horn on the banner chart above. Every smart scope's catalog
has it too. From
North Adams it climbs to about 57 degrees. It crosses the meridian near midnight in late December
and around 10:30 PM by mid-January, so January through March evenings are the comfortable
window.

### Naked Eye and Binoculars

S Mon itself is a naked-eye star from a reasonably dark site. Binoculars show it with a scatter
of fainter stars around it. Rotate your head or the chart so south is up, and look for the triangle.

### Small Telescope

Low power and a wide field show the tree best -- and remember that a Newtonian already has it the
right way up. The nebula is another matter. Even with large apertures it's faint; one experienced
observer reports only guessing at the Cone with a 14-inch telescope, a hydrogen-beta filter, and
averted vision. If you don't see it, you're in Herschel's company.

### Smart Scopes

This is where the region opens up. A smart scope collecting light for half an hour or more will
show the red hydrogen glow, the blue haze around S Mon, and the Cone itself -- everything Herschel
doubted, from a backyard. A dual-band filter helps a lot with the red. Expect the tree to come out
at some odd angle; rotate the image afterward if you want it standing up.

## Wrapup

The tree is an accident of where a few bright stars happen to fall. What's real is stranger: a
cluster you can see in binoculars, built over five million years from north to south, with stars
at its tip that haven't finished forming and stars around its base that have already lost their
disks. Herschel could barely see the nebula, and Walker used this cluster to show that small
stars arrive late. On a January night, put south at the top and look again. The stars at the tip
of that tree are among the youngest in the winter sky.
