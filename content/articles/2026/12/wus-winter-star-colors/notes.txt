## Central picture

Stars have colors, and the colors are thermometers. A red star is cool, a blue-white star is hot,
and the winter sky puts the full range on display at once, bright enough to see from town.

Everyday anchor: a piece of iron in a forge, or an electric stove element. It glows dull red,
then orange, then yellow-white as it heats. Stars follow the same sequence.

The challenge: see the colors for yourself, rank them, and work out which stars are hottest.

## Stories to dig for ("wait, why is that true?")

1. **Why color means temperature.** Thermal (blackbody) glow: hotter objects emit more light
   overall and peak at shorter wavelengths [verify wording; Wien's law as an optional aside].
2. **Why there are no green stars.** A star whose output peaks in the green still emits strongly
   across the whole visible range, so the eye sees white. The Sun's peak is near green, and the
   Sun is white, not yellow, seen from space [verify peak wavelength and the "white Sun" framing].
   Probably the best "wait, why?" moment in the piece.
3. **Why your eye undersells star colors.** Cones (color) need more light than rods. Bright stars
   show color; faint ones look white. Tricks: compare stars side by side, slightly defocus
   binoculars or a telescope to spread the light [verify the defocus advice and why it helps].
4. **Twinkling colors aren't the star's color.** Sirius low in the sky flashes red, green, blue:
   that's our atmosphere (scintillation and dispersion), not the star [verify mechanism wording].
5. **Spectral classes, briefly.** O B A F G K M as a temperature sequence, with one winter star
   per class where possible. Keep the history (Harvard computers, Annie Jump Cannon) to a few
   lines; it may deserve its own Historical Perspectives piece later [Bob].
6. **Same color, different reasons for brightness.** Betelgeuse and Aldebaran are both orange-red
   but differ hugely in size and luminosity [verify]: color tells temperature, not size. A small
   step toward the H-R diagram without naming it yet (or naming it, linking an existing quiz —
   the H-R diagram quiz exists).
7. **Carbon stars: the reddest of all.** Hind's Crimson Star (R Leporis): a variable carbon star,
   strongly red, within binocular reach near maximum [verify range, period, current phase for the
   publication date]. Why carbon makes it so red [verify].
8. **Cameras don't see color the way eyes do.** Smart-scope images: bright stars saturate to white
   cores; white balance and processing change apparent color [verify Seestar/eQuinox behavior].
   Turns into a mini-experiment (below).

Pick 5–6 for the article. Strongest spine: 1 → 3 → 2 → 6 → 7, with 4 as a sidebar.

## The challenge (format)

- Naked eye from town: rank five bright stars by color, then check the ranking against the
  temperature table.
- Binoculars: Betelgeuse/Rigel side by side; then find R Leporis.
- Smart scope: image Betelgeuse and Rigel with the same settings and compare; try a short
  exposure so cores don't saturate [verify feasibility].
- Optional: record observations (sketch, notes) and come back in a month — Betelgeuse varies.

## Timing

Whole winter evening sky; best roughly January–February evenings [verify for the publication
month]. Works under light pollution and moonlight, which makes it a good beginner piece.
