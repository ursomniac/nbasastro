## Central picture

Stars have colors, and the colors are thermometers. A red star is cool, a blue-white star is hot,
and the winter sky puts the full range on display at once, bright enough to see from town.

Everyday anchor: a piece of iron in a forge, or an electric stove element. It glows dull red,
then orange, then yellow-white as it heats. Stars follow the same sequence.

WUS framing: "these are up, and they're worth a look — here's what you're seeing."
(Not a challenge: no ranking exercise, no scoring.)

## Photography aside (Bob, 2026-10-20)
Star-trail trick: during a trail exposure, step the focus every couple of minutes. Each trail
widens into a cone, and the color and saturation change along it — spreading the light keeps the
core from saturating, so the true color shows. Same physics as the visual defocus tip (story 3)
and a better version of story 8. Bob has examples on slides only (no scanner) — options: photograph
the slide on a lightbox/tablet screen with a phone or camera; or describe it as a "try it
yourself" with no image.

## Fact pass 1 (2026-10-20) — results

**Settled enough to write:**
- No green stars: a star peaking in the green also emits nearly as much red (and blue); the eye
  blends it to white. The Sun peaks in the green and looks white from space. (BBC Science Focus,
  Discover, LCO Spacebook.) Sun's peak by Wien's law: 2.898e-3 m·K / 5772 K ≈ 502 nm
  (blue-green). Computed — exact given the inputs.
- Defocus advice: slightly out of focus averages out scintillation and gives a better read of a
  star's color; for doubles, don't defocus so far the disks overlap (Astronomy, Oct 2015).
  Burnham also says Albireo's contrast is best seen slightly out of focus.
- Betelgeuse: M1–M2 Ia–Iab; Teff ~3,500–3,700 K, varying (JAAVSO 50(2), 2022: 3,528–3,731 K);
  radiates ~10–20× more in near-IR bands than in V (same paper). "Only ~13% of its energy is
  visible light" appears in the Paris Observatory dictionary — usable with attribution, or
  rephrase as "most of its light is infrared."
  Variability: semiregular, periods ~400 d and ~2,100 d. Bob's instinct holds: month-to-month
  change is too small for a satisfying follow-up. Great Dimming 2019–20: dust + small temp drop.
- R Leporis: carbon star; V 5.5–11.7; period ~427 d (GCVS), meandering 427–446 d; maxima vary
  5.5–9.5 with a possible ~40-yr cycle (AAVSO LPV of the Month, 2017). Hind 1845, "like a drop
  of blood on a black field." **Key nuance: the red is LESS pronounced near maximum** and most
  striking when the star is fainter (Wikipedia; cosmicpursuits). So "binocular-bright" and
  "most crimson" don't coincide — a nice honest wrinkle.
  STILL OPEN: phase/brightness for Dec 2026–Jan 2027 — needs the AAVSO light curve (Bob, or a
  fetch attempt).
- Red Sirius: Ptolemy's Almagest (c. AD 150) uses ὑπόκιρρος for six stars: Sirius plus
  Aldebaran, Antares, Arcturus, Betelgeuse, Pollux. Translation is disputed ("reddish" vs
  "yellowish"/"fiery"). Seneca: redder than Mars. Manilius (1st c.) and Avienus: "sea-blue."
  Chinese records 2nd c. BC–7th c. AD: white (Sirius was their white standard). Discrepancy first
  raised by Thomas Barker, Royal Society, 1760. Physical explanations intrinsic to Sirius (e.g.,
  Sirius B as a red giant) don't work; atmospheric reddening near the horizon is consistent with
  both the physics and the history (MNRAS 310, 355 — Whittet 1999 [confirm author]).
  Honest summary: almost certainly not a real change; translation + horizon observations.
- Almach (γ And): K3 giant + B9 companion (itself a close pair, 0.3″); A–BC ≈ 9.4–9.8″; colors
  usually given as yellow/orange and blue, sometimes greenish-blue.
- 145 CMa / h3945: K0 + F0, mags 5.0 & 5.8, sep. ~26.8″ (S&T, Bob King). Orange + blue.
  **Optical double** — distances ~6,000 ly vs ~260 ly (baskies.com.ar; [confirm with Gaia]).
  Low from North Adams, but a real December–January target.

**New story found:** Betelgeuse may have been *yellow* ~2,000 years ago — a 2022 study of
ancient color records (Neuhäuser et al., MNRAS [confirm]) argues it changed from yellow to red.
Pairs naturally with the red-Sirius story: one claimed change probably isn't real, the other
might be. [Bob: in or out?]
