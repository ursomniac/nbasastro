## Colors You Can See From Town

Step outside on a December or January
evening and the brightest stars come in a real range of colors: Betelgeuse a ruddy orange, Rigel
blue-white, Capella yellow, Aldebaran a softer orange, Sirius a brilliant white. Nowhere else in
the year do so many bright stars of so many different colors crowd into one part of the sky.

You don't need a dark site for this. Bright-star colors survive light pollution and moonlight
far better than any galaxy or nebula does -- this is one of the few observing projects that works
just as well from a driveway in town.

And the colors aren't decoration. Each one is a temperature reading.

## Why Color Means Temperature

Think of an electric stove element. Switch it on and, for a moment, nothing shows. Then it glows a
dull red, then a brighter orange, and if it could get hotter still it would go yellow, then white.
Anything hot enough to glow follows that sequence, and stars are no exception.

Two things happen as an object heats up. It gives off more light at every wavelength, and the
peak of that light -- the wavelength where it puts out the most -- slides toward the blue end of
the spectrum. A cool star peaks in the infrared, and the visible light it does give off is
weighted toward red. A hot star peaks in the ultraviolet, and its visible light is weighted
toward blue. Stars in between come out orange, yellow, or white.

Astronomers sort stars into spectral classes that run in order of temperature: O, B, A, F, G, K,
M, from hottest to coolest. The odd letter order is a leftover from history. In the Harvard survey
of the 1880s and 1890s, the classes were first lettered alphabetically by the strength of hydrogen
lines in each star's spectrum, with A being the strongest. Annie Jump Cannon and her Harvard colleagues
then merged and reordered them into the sequence used today -- without knowing, at the time, that
what they had lined up was temperature.

The Sun is a G star, at about 5,800 K. Rigel is a B star, roughly twice as hot. Betelgeuse is an
M star, at around 3,600 K. Those three are the whole story of this article in miniature.

## Why There Are No Green Stars

Here's the puzzle. Red stars exist. Orange, yellow, white, and blue stars exist. Green sits right
in the middle of the rainbow -- so where are the green stars?

The Sun answers it. Its output peaks at about 500 nanometers, in the blue-green. By the logic of
"peak color is star color," the Sun ought to be green. It isn't, because a star never emits just
one color. A star whose light peaks in the green is also putting out nearly as much red, and
plenty of blue, and the eye blends that mixture into white. To look green, a star would have to
emit green light and very little else, and nothing that glows from heat can do that.

(This is also why the Sun is, strictly speaking, white rather than yellow. Seen from space, with
no atmosphere in the way, it's white. The yellow tint we see from the ground comes from our own air
scattering some of the blue out of its light.)

### But I've Seen a Green Star...

{{< nbas-image src="Almach_double_star.jpg" width="250" align="right"
caption="Gamma Andromedae, Credit: NVN271, CC BY-SA 4.0, via Wikimedia Commons"

You may well have -- in a telescope, next to a bright orange one. Double stars with an orange or
gold primary often show companions described as blue-green or even green. Almach, in Andromeda,
is the classic case, and it's still high in the sky on December evenings. The green is real in
the sense that you really see it, but it comes from your eye, not the star: next to a strongly
orange star, a white or bluish one gets pushed toward green by contrast. We looked at Almach and
two other colorful doubles in the [Fall 2026 Preview](/articles/2026/08/fall-2026-preview-the-best-easy-access-sky-objects/).

### Betelgeuse and Rigel

The two brightest stars in Orion sit at opposite ends of the temperature scale, about 18° apart in
the same constellation. Betelgeuse, Orion's shoulder, is a red supergiant at around 3,600 K, and
its color is unmistakable even to a casual glance: a warm orange, sometimes described as reddish.
Rigel, at Orion's foot, is a blue supergiant at roughly 12,000 K, and looks crisp, slightly bluish
white.

This is the comparison to start with. Look from one to the other a few times and the difference
stops being subtle. Bellatrix, Orion's other shoulder, is hotter still -- a B2 star at around
22,000 K -- though at second magnitude its color is harder to see than Rigel's.

### Castor and Pollux

The Gemini twins don't match. Castor is white, an A-type star around 10,000 K (it's actually a
six-star system, though that's a story for another time). Pollux is an orange K0 giant, around
4,800 K. They're less than 5° apart, close enough to compare without moving your head.

### Aldebaran and the Hyades

Aldebaran, the orange eye of Taurus, looks as though it belongs to the V-shaped Hyades cluster
around it. It doesn't -- it's a foreground star, less than half the cluster's distance, as we saw
in [our look at Taurus](/articles/2026/12/taurus/). It's a K5 giant at about 3,900 K, and the color
contrast with the white and yellowish Hyades stars behind it is easy in binoculars.

### Capella

Capella, high overhead in Auriga, is the yellow star of the winter sky -- the closest color match
to the Sun among the bright stars. It's actually two yellow giants orbiting each other too closely
to split, at roughly 5,000 K and 5,700 K.

### Sirius and Procyon

Sirius, the brightest star in the night sky, is an A-type star at about 9,900 K: white, with a
faint blue edge. Procyon, in Canis Minor, is an F star around 6,600 K and reads as a slightly
warmer, creamier white. The difference between them is subtle -- a good test once you've warmed up
on Betelgeuse and Rigel. Interestingly, both stars harbor white dwarf companions.

## Same Color, Very Different Stars

Color tells you a star's surface temperature. It doesn't tell you how big the star is, or how much
light it gives off -- and stars of the same color can differ enormously in both.

{{< nbas-image src="fig-gl205-finder.svg" width="380" align="right"
caption="Gliese 205 lies about 2° northwest of the Orion Nebula, roughly halfway up to the Belt. Stars to magnitude 8; M42 shown schematically. Star data: HYG database (CC BY-SA 4.0)."
alt="Star chart with north up and east left, showing Orion's Belt stars Mintaka, Alnilam and Alnitak at the top, M42 and Hatysa at the bottom, and Gliese 205 marked by a gold ring between them, right of center." >}}
Betelgeuse is a good example, and you don't have to leave Orion to see a comparison. About 2° north-northwest
of the Orion Nebula sits an eighth-magnitude star called Gliese 205: a red dwarf, about 18.6
light-years away, with a surface temperature of around 3,770 K -- nearly the same as Betelgeuse's.
Put the two side by side at the same distance and they'd be close to the same color. But Betelgeuse
is hundreds of times wider than the Sun and something like 100,000 times as luminous, while
Gliese 205 gives off only about 6% of the Sun's light. Nearly the same thermometer reading; a difference
in output of more than a factor of a million.

To find Gliese 205, start at the Orion Nebula and work about halfway up toward the Belt, then a little to the west
(right, as Orion stands in the evening sky). At magnitude 7.9 it's just within reach of binoculars,
and easy in any small telescope. It looks like nothing special: a faint, slightly orange dot.
That's the whole idea. Betelgeuse is the same kind of star, temperature-wise, swollen to an enormous
size.

{{< clear >}}

That's the idea behind the Hertzsprung-Russell (H-R) diagram, which plots stars by temperature
along one axis and luminosity along the other. Stars don't scatter randomly across it. Most fall
on a diagonal band, the main sequence, where stars like the Sun and Sirius spend most of their
lives. Above it sit the giants, like Aldebaran and Pollux, and above those the supergiants like
Betelgeuse and Rigel. A single color -- a single column on the diagram -- can hold a dwarf, a
giant, and a supergiant.

There's one more twist. What we see isn't all there is. A cool star like Betelgeuse puts most of
its energy out in the infrared, which the eye can't detect -- it radiates something like ten to
twenty times more in the near-infrared than in visible light. If our eyes covered the infrared,
Betelgeuse would be the brightest star in the sky. Hot stars like Rigel have the opposite problem:
much of their output is ultraviolet, also invisible. How bright a star looks depends on how much of
its light happens to land in our narrow visible light window.

For the deepest red in the winter sky, go below Orion to the fainter constellation Lepus, the Hare.
R Leporis is a carbon star: a cool giant whose outer layers have been enriched with carbon dredged
up from inside. The carbon-bearing molecules in its atmosphere absorb much of its blue light, which
pushes its color past orange into a deep red. John Russell Hind, who found it in 1845, described
it as "like a drop of blood on a black field."

R Leporis is also variable, ranging from about magnitude 5.5 down to 11.7 over a cycle of roughly
14 months, and the brightness at each maximum differs from cycle to cycle. Its color changes with
its brightness, too: the crimson is least striking near maximum and most intense when the star is
fainter.

That works out nicely this season. R Leporis is climbing slowly out of a minimum, and its next
maximum isn't expected until around May 2027 -- when, as it happens, it will be lost in the evening
twilight. So this winter it's in its fainter phase, likely a small-telescope target rather than a binocular one,
but it's also at its reddest. For its current brightness, check the AAVSO's light curves at
[aavso.org](https://www.aavso.org/) before you go looking.

From North Adams it never gets higher than about 33°, so catch it near its highest: around
11:30 PM in mid-December, or about 9:30 PM in mid-January.

## Was Sirius Ever Red?

Today Sirius is the very definition of a white star. Yet around AD 150 Ptolemy, in the star
catalog of his *Almagest*, applied the same color word to Sirius as to Betelgeuse, Aldebaran,
Antares, Arcturus, and Pollux -- a list of orange and red stars. The Roman writer Seneca went
further and called Sirius redder than Mars.

The puzzle was first raised before the Royal Society in 1760, and it has never quite gone away.
But the case for a genuinely red Sirius doesn't hold up well:

- **The word is slippery.** Ptolemy's term, *hypokirros*, is usually translated "reddish," but
  its meaning is disputed; some scholars read it as closer to "yellowish" or "fiery."
- **Other sources disagree.** The Roman poet Manilius described Sirius as sea-blue, and Chinese
  records from the 2nd century BC to the 7th century AD consistently call it white -- Sirius was
  the standard of whiteness in Chinese star lore.
- **The physics doesn't cooperate.** Sirius's white-dwarf companion was once a red giant, and it's
  tempting to imagine it still glowing red in Ptolemy's day. But Sirius B finished that stage
  something like 120 million years ago, not two thousand.
- **Horizons redden everything.** Much ancient attention to Sirius concerned its first appearance
  before dawn after weeks lost in the Sun's glare. Seen that low, through a thick slab of air, any
  star turns red and flickers wildly.

The likeliest answer is a mix of translation and low-altitude viewing. Sirius almost certainly
hasn't changed.

### Betelgeuse, on the Other Hand...

Betelgeuse may be a different story. A 2022 study of historical records found that two
independent ancient sources -- the Roman author Hyginus and the Chinese historian Sima Qian --
described Betelgeuse two thousand years ago as yellowish rather than red, and argued that the star
really has changed color since, as it settles into its red supergiant stage. One claimed color
change that probably isn't real, and one that might be.

## Seeing the Colors for Yourself

Star colors are real, but they're subtle, and the eye doesn't make them easy. A few things help.

**Start with the bright ones.** The eye has two kinds of light detectors. Cones see color but
need a fair amount of light; rods are far more sensitive but colorblind. Faint stars only register
on the rods, so they all look white. The brighter the star, the more color you'll see -- which is
why winter, with its cluster of first-magnitude stars, is such a good season for this.

**Compare side by side.** A single star in isolation is hard to judge. Two stars of different
colors, looked at back and forth, make the difference obvious. Betelgeuse and Rigel, and Castor and
Pollux, are the pairs to use.

**Wait until they're well up.** Near the horizon, any star is dimmed and reddened by the extra air
in the way. Compare stars when both are high. In mid-December that means late evening -- around
11 PM, Betelgeuse is about 50° up and Rigel nearly 40°. By mid-January the same view arrives around
9 PM.

**Don't trust a twinkling star near the horizon.** Sirius never climbs above about 31° from North
Adams, so it spends the whole winter in thick, turbulent air. On a cold, unsteady night it can
flash red, blue, and green in rapid succession. That's our atmosphere acting like a prism, not
the star; the true color is the white you see when the air settles.

**Try a little defocus.** In binoculars or a telescope, turning the focus slightly off spreads each
star into a small disk. That averages out the twinkling and makes the color easier to read. For a
double star, don't defocus so far that the two disks overlap.

**Watch for impostors.** Late in the evening, two bright "stars" rise in the east, in Leo: Jupiter
and an orange Mars. Planets shine by reflected sunlight, so their colors have nothing to do with
temperature -- Mars is orange because its surface is rusty.

One related trick: in dim light, the eye's sensitivity shifts toward blue, so red stars can look
fainter to a dark-adapted eye than they really are. Variable-star observers who estimate the
brightness of red stars have to watch out for it.

### A Camera Trick

{{< nbas-image src="Blurred_star_trails_in_Orion_(54444410402).png" width="200" align="right"
caption="Credit: Mark McCaughrean, CC BY-SA 4.0, via Wikimedia Commons"
alt="Time lapse of the sky centered on Orion with the camera defocused at intervals causing the star trails to be cone-shaped highlighting their color" >}}

The same idea works in photography, and it makes a striking picture. Put a camera on a tripod, aim
it at a bright part of the winter sky, and start a long star-trail exposure. Every couple of
minutes, turn the focus a little further out. Each star's trail widens into a cone, and as the
light spreads, the core stops saturating to white and the star's real color comes through,
changing in strength along the length of the cone.
