## Three Showers, Three Very Different Nights

{{< nbas-image src="banner.webp" fullwidth="true"
   alt="Banner image for the winter meteor showers article." >}}

It's tempting to give up on meteors once the nights turn bitter. Don't. Winter has the single best
meteor shower of the year, and this year the Moon is almost entirely out of its way. It also has the
most knife-edged shower of the year, one that can rival it for a few hours and then all but vanish.
And between them sits a quiet little shower whose radiant never sets, so you can check on it any
clear night you happen to be out.

The long nights help, too. By 6 PM it's fully dark, which means a good part of the action happens at
an hour when you'd still be awake anyway.

Quick orientation before the details: here's the shape of the season.

| Shower | Peak (2026–27) | Active Dates | Moon at Peak | ZHR (ideal skies) | Parent Body |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | ---: | :--- |
| Geminids | Night of Dec 13–14 | Dec 4–20 | ~20%, waxing crescent – sets 8:30 PM | 150 | Asteroid 3200 Phaethon |
| Ursids | Dec 22 | Dec 17–26 | ~97%, nearly full | 10 | Comet 8P/Tuttle |
| Quadrantids | Jan 3 (late evening, EST) | Dec 28–Jan 12 | ~10%, waning crescent – rises ~5 AM | 80 (varies ~60–200) | Asteroid 2003 EH1 |

**Personality, at a glance:**
- **Geminids** — the year's most reliable big show, from a parent that isn't really a comet
- **Ursids** — modest, overlooked, and occasionally surprising
- **Quadrantids** — a few hours of glory, named for a constellation that no longer exists

   caption="Activity profiles for the three winter showers: peak dates and rates from the IMO 2027 calendar, curve shapes from Jenniskens (1994). Note how narrow the Quadrantid peak is."

High radiants don't automatically mean early evenings, by the way. All three radiants are well north,
but only the Geminids are an evening shower. The Ursid radiant never sets at all from New England, and
the Quadrantid radiant spends the evening scraping the northern horizon and only climbs high before
dawn at this time of year. Each section below says when to actually be out.

## Geminids — The Best of the Year, From a Rock

{{< nbas-image src="geminids-1214-0200.webp" fullwidth="true"
   caption="Geminids radiant, 9:00 PM EST Dec 13 (02:00 UTC Dec 14), looking east-northeast."
   alt="Sky chart looking east-northeast at 9 PM on December 13, showing the Geminid radiant near the star Castor in Gemini, about a third of the way up the sky." >}}

If you only go out for one meteor shower a year, make it this one, and make it this year. Under dark
skies the Geminids produce well over a hundred meteors an hour at their peak, more than the Perseids,
and they're unusually kind to the observer: medium-speed, often bright, and the radiant, near Castor
in Gemini, rises in the early evening. By 9 PM it's a third of the way up the sky; around 2 AM it's
nearly overhead.

The Moon cooperates in 2026. The waxing crescent sets at about 8:30 PM on the 13th, leaving the rest of
the night dark.

The peak itself, by the International Meteor Organization's (IMO) reckoning, falls during daylight 
hours on the 14th here, but the Geminid maximum is broad. The night of December 13–14 is the one 
to circle, and the nights on either side are worth a look too.

### About the Source: Asteroid 3200 Phaethon

{{< nbas-image src="PIA22185.gif" width="220" align="right"
caption="Radar images of Phaethon from the Arecibo Observatory, December 15–19, 2017, when it passed 10.3 million km from Earth – its closest until 2093. Credit: Arecibo Observatory/NASA/NSF, Public domain, via Wikimedia Commons"
alt="Animated sequence of grainy radar images showing Phaethon as a roughly spherical body slowly rotating." >}}

Almost every other major shower comes from a comet. The Geminids come from something that _looks like_ an
asteroid. In October 1983, Simon Green and John Davies, at the University of Leicester, spotted an
object with a very unusual orbit in data from the IRAS infrared satellite. Fred Whipple soon noticed
that its orbit matched the Geminids. It was named 3200 Phaethon, after the son of the Sun god Helios
who lost control of his father's chariot, and the name fits: Phaethon swings closer to the Sun than
any other named asteroid, about 0.14 AU, less than half of Mercury's closest distance, and its surface
reaches roughly 750°C.

That closeness makes Phaethon do comet-like things. Spacecraft watching it near the Sun in 2009 and
2012 saw it brighten and grow a short tail. However, a 2023 study found that tail is sodium gas, not dust,
which leaves a real puzzle: if Phaethon isn't shedding dust today, how did it make the Geminids? One
study, using dust measurements from NASA's Parker Solar Probe, suggests the stream was born in a
sudden, violent event — a collision, or an explosive release of gas — rather than slow, comet-like
shedding. We may find out more soon: Japan's DESTINY+ spacecraft is scheduled to
launch in 2028 and fly past Phaethon about two years later.

### The Young Upstart

The Geminids are a newcomer. While the Perseids appear in records going back two thousand years and the
Leonids for more than a thousand, nobody seems to have noticed the Geminids until 1862, when Robert Greg in
Manchester, England, and, independently, B.V. Marsh and Alexander Twining in the United States
recorded them. The observed early rates were modest. Through the twentieth century the shower grew into the
powerhouse it is now, and it's _still_ growing: one study found peak rates rose about 20% between 2000
and 2016, as the densest part of the stream drifts toward Earth's orbit.  This is a meteor shower that's
already one of the major events of the year, and it seems that it will only get more intense for some time
to come.

Their solid parent may explain why Geminids look the way they do. The particles are denser than the
fluffy dust that comets shed, so they tend to survive longer in the atmosphere and burn as slower,
brighter streaks. And one more date for the calendar: Phaethon will pass about 3 million km from Earth
on December 14, 2093 — Geminid peak night.

### Quick Reference

- **Peak:** Night of December 13–14, 2026 — good from about 9 PM, best from late evening into the early morning
- **Active:** December 4–20
- **Moon:** ~20% waxing crescent, sets about 8:30 PM — dark the rest of the night
- **Expected rate:** ZHR ~150 under ideal skies; well over 100 an hour is realistic at a dark site
- **Radiant:** Constellation Gemini, near Castor, high in the sky after midnight
- **Parent body:** Asteroid 3200 Phaethon

## Ursids — The Quiet One That Never Sets

{{< nbas-image src="ursids-1223-0300.webp" fullwidth="true"
   caption="Ursids radiant, 10:00 PM EST Dec 22 (03:00 UTC Dec 23), looking north."
   alt="Sky chart looking north at 10 PM on December 22, showing the Ursid radiant near Kochab in the bowl of the Little Dipper, about 30 degrees above the horizon." >}}

The Ursids are a shower most people have never heard of, and in 2026 they won't win any converts:
the peak comes a day before the Full Moon. Expect a handful an hour at best.

But the Ursids have one trick none of the others share. Their radiant sits in the Little Dipper, near
Kochab, close enough to the pole that from New England it never sets. It's at its lowest in the evening,
still about 30° up, and climbs toward dawn. So there's no special hour to wait for. If you're out on
any clear night around December 22, for the Geminids' tail end, or to look at the Moon, or to walk the
dog, glance north.  Maybe you'll get lucky.

### About the Source: Comet 8P/Tuttle

{{< nbas-image src="8P_Tuttle.webp" width="400" align="right"
caption="Comet 8P/Tuttle imaged by Arecibo radar in January 2008, showing its two-lobed nucleus. (NOTE: credit)"
alt="Radar image of the nucleus of comet 8P/Tuttle, showing two rounded lobes joined together." >}}

Comet 8P/Tuttle circles the Sun every 13.6 years, from near Earth's orbit out to beyond Saturn's. It
was first seen in 1790 and found again in 1858 by Horace Tuttle, which is how it got his name. When it
passed Earth in January 2008, radar from the Arecibo Observatory showed its nucleus to be two lobes
stuck together, a "contact binary" about 4.5 km across.

### Mr. Tuttle's Meteors

If the name sounds familiar from our fall article, it should. Horace Tuttle was one of the great comet
hunters of the nineteenth century. He co-discovered 55P/Tempel-Tuttle, the parent of the Leonids, and
109P/Swift-Tuttle, the parent of the Perseids, and he independently recovered Comet Encke, the source
of the Taurids. Between them, comets with his name on them produce three of the year's showers, and
he had a hand in a fourth.

### Outbursts at the Wrong Time

The Ursids were probably first noticed by the English meteor observer William Denning around 1900, but
nobody paid much attention until 1945, when a Czech astronomer, Antonín Bečvář, saw rates of about 169
an hour. Another major outburst came in 1986, and smaller ones since.

The strange part is when they happen. You'd expect a shower to flare when its comet is nearby, freshly
shedding dust. The biggest Ursid outbursts have come instead when Tuttle was near the far end of its
orbit, out beyond Saturn. The explanation of why that is involves Jupiter: old meteoroids that have settled into a
slightly different rhythm with Jupiter from the parent comet.  Thus, they have been slowly drifting out of 
step with comet 8P/Tuttle, until, after centuries, they now arrive at Earth's orbit half an orbit 
apart from the comet. Tuttle's next trip to the far end of its orbit is around 2028, so the next 
few Decembers are worth watching. IMO doesn't predict anything unusual for 2026's Moon-washed return, though.

## Quadrantids — A Shower Without a Constellation

{{< nbas-image src="quadrantids-0104-0600.webp" fullwidth="true"
   caption="Quadrantids radiant, 1:00 AM EST Jan 4 (06:00 UTC), looking northeast."
   alt="Sky chart looking northeast at 1 AM on January 4, showing the Quadrantid radiant in northern Boötes, below the end of the Big Dipper's handle and about 20 degrees above the horizon." >}}

The Quadrantids can match the Geminids for sheer numbers, but only for a few hours. Their peak is so
narrow that rates stay above half their maximum for only about eight hours, compared with a couple of
days for the Perseids. Catch the peak and you might see a meteor a minute; miss it by half a day and
you'll wonder what the fuss was about. That's why Quadrantid reports swing so wildly from year to year.

This year the peak, by IMO's best estimate, comes at about 10:25 PM EST on January 3 — give or take a
couple of hours — when the radiant, in northern Boötes, is only a few degrees above our northern
horizon. That doesn't mean you'll miss it. You don't watch the radiant anyway: the usual advice is to
look 20° or more away from it, and meteors fly outward from it in every direction. With the radiant
that low, only the half of the shower heading *up* from the horizon reaches you, so expect a fraction
of the full rate — perhaps 5 to 10 an hour under dark skies around the peak. But those few can be
the best of the night: meteors from a radiant near the horizon skim the top of the atmosphere at a
shallow angle and can cross a huge stretch of sky. These long, slow "earthgrazers" are worth the cold
on their own.

Or set the alarm. As the night goes on, the shower fades but the radiant climbs, and the two roughly
cancel out: from about 1 AM until dawn on January 4, with the radiant from 20° to more than halfway up
the sky, a dark site could give something like 20 an hour. The Moon, a thin waning crescent, doesn't
rise until about 5 AM. And IMO notes the stream is shifted slightly toward the Sun this year, which
might boost activity. Nobody can promise that, but it's a reason to try — at either end of the night.

### About the Source: 2003 EH1 (and a Comet From 1490?)

For a long time the Quadrantids were an orphan. The parent wasn't identified until 2003, when Peter
Jenniskens linked them to a newly discovered object, 2003 EH1. Like Phaethon, it looks more like an
asteroid than a comet. It may be a surviving piece of a comet that astronomers in China, Japan and
Korea recorded at the end of 1490.

That would fit the shower's sharp peak. A newly-created meteor stream starts out narrow and spreads over time as the
planets tug on it. The Quadrantid stream is still tight, which suggests it formed only about 500 years
ago — young enough to fit a breakup around the time of that 1490 comet.

Don't expect to see the parent, though. 2003 EH1 is a dark body about 4 km across. When Toshihiro
Kasuga and David Jewitt photographed it with the 10-meter Keck telescope in 2013, it showed up as a
faint point of light around magnitude 20, with no sign of a coma or dust — whatever made the
Quadrantids, it isn't making many more now.

And 2003 EH1 never gets much brighter. Its orbit is tilted steeply, about 71° to Earth's, so it's only near
us when both happen to be close to where the two orbits cross — and Earth is at that spot only in early
January, the night of the shower. If 2003 EH1 were there at the same moment, it could reach about
magnitude 13. The closest it comes to that within the next few decades is December 28, 2052 — just days
before that year's Quadrantids — when it passes about 50 million km from Earth, a third of the
distance to the Sun, and should reach about magnitude 15½: a target for a large amateur
telescope or a camera, not the eye. This winter it's out near the far end of its orbit, around
magnitude 23. The shower is the trail it left behind; the parent is somewhere else entirely.

### Why "Quadrantids"?

{{< nbas-image src="quadrans.webp" width="450" align="left"
caption="Quadrans Muralis, shown on a card from _Urania's Mirror_, c. 1825. (NOTE: credit)"
alt="Hand-colored star chart card showing the constellation Quadrans Muralis, drawn as a wall-mounted quadrant, between the figures of Boötes and Draco." >}}

There's no constellation called Quadrans. There used to be - for a time. In 1795 the French astronomer Jérôme
Lalande carved out a new constellation between Boötes and Draco and named it Quadrans Muralis, the
Mural Quadrant, after the large wall-mounted instruments observatories used to measure star positions.
(There's a famous 1598 engraving of Tycho Brahe that depicts him using a mural quadrant.)

An Italian observer, Antonio Brucalassi, reported a burst of meteors radiating from that corner of the
sky in early January 1825. By 1839, Adolphe Quetelet in Brussels and Edward Herrick in Connecticut had
each realized it happened every year. The shower took its name from Lalande's constellation.

But then the constellation was demoted. When the International Astronomical Union fixed the official list
of 88 constellations in the 1920s, Quadrans Muralis didn't make the cut, and its stars went back to
Boötes and Draco. The meteor shower kept the name anyway: it's the only major shower named for a constellation
you won't find on a modern star chart.

### Quick Reference

- **Peak:** January 3–4, 2027 — IMO's peak is about 10:25 PM EST Jan 3 (±2.5 hours). Late evening: low rates but a chance at long earthgrazers; 1 AM to dawn Jan 4: the most meteors
- **Active:** December 28–January 12
- **Moon:** ~10% waning crescent, rises about 5 AM — no interference before then
- **Expected rate:** ZHR ~80 at the peak (anywhere from ~60 to 200 in recent years); fewer on the falling side
- **Radiant:** Northern Boötes, near the end of the Big Dipper's handle (the old Quadrans Muralis)
- **Parent body:** 2003 EH1, possibly a remnant of a comet seen in 1490

## How to Watch — Get **Very** Comfortable (Winter Edition)

Everything in [the fall guide](/articles/2026/09/fall-2026-a-season-of-meteor-showers/) still
applies: get away from lights, give your eyes 20–30 minutes to adjust, keep your phone in your pocket,
and lie back rather than staring at one spot. Winter adds a few things.

- **Dress for standing still in the cold.** You're not moving for an hour or two. Layers, a warm hat,
  insulated boots, and mittens over thin gloves.
- **Insulate from below.** A closed-cell foam pad under a sleeping bag beats any blanket on frozen
  ground; a reclining lawn chair beats both.
- **Hand and foot warmers** are cheap and work.
- **Keep your phone warm.** Cold drains batteries fast. Inside pocket, not the chair arm.
- **Expect frost.** Anything you leave out — chair, binoculars, the thermos lid — will be wet or frosted
  within the hour.
- **Bring a thermos.** It's comfort, and an excuse to stay out ten more minutes.
- **Use the long nights.** Full darkness by 6 PM means the Geminids are a good show at an hour when the
  whole family is still up.

Three showers, three very different personalities: a powerhouse born from a sun-scorched rock, a quiet
one that's always up, and a brief burst named for a constellation that no longer exists. Two of them
get dark, moonless skies this season. So bundle up, bring the
thermos, and give winter a chance.
