## The Bull

{{< nbas-image src="Lascaux_IV-bull.webp" fullwidth="true"
caption="An aurochs from the Lascaux cave paintings, as reproduced in the Lascaux IV replica. Credit: Renhour48, CC0, via Wikimedia Commons"
alt="A black-outlined aurochs painted on pale cave rock, its head at center. A cluster of dark spots sits on and around the face, and a separate group of six dots lies above its back." >}}

Taurus may be the oldest constellation we can still point to. In 1940, four teenagers in the
Dordogne region of France squeezed into a hole left by an uprooted tree and found the cave of
Lascaux, its walls covered with horses, deer, and enormous black aurochs -- the wild ancestors of
modern cattle -- painted somewhere around 17,000 to 22,000 years ago. On one of those aurochs, a
cluster of dark spots sits on and around the face, and a tight group of six dots floats just above
its back. In the 1990s, the German researcher Michael Rappenglück proposed that this is a sky
picture: the dots on the face are the Hyades, the group above the back is the Pleiades, and the
animal itself is the Bull, drawn in the same place the Greeks would put one some fifteen thousand
years later.

It's a genuinely striking match -- and it's a hypothesis, not a finding. Paleolithic artists left
no captions, and there are other readings of the same kind of marks. A 2023 study by Bennett Bacon
and colleagues looked at hundreds of sequences of dots and lines painted next to Ice Age animals
and argued that they're counts of lunar months, recording when each species mates and gives birth
-- a hunter's calendar rather than a star chart. That idea has its own critics. The honest summary
is that people were clearly paying attention to the sky and to the seasons, and that some of the
oldest art we have *might* include this exact patch of stars. The stars themselves haven't changed:
step outside on a winter night, find the Bull, and judge the resemblance for yourself.

{{< nbas-image src="hist_TAU.webp" width="550" align="left" caption="From _Urania's Mirror_, c. 1825" >}}

On firmer ground, the Bull is among the oldest constellations in the written record. Mesopotamian
astronomers called these stars the Bull of Heaven, and in the *Epic of Gilgamesh* the goddess Ishtar
sends that bull down to destroy Gilgamesh after he rejects her; he kills it instead, and his
friend Enkidu pays for the sacrilege with his life. The Greeks inherited the animal and gave it a
new story: Zeus, disguised as a gentle white bull, coaxed the Phoenician princess Europa onto his
back and swam off with her to Crete. That story also offers one explanation for an oddity of the
figure -- only the front half of the bull is drawn, as if the rest were still under the waves.
Another explanation is that the bull once stretched further west, into the space Aries occupies
now.

This is a part of the sky people have been recording for a very long time, with very different
levels of certainty: a possible Ice Age painting at Lascaux, the Pleiades on a Bronze Age disk
from Germany, a written record of a star exploding in 1054 -- and a painting in New Mexico that may
or may not show that same explosion. All of them turn up below.

## Map of Taurus
{{< nbas-image src="TAU.gif" caption="Map of Taurus" >}}

The Bull's face is the easiest part to find: a sideways "V" of stars, the Hyades, with orange
Aldebaran marking one tip as the bull's glaring eye. From the open end of the V, two long horns
reach northeast, ending at Elnath (β Tauri) and Zeta Tauri. Elnath sits right on the border with
Auriga, and for a long time it did double duty as part of both constellations' figures (as γ Aurigae). 
Off the bull's shoulder, to the northwest, sits the Pleiades.

Taurus is a zodiacal constellation: the ecliptic runs through it, between the Hyades and the
Pleiades, and the Sun is in Taurus from about May 14 to June 21. That's why the Moon and planets
regularly pass through this field, sometimes right between the two clusters. Its
place on the ecliptic also gave it a role in the calendar. As our [Aries](/articles/2026/11/aries/)
article describes, precession slowly drags the vernal equinox westward along the zodiac. Before it
crossed into Aries in 1866 BCE, it spent roughly 2,700 years in Taurus, from about 4530 BCE: through
the stretch of history when the first cities and the first writing appeared, the Bull marked
the start of spring.

The Milky Way runs along the bull's eastern edge, and the point in the sky directly opposite the
center of our galaxy -- the galactic anticenter -- lies just over the border in Auriga, about 4°
from Elnath. Looking toward Taurus, we're looking roughly outward, away from the crowded heart of
the galaxy.

### The Sky 17,000 Years Ago

{{< nbas-image src="orion-late-july-15000bce.webp" align="right" width="500"
caption="The southern sky from Lascaux on a spring night about 17,000 years ago (15,000 BCE), simulated in Stellarium. Orion barely clears the horizon."
alt="A simulated night sky looking south from a dark site. A small tight cluster sits high near the center, with a V-shaped group and a bright orange star below it. Near the horizon, three stars in a short line form Orion's Belt, flanked by an orange star to the left and a blue-white star to the right." >}}
Would an Ice Age observer have seen the same pattern of stars? Yes. Over 17,000 years the stars of
the Hyades and the Pleiades have barely moved relative to each other; the pattern on the painting,
if it is the sky, is the pattern you can see tonight. What *has* changed is where that pattern
sits. Precession -- the slow wobble of Earth's axis -- turns the whole sky together, and 17,000
years is about two-thirds of a full cycle. Back then the vernal equinox sat in Libra or Scorpius,
nearly across the sky from where it is today, so the Bull belonged to a different season, and it
rode much lower: from Lascaux, Aldebaran climbed only about 30° above the southern horizon, compared
with more than 60° today.

Orion fared worse. From the same cave, 17,000 years ago, Orion's Belt rose less than 10° above the
horizon, and a couple of thousand years later it didn't rise at all. That's worth keeping in mind
before reading any of the cave's other marks as the Belt: depending on exactly when the painting was
made, its artists may have seen it only skimming the horizon, or not at all.

### The Closest Star Cluster

{{< nbas-image width="400" align="right" src="hyades.webp" >}}

The Hyades are the nearest open cluster to the Sun, about 153 light-years away -- close enough that
the cluster spreads across more than five degrees of sky, far too big for most telescopes to frame
though just right for binoculars. That nearness makes them one of the most important clusters in
astronomy: their distance can be measured several independent ways, and the methods agree, which
makes the Hyades a foundation of the ladder astronomers use to measure distances across the
universe. At about 625 million years old, the cluster is middle-aged by cluster standards, old
enough that several of its brightest stars have swollen into orange giants. Messier left the Hyades
out of his catalog, so it goes by Caldwell 41 instead. In Greek myth, the Hyades were daughters of
Atlas and half-sisters of the Pleiades, who wept so long for their dead brother Hyas that they
became stars associated with rain.

The bull's "eye" isn't actually part of the cluster. Aldebaran, the brightest star in Taurus, is an
orange giant only about 65 light-years away -- less than half the distance to the Hyades -- and just
happens to sit on the same line of sight. It's a foreground star photobombing the cluster, and
the brightest part of the bull's face is a coincidence of perspective.

### The Brightest Messier Object

{{< nbas-image width="400" align="left" src="m_45-s30-69m-mosaic.webp"
caption="Bob Donahue, NBAS, Seestar S30, 69 min - mosaic" >}}

At an integrated magnitude of 1.6, the Pleiades outshine every other entry in Messier's catalog,
and they're the most obvious star cluster in the sky for the naked eye. Six or seven stars are easy;
under dark skies and with good eyes, people have counted more than a dozen. The cluster is about
444 light-years away and young -- most estimates fall between about 75 and 150 million years, with
recent work around 125 million. Why Messier included something this obvious in a catalog meant to
keep comet hunters from being fooled is a minor mystery; one suggestion is that he simply wanted a
longer list than his rival Lacaille's.

The Pleiades are also the oldest firmly identified star group in this article's story. The Nebra
sky disk, a bronze disk with gold inlays found in Germany and dated to around 1600 BCE, shows the
Sun or full Moon, a crescent Moon, and a tight cluster of seven gold dots that's widely read as the
Pleiades -- older than any surviving written description of them.

Long exposures show the cluster wrapped in blue reflection nebulosity, which looks like the cloud
the stars were born from but isn't. The Pleiades are passing through an unrelated cloud of
interstellar dust, moving at about 18 km/s relative to it. The brightest patch, around the star
Merope, has its own catalog number: NGC 1435, the Merope Nebula.

### The Taurus Dark Cloud

{{< nbas-image width="400" align="right" src="ngc_1555-noirlab.webp"
caption="T Tauri and NGC 1555, Hind's Variable Nebula. Credit: T. A. Rector/University of Alaska Anchorage, H. Schweiker/WIYN and NOIRLab/NSF/AURA, CC BY 4.0"
alt="A small, fan-shaped reflection nebula glowing yellow-orange beside a bright star, T Tauri, against a dark, sparsely starred background." >}}

Much of Taurus is darker than it should be. Spread across the region between the Pleiades and the
Hyades, and on into Auriga, lies the Taurus Molecular Cloud: cold gas and dust about 430-450
light-years away. In visible light, it shows up mostly as an absence -- patches of sky with too few
stars, where the background is blotted out. It's possibly the nearest large star-forming region to
us, home to hundreds of newly formed stars, and that closeness has made it one of the most studied
places in the sky for how stars and planets begin.

You can't see the cloud itself visually, but you can see something it made. T Tauri, a young star still
settling down after its formation, gave its name to a whole class of newborn stars, and it lights
a small, faint nebula beside it: NGC 1555, Hind's Variable Nebula, discovered by John Russell Hind
in 1852. As the light from T Tauri and the dust around it change, the nebula brightens and fades
over the years -- sometimes dramatically. Within a few years of Hind's discovery it had faded from
view, and by 1868 Otto Struve couldn't find it. Struve did report a different small nebula a few
arcminutes away, which went into the catalog as NGC 1554. In 1890, E. E. Barnard, working with the
36-inch refractor at Lick Observatory, glimpsed both. No one has seen NGC 1554 since, and it's
known today as Struve's Lost Nebula. NGC 1555 is still there, but it's a faint wisp about an
arcminute across that rewards a large telescope and a lot of patience.

Infrared telescopes see the cloud the other way around: the dust itself glows faintly at long
wavelengths, revealing filaments and knots where new stars are forming. Radio telescopes find
something even more surprising inside it. One dense ridge, TMC-1, has yielded more than a hundred
different molecules, many of them carbon chains and rings. In 2018, a team using the Green Bank
Telescope detected benzonitrile there, and in 2021 the same program found polycyclic aromatic
hydrocarbons -- multi-ringed carbon molecules that weren't expected in a cold, dark cloud where no
stars have formed yet.

For imagers, the darkest parts of the cloud are targets in their own right. E. E. Barnard, the same
observer who recovered Hind's nebula, catalogued a dozen or so dark clouds across Taurus and
Auriga. One of them, Barnard 22, is a lane of dust about two degrees long that hides some of the
cloud's youngest stars -- a shadow you can only photograph by capturing the faint starlight around it.

### The Supernova of 1054

{{< nbas-image width="400" align="right" src="m_1-equ-31m-square.webp"
caption="Bob Donahue, NBAS, eQuinox 2, 31 min" >}}

On July 4, 1054, Chinese astronomers recorded a new star near the tip of the bull's southern horn,
bright enough to see in daylight; it stayed visible to the naked eye for about two years. Nearly
seven centuries later, in 1731, the English astronomer John Bevis found a faint nebula in the same
spot. In 1758, Charles Messier came across it while searching for the predicted return of Halley's
Comet and at first took it for the comet; when it didn't move, he realized the sky held objects
that could fool comet hunters, and began the list that made him famous. This nebula became its
first entry. Lord Rosse, sketching it with his large telescope in the 1840s, saw crab-like
filaments, and the name stuck.

Today the Crab Nebula is the expanding debris of that 1054 explosion, about 6,500 light-years away
and roughly 11 light-years across, still spreading at about 1,500 km/s. At its center is the
collapsed core of the star: a neutron star spinning about 30 times a second, sweeping a beam
across the Earth like a lighthouse. Its discovery as a pulsar in 1968 helped confirm that pulsars
are rotating neutron stars. It's easy to find, about a degree northwest of Zeta Tauri.

{{< clear >}}

{{< nbas-image width="400" align="left" src="Anasazi_Supernova_Petrographs.webp"
caption="Alex Marentes, CC BY-SA 2.0, via Wikimedia Commons"
alt="A painting on the underside of a sandstone overhang: a large star with radiating points, a crescent Moon beside it, and a red handprint." >}}
Did anyone in the Americas record the 1054 supernova? The most famous candidate is a painting on a
rock overhang near the Peñasco Blanco great house in Chaco Canyon, New Mexico: a large star, a
crescent Moon, and a handprint. (It's a pictograph -- painted -- rather than a petroglyph, which is
carved.) The case for it is appealing. Calculations show that at dawn on July 5, 1054, the day after
the Chinese record, a thin crescent Moon sat only about two degrees from the new star, low in the
east, and the painted crescent and star match that pairing remarkably well. The great house was
occupied at the time.

The case against is just as real. The same symbols -- a star, a crescent, a hand, and a Sun -- appear
at known Pueblo Sun-watching stations, where the star is read as the morning star. Some researchers
argue on stylistic grounds that the panel was painted well after the 1100s. The astronomer E. C.
Krupp, who has studied the claimed "supernova" rock art across the Southwest, concludes that none of
it is a convincing record of 1054. Like the Lascaux aurochs, it's a match that's easy to see and
hard to prove -- and a good place to end the story of how long people have been watching this patch
of sky.

### Poor Man's Double Cluster

{{< nbas-image width="400" align="right" src="ngc_1807-equ-5m-full.webp"
caption="Bob Donahue, NBAS, eQuinox 2, 5 min" >}}

About eight and a half degrees east of Aldebaran, NGC 1807 and NGC 1817 sit 27 arcminutes apart,
at magnitudes 7.0 and 7.7 -- a pair close enough to share a low-power field, which earned them the
nickname "the poor man's Double Cluster." The catch is that it may be only half a double cluster.
NGC 1817 is a real, extended open cluster, about 1.1 billion years old and roughly 5,000
light-years away. But a 2004 study of the stars' colors, brightnesses, and motions found no
evidence that NGC 1807 is a cluster at all: its stars don't share a common motion or form a
coherent group, and the brightest of them is moving much too fast to belong. NGC 1807 appears to
be a chance grouping of unrelated stars, seen in the same direction. Through the eyepiece it still
looks like a pair -- which makes it a good reminder that a cluster is defined by stars traveling
together, not just stars sitting together.

### Tiny Crystal Ball

{{< nbas-image width="360" align="left" src="ngc_1514-equ-10m-square.webp"
caption="Bob Donahue, NBAS, eQuinox 2, 10 min" >}}

On November 13, 1790, William Herschel came across what he called "a most singular phenomenon": a
star surrounded by a faint, glowing haze. Herschel had come to believe that every nebula would
eventually resolve into a mass of stars with a big enough telescope. This one wouldn't: a single
star sat in the middle of glowing material that clearly wasn't made of stars. NGC 1514 changed his
mind, and with it the question of what nebulae could be. Today we know it's a planetary nebula,
about 1,500 light-years away, the cast-off outer layers of a dying star. The central star is
actually a pair, orbiting each other about every nine years. In 2010, the WISE infrared survey
found two faint rings around the nebula that are almost invisible in ordinary light; in 2025, the
James Webb Space Telescope looked again and resolved them into clumps and filaments.
