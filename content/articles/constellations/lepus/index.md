## The Hare

If you can find Orion, you can find Lepus. Drop down from Rigel, the bright blue-white star at
the Hunter's foot, and the small huddle of stars just below is the Hare. It isn't big and its
stars aren't bright, but it has been crouching under Orion's feet for well over two thousand
years.

{{< nbas-image src="hist_LEP.webp" width="550" align="left" caption="From _Urania's Mirror_, c. 1825" >}}

Lepus is old. Ptolemy included it among his 48 constellations in the 2nd century, and it was
already old then. The Greek poet Aratus, writing in the 3rd century BC, put the Hare "beneath
both feet of Orion," chased through all time by the Dog that rises right behind it. That Dog is
Canis Major, and its brightest star, Sirius, still follows the Hare across the sky every winter
night.

What Lepus never got was a proper myth. Most classical constellations come with a hero, a
monster, or a god behaving badly. The Hare has almost nothing. The one real story comes from
the Roman writer Hyginus: hares brought to the island of Leros bred so fast that they ate the
crops and caused a famine, and a hare was set in the sky as a reminder.
<!-- VERIFY: Leros story against a translation of Hyginus, De Astronomia 2.33. Seen only in
  secondary sources so far. -->
Mostly, though, the Hare is there because Orion is there. A hunter needs game, and his dogs
need something to chase.

The astronomers of the Islamic world kept the Greek hare. The name of the constellation's
brightest star, Arneb, is simply the Arabic word for "hare." But they also preserved older
desert traditions about the same four stars, α, β, γ, and δ Leporis, which form a lopsided box.
Early Arab skywatchers saw it as a throne or chair for al-Jawzā', the giant figure they placed
in the region of our Orion. The 10th-century Persian astronomer al-Ṣūfī, writing in Arabic,
recorded another name for the box: al-Nihāl, camels quenching their thirst at a river in the
sky. That name survives today on β Leporis, which is called Nihal.

## Map of Lepus
{{< nbas-image src="LEP.gif" caption="Map of Lepus" >}}

Lepus is compact: about 290 square degrees, 51st in size among the 88 constellations. Its core
is that lopsided box of four third-magnitude stars, sitting about 10° south of Rigel.

The box's two brightest stars are a good lesson in how misleading brightness can be. Arneb (α)
and Nihal (β) look like a matched pair: magnitudes 2.6 and 2.8, about 3° apart. But Nihal is a
giant star about 160 light-years away, while Arneb is a yellow supergiant about 2,200
light-years away, nearly fourteen times farther. To look almost the same from that far off,
Arneb has to shine with roughly 27,000 times the Sun's light. Nihal also has a faint companion,
about seventh magnitude and less than 3″ away, a tough split even in a good telescope.

Arneb and Nihal also point the way to the constellation's best deep-sky object. Start at Arneb,
go through Nihal, and keep going a little farther than that same distance again, about 4°. You'll
land on the globular cluster M 79.

From our latitude, Lepus never gets high. In early January it crosses the meridian around
10:30 PM EST, about two hours earlier by early February. At that point Arneb is about 30° above
the southern horizon and M 79 only about 23°. You'll want a clear view to the south, and as
little haze as possible.

### Lonely Globular Cluster

{{< nbas-image width="400" align="left" src="m_79-noao.webp"
caption="NOIRLab, CC BY 4.0, via Wikimedia Commons" alt="Globular cluster M 79: a dense ball of stars with a bright core"  >}}
Most of the Milky Way's globular clusters crowd toward the center of the galaxy, which is why
summer skies in Sagittarius, Scorpius, and Ophiuchus are full of them. M 79 is the odd one out:
a globular in the winter sky, on the far side of the galaxy from the center, about 42,000
light-years away. Pierre Méchain found it in 1780.

Why is it out here? One idea is that M 79 isn't originally ours. It may have been stripped
from a small galaxy that the Milky Way absorbed long ago. Astronomers still argue about which
galaxy, and even whether the leading candidate (the "Canis Major Dwarf") is a real galaxy at
all. M 79 is also trailing stars behind it in a long tidal tail as our galaxy slowly pulls it
apart.

At magnitude 7.7 it's an easy binocular target, a small fuzzy "star." A small telescope shows a
bright, compact core; it takes more aperture, and a steadier sky than our low southern view
usually allows, to start resolving individual stars.

### Spirals Within a Spiral

{{< nbas-image width="400" align="right" src="ngc_1964-eso.webp"
caption="ESO, CC BY 4.0, via Wikimedia Commons" alt="Spiral galaxy NGC 1964, a tilted oval with a bright center among foreground stars" >}}
NGC 1964 is the brightest of the three galaxies here, at about magnitude 10.8, and roughly 65 to 70
million light-years away. It has two sets of arms: a pair wound tightly around the bright
central region, and a looser, more open pair beginning farther out near an inner ring. The
long, tilted oval in a telescope is mostly the bright inner part; the outer arms belong to
photographs.

It also gave amateurs a treat in 2021. A Type Ia supernova, SN 2021jad, appeared there in April
and reached about magnitude 13: a single exploding star, 65 million light-years away, visible
in a backyard telescope.

### A Bar and an Oddball Supernova

{{< nbas-image width="400" align="left" src="ngc_1784-dr10.webp"
caption="Legacy Surveys / D.Lang (Perimeter Institute) & Meli thev, CC BY-SA 4.0, via Wikimedia Commons"
alt="Barred spiral galaxy NGC 1784 with a central bar and two spiral arms" >}}
NGC 1784 is a barred spiral about 80 million light-years away, first logged by John Herschel
in 1836. At magnitude 11.7 it's a faint smudge visually, better suited to imaging, where the
central bar and the arms trailing from its ends show up.

In October 2022 it hosted SN 2022xkq, and that supernova turned out to be unusual. It was a
Type Ia, the kind astronomers use as "standard candles" for measuring distances, but a dim,
oddly red one, from a rare subgroup that doesn't follow the usual rules. It was strange enough
that the James Webb Space Telescope was turned on it to study it in the infrared.

### A Grand-Design Spiral

{{< nbas-image width="400" align="right" src="ngc_2196-pans.webp"
caption="Donald Pelletier, CC BY-SA 4.0, via Wikimedia Commons" alt="Spiral galaxy NGC 2196 with a large bright core and smooth outer arms" >}}
NGC 2196 is a "grand-design" spiral: instead of a patchwork of arm fragments, its outer arms
are long and well defined, almost closing into a ring around a large, bright central bulge.
William Herschel found it in 1784. It's about 100 million light-years away and, at roughly
85,000 light-years across, a little smaller than our own galaxy. It's the leading member of a
small group that includes NGC 2211 and NGC 2212.

At magnitude 10.9 a medium telescope shows the bright core and a hint of the surrounding
glow. The arms are a job for a camera.

### The Spirograph Nebula

{{< nbas-image width="400" align="left" src="ic_418-hst.webp"
caption="Pelligton, CC BY-SA 4.0, via Wikimedia Commons" alt="Planetary nebula IC 418, an oval shell with fine textured patterns around a central star" >}}
IC 418 is a planetary nebula: the cast-off outer layers of a dying star, about 2,000 light-years
away. A few thousand years ago its central star was a red giant. Now its exposed hot core floods
the shell with ultraviolet light and makes it glow. Hubble's 2000 image showed the fine,
interlaced texture that earned it the nickname "Spirograph Nebula," and nobody is quite sure
what causes that pattern.

It's small, only about 12″ across, so use plenty of magnification. At about magnitude 10.6 it
looks like a tiny oval disk around an unusually easy central star of magnitude 10.2. Some
observers see a reddish or pink tint at its edges, which is rare for a planetary nebula; most
look blue-green.

### Hind's Crimson Star

{{< nbas-image width="400" align="right" src="r_lep-light-curve.webp"
caption="Light curve provided by the [American Association of Variable Star Observers](https://aavso.org). We gratefully acknowledge the observations contributed by worldwide observers to the AAVSO International Database."
 alt="AAVSO light curve of R Leporis showing its regular rise and fall in brightness" >}}
When John Russell Hind came across R Leporis in 1845, he compared it to a drop of blood against
the sky. It's one of the reddest stars you can see. It's a carbon star: a cool giant whose
atmosphere is rich in carbon compounds that soak up blue light, leaving mostly red.

It's also a Mira-type variable, swelling and shrinking over a cycle of about 14 months, about
430 days. At its brightest it reaches about magnitude 5.5, within binocular reach and naked-eye
on a dark night. At its faintest it drops to nearly 12th magnitude and needs a telescope. Its
color changes too. It looks reddest when it's faint and least red near maximum.

The light curve shows its rise and fall. If you want to follow it yourself, the AAVSO has charts
and tips for estimating red stars, which is trickier than it sounds.
