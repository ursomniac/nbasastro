## Remember T CrB?

If it hasn’t already blown, there are some indications that the
accretion rate to the white dwarf has increased. An interesting
analysis of the previous three eruptions finds that they are spaced
by close to an integral number of orbits of the binary star pair:
the orbital period is ~227.6 days, and it appears that the eruptions
typically happen after 125–129 orbits (so there’s a range in the
frequency between 78–81 years - we’re in year 79 now.

{{< nbas-image src="tcrb-2025-03.jpeg" align="right" width="550"
caption="Light of T CrB" >}}

This means there are particular dates that might have a higher
probability of being close to an eruption (within a few days; see
the table to the right).

But we won’t know until it happens! Fortunately, it’s now above the
horizon just after twilight in the East - easy to find as it’s next
to bright Arcturus.

## Smiley Morning

The morning of April 25th, there’s a triple conjunction between
Venus, Saturn and the crescent Moon (also Neptune and Mercury though
they’ll be largely invisible in the twilight).

{{< nbas-image src="smiley.png" caption="Venus, Saturn and the Moon on April 25th"  >}}

{{< nbas-image src="tel_venus.png" align="left" width="300" >}}
**Venus** is still a thinnish crescent, shining at magnitude -4.7:
It will higher over the next few months, and will be the “morning
star” all the way through to the end of the year.  

{{< clear >}}

{{< nbas-image src="tel_saturn.png" align="left" width="300" >}}

**Saturn** fainter at mag 1.2 has almost edge-on rings that might be
essentially invisible in small telescopes (the rings disappeared
in late-March, though Saturn was behind the Sun at that time).
They’ll widen slightly through the Summer and into Fall, and then
almost disappear against late in the year, after which they’ll be
one more visible heading into 2026.

{{< clear >}}

## This Month's Image

{{< nbas-image src="ngc_3344092U-PIXRCAGRAXGHS-square.jpg" caption="Bob Donahue, NBAS" >}}

NGC 3344 in Leo Minor is the “Sliced Onion” Galaxy, almost face-on
with several, thin and extensive spiral arms. It’s about the same
size as our Galaxy.

Being in a small faint constellation and not in the Messier/Caldwell
catalogs, it might get overlooked, but it’s generally findable in
small telescopes (under dark skies). It’s located East and slightly
North of the “Sickle” asterism in Leo.
