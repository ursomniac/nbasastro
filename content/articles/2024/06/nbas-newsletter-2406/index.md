## Prepping for Summer

The Summer Solstice happens on June 20th - with the shortest nights
for observing (only just over 4 hours for full darkness) which means
we have to cram in observations as we can.

This month, let’s highlight two objects that are in the June skies
that are of particular interest this year.

### Incoming Comet

Though comet C/2023 A3 (Tsuchinshan-ATLAS) is still almost 2 AU
away is speeding towards the Sun from the Oort Cloud, and will make
its closest approach to Earth in October.

{{< nbas-image src="c2023-159.png" width="500" align="right" >}}

It has the potential of being a spectacular comet, easily visible
to the naked eye, but you can catch it now at 9-10th magnitude in
the West as it skims the celestial Equator from Virgo into Leo over
the month of June.

Its orbit is nearly hyperbolic, and there’s the chance that it won’t
remain bound to the Sun after this encounter: as it’s being tugged
upon by the planets, it could find itself ejected from the solar
system. Even if it does manage to “stay with us”, the orbit is so
extended that it won’t return for upwards of a few million years.

## Recurrent Nova T Coronae Borealis

The “Blaze Star” T CrB is a binary system, 3,000 light years away
consisting of a red giant and a white dwarf. As the red giant loses
mass, it’s transferred to an accretion disc surrounding the white
dwarf.

{{< nbas-image src="t_crb-system.jpg" width="350" align="right" >}}

About every 80 years, this causes the white dwarf to heat up, causing
an explosion which brightens the system from its normal visual
magnitude of ~10 to as bright as +2 - about the brightness of the
North Star - for a few weeks, after which it’ll return to its
quiescent phase for another 80 yr.

{{< clear >}}

{{< nbas-image src="t_crb-finder.jpg" fullwidth="true" >}}

To find T CrB, first find the semi-circle of stars that is the
Northern Crown (Corona Borealis) just “left” of Boötes with the
bright star Arcturus. It’s placed just a few degrees from the SE
part of the crown. When the outburst occurs, it will shine as bright
as Alphecca - the brighter star in the bottom of the crown. When
not in outburst, with a telescope the star should appear noticeably
red compared to the stars nearby.

## This Month's Image

{{< nbas-image src="m_84+86-90U-PIX+RCA-167.jpg" caption="Bob Donahue, NBAS" >}}

M 84 and M 86 are part of Markarian’s Chain - a string of 8 primary
galaxies that extends for 1 1/2° in Virgo. The small edge- on galaxy
in the upper left is NGC 4402; tiny NGC 4387 is center-bottom. Most
of the galaxies in the chain are 60 Mly away; the Chain is just one
part of the Virgo Cluster.
