
The drift of Jupiter's Great Red Spot (GRS) is calculated by monitoring its longitudinal movement over time in System II coordinates, currently drifting westward at roughly 1.75°–24° per year (or ~0.1939° per day, accelerating over time). The longitude, \(\lambda \), at a given time is predicted by taking a base longitude and subtracting the yearly drift rate, though recent years show a steady, roughly linear, accelerating decrease in longitude.Formula for GRS LongitudeTo predict the GRS longitude (\(\lambda \)) for a specific year, researchers often use linear fits based on historical tracking data (JUPOS):\(\text{Longitude\ }(^{\circ })\approx 405.0-0.1939\times \text{Year}\quad \text{(derived\ from\ historical\ data)}[5]\)Note: The negative sign indicates a westward drift (lower longitude).Calculating Current Drift (Transit Time Method)To determine when the GRS will cross the central meridian of Jupiter, observers use the formula:\(\text{Current\ Longitude}=\text{Reference\ Longitude}-(\text{Drift\ Rate}\times \Delta t)\)Recent Trend: Approximately \(1.75^{\circ }\) west per month.Transit Prediction: If the GRS is 1° longitude off, the transit time is adjusted by approximately \(1\frac{2}{3}\) minutes early or late.Key ObservationsAcceleration: The drift rate is not constant; the GRS has generally accelerated westward over the past century.Shrinking: The spot is shrinking, with its length, width, and longitude changing according to recent trends.JUPOS Data: The most accurate way to calculate the spot's position is using updated JUPOS table measurements.
